# My CEO Husband Gave Her My Corner Office

## Chapter 1 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

My office is hanging in the sky over Monroe Avenue, turning a quarter inch at a time, and it has never looked better.

Walt Kasprzak has it on four chains off the hook of a hundred-ton crane that takes up both lanes and a bus stop. Snow is coming down sideways off the river, the small mean kind that gets into your collar and stays. Every streetlight on the block has a cone of it. In the middle of all that, ten feet by twelve of my own glass and cedar swings out of the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building like a drawer somebody finally opened.

"Lower," I say into the radio. "Lower. Okay, stop, you're kissing the mullion."

"I'm not kissing anything," Walt says. "I'm forty years married. I don't kiss."

"Your hook block is two inches off my glass."

"Your glass is two inches off my hook block. It's a matter of where you stand."

Lolo is beside me on the sidewalk holding the tag line, both fists, leaning back like she's got a marlin. She's sixty-three, five foot one in her steel toes, and wearing a Clyde Park Lanes jacket with SPARE PARTS across the back in iron-on letters that have mostly given up. She came straight from second shift. She brought a thermos and drank all of it before the crane arrived, which I respect.

"Hon," she says, not taking her eyes off the room, "if this thing comes down on a city bus I am telling everybody it was your idea."

"It was my idea."

"I know. That's why it'll stick."

My key still worked at a quarter to two. Facilities moved the badge reader on Friday. Nobody moved the lock my father put in.

Up on four, Darnell is leaning out of the hole where the window bay used to be, one hand on the frame and one giving Walt a thumbs-up he doesn't need. The bay came out in eleven minutes. I designed it to come out in ten. In 2018, when we craned the room in, every engineer on the job told me a removable window wall on the fourth floor of a hundred-year-old furniture factory was vanity. I told them it was a door in the sky. Tonight it's a door in the sky.

The room clears the building. For a second it hangs there with nothing around it, the two glass walls meeting at the corner with no post between them, and the streetlight goes right through the corner and comes out the other side. No post. That corner took me three years and a burned eyebrow. I watch it the way you'd watch a kid walk out on a stage.

"Pretty," Lolo says.

"Don't."

"I'm allowed to say pretty."

"You're holding a rope."

Behind us, by the crane outrigger, the intern is filming. Tavi Okonkwo, twenty-three, two weeks into a six-month design internship, wearing a coat that was clearly purchased for a city with a gentler idea of January. He heard about this at quarter to midnight because he was still at his desk on four, and he asked if he could come, and I said yes because I couldn't think of a reason fast enough to say no. He's been filming for forty minutes with his phone held out in front of him, very steady, the way young people hold phones, like it's a candle in a procession.

"You're not posting that," I tell him.

"No," he says. "No. Definitely not. I would never."

He keeps filming, holding the phone steadier than he's holding his voice.

Elvin, the night guard from the Lindqvist lobby, comes out through the revolving door in his parka and stands on the step with his hands in his armpits. He's worked nights in that building since before we moved in. He used to bring me the newspaper some mornings when I was the first one up the elevator. He looks up at the room, then at me.

"Ms. Sobczak," he says. "I'm going to need you to tell me this is fine."

"It's mine," I say. "It's always been mine. It's on a lease to the company for a dollar a year, and I'm ending the lease."

"Okay," he says, and nods for a while, as if the nodding is a form he has to fill out. "Is the dollar in there?"

"I'll mail you the dollar, Elvin."

"Mail it to Mr. Kerrigan," he says, and goes back inside, and that's the closest anybody comes all night to saying his name out loud.

The room comes down the last twenty feet like it's being lowered onto a cake. Walt is the best crane man in West Michigan and he knows it and he makes you wait. The lowboy trailer is backed up against the curb with fresh cribbing laid across the deck, four stacks of six-by-sixes where the four lift points will sit. I drew those lift points on a napkin at Russo's in 2013. The napkin is in a frame in my mother's hallway next to my First Communion.

"Lolo, walk it," I say. "Ease it around. Corner to the cab."

She walks it. The room turns its glass corner toward the truck cab, gentle, the way you'd turn an old woman toward a better chair.

"Set," says Walt.

It sets. The trailer takes the weight and sighs on its springs. The chains go slack and swing and clink against the cedar, and I'm across the sidewalk and up the trailer steps before Walt's out of the cab, putting my hand flat on the glass by the corner. It's cold enough to sting.

Inside, the room is empty except for my desk and my chair. I had Darnell carry everything else out before we unbolted it. Everything else means Simone Achterberg's things. A ficus in a ceramic pot. A framed print of a lighthouse. A laptop dock. Two banker's boxes she had unpacked halfway, so that her pens were already in my drawer and her stapler was already on my desk and a photo of a girl in a Michigan State sweatshirt was already looking out my window. We took all of it out. We stacked the boxes on the floor up on four, square, labels out, against the wall where my room used to be, with the ficus on top and the lighthouse leaning on the ficus. Darnell watered the ficus. I didn't ask him to. That's the kind of crew I have.

I want that noted somewhere. We didn't throw her things in the hall. We made a nice stack.

"Strap it," Walt says, coming around the trailer with his ratchet straps over his shoulder like a bandolier. "Then you can be emotional at it."

"I'm not emotional at it."

"You're touching the window."

I take my hand off the window.

There are three lines of pencil inside the door that I can just make out through the glass from here, writing on the birch ply in two different hands, and I don't look at them. I look at the straps. I check every ratchet myself, which Walt hates, and he lets me, which is why I hire him.

At four-fifteen the snow lets up for a minute, like the weather wants to watch too. The crane's boom comes down in sections, groaning. Darnell comes out of the lobby with his hard hat under his arm and his face pink from the wind up on four.

"Bay's back in," he says. "Sealed. You'd never know."

"You'd know," Lolo says. "There's a hole in the floor where a room was."

"The floor's fine. It's four bolt plates."

"Four bolt plates and a lady's ficus."

"The ficus is watered," Darnell says, with dignity.

Tavi is still filming. He's filming the bolt-plate conversation. I go over to him and he lowers the phone a quarter of an inch, which for him is a confession.

"How much of that do you have?" I ask.

"All of it," he says, and his voice cracks a little on it. "It's really good, Pip. The part where it comes out of the building and the streetlight goes through the corner? You can't buy that. People pay agencies to make videos like that and they look like videos. This looks like a crime."

"It isn't a crime."

"No. It looks like one. In a good way."

"Tavi."

"I'm not posting it." He holds the phone against his chest. "I'm just saying, if a person *were* going to post it, it would do numbers."

I think about the boardroom on six, and Ruth Baskin from Lakeshore Ventures, who once told me in front of the whole board that my corner was "a lovely little indulgence." I think about how the room looked from the street with the light going through it.

"Don't post it," I say. "Send it to me."

"Okay."

"And go home and sleep. You have a review at ten."

"With who?"

I pause, because I don't actually know who his reviews are with anymore. I used to know everybody's reviews.

"With somebody," I say. "Be early."

He goes off down Monroe with his phone lit up in his hand, a small blue light moving away under the streetlights, and I watch him all the way to the parking ramp and do not think about the fact that I just handed my entire legal defense to a twenty-three-year-old in a fashion coat.

Walt has the straps tight. Lolo is sitting on the bumper of her Buick pouring the last nothing out of her thermos and looking at it with betrayal.

"You riding with me or him?" she asks.

"Him."

"Figures, he's got heat." She screws the cap back on. "Clyde Park?"

"Clyde Park, in the yard. Put it on the pad by the fence where the old Eight used to sit."

"And then what?"

That's the question. That's been the question since yesterday at ten past nine in the morning when the elevator doors opened on four. Lolo asks it the way she asks everything, like she's asking if I want ketchup.

"And then I go to work," I say.

"In it?"

"Next to it."

She looks at me for a while, chewing the inside of her cheek. Lolo was a line lead at Lindqvist Furniture when I was in grade school. My dad trained her on a press brake in 1994 and she still says he was the only man in that plant who never explained anything to her twice. She's watched me grow up from a kid who sat on an upturned bucket by the welders to a woman who owns a third of a company the newspaper calls a unicorn. She doesn't think much of unicorns. She thinks a lot of me, which is worse, because she says it with her eyes and then insults you so you don't make a thing of it.

"You know what you look like right now?" she says.

"Don't."

"Like your mother at a wedding when the band plays the wrong polka."

"That's a very specific face."

"It's a very specific situation, hon."

I climb up into Walt's cab. It smells like diesel and spearmint gum and the vanilla tree air freshener that's been hanging off his mirror since Obama. He pulls out slow, the trailer creaking, the room riding behind us with its corner to the cab like it's looking where we're going. Monroe Avenue is empty except for a salt truck heading the other way. The driver honks twice and waves. Everybody in this town loves a wide load at four in the morning.

My phone, which has been in my coat pocket all night on silent, starts buzzing against my hip. I take it out.

It says ADAM.

It's four twenty-six. He's in Chicago at a hotel near O'Hare because he had a dinner last night with a man from Minneapolis. He should be asleep. He's never been a good sleeper, though. He says he sleeps fast.

I watch it ring. The screen lights my lap and Walt's dashboard and the little vanilla tree. I think about picking up and saying *Good morning, I moved my desk.* I think about the face he'd make. I know every face he makes. I've had eleven years to learn them, and I've been studying the back of his head for two.

It stops ringing. MISSED CALL. Then a text, before I can put it away.

*Simone just called me. Pip. Where is the room.*

No question mark. Adam always uses a question mark. He's a question mark man. He ends statements with them on purpose so people feel invited.

Walt glances over at the screen and back at the road.

"That him?"

"That's him."

"You want me to take the long way?"

I put the phone face down on the dash, next to his gum.

"Take the longest way you know, Walt."

## Chapter 2 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Twenty hours earlier, I'm standing in the elevator of the Lindqvist building with a box of donuts from Marge's, because it's the first Monday of the year and I'm the kind of co-founder who brings donuts on the first Monday of the year to a floor she's been on four times since September.

The elevator is the original freight car, cage doors and a brass lever nobody's allowed to touch anymore. We put a button panel in it in 2018. It still goes up like it's thinking about something else. Two women from accounts payable get on at two, see me, see the box, and say "Oh my God, Pip" in the voice you use for a cousin who flew in for a funeral. I let them take a cruller each. They tell me they love the new color on the Eight. I didn't change the color on the Eight. Somebody changed the color on the Eight.

"It's very calming," says the taller one, whose name is Bridget.

"It's beige," I say.

"Oatmeal," says Bridget.

"Oatmeal's a breakfast."

"Who made it oatmeal?" I ask.

"It came down from the integration team," Bridget says, and then her friend elbows her, and Bridget gets very interested in the floor numbers.

They laugh like I'm doing a bit. I'm half doing a bit. On three they get off. On four the doors open and I step out onto the floor with the donuts held up in front of me like a peace offering, and I'm already saying "Happy New Year, you degenerates" when I look across the floor at the northwest corner.

There's a woman in my room.

The floor of the Lindqvist building is long and old, a hundred feet of maple boards with the bolt holes still in them from the furniture presses, brick walls, steel columns painted a green that was popular in 1922. We put ninety people up here. Sales on the east side by the windows, marketing in the middle, design studio along the south wall under the skylights. And in the far northwest corner, where the two big windows meet over Monroe Avenue and the Sixth Street bridge, the first room I ever built, sitting on its four bolt plates like a cat that found the one sunny spot in the house.

Cedar siding gone silver. Two glass walls meeting at the corner with no post. A door on the east side, birch, with a brass pull my father turned on his lathe.

The door is propped open with a banker's box. There's a ficus on my desk.

Nobody is watching me. Ninety people are very busy not watching me. Monitors are being looked at with tremendous concentration. A man from sales is on the phone saying "Absolutely, absolutely" to what I would bet is a dial tone. Somebody in design has their headphones on and their eyes so wide open they look like a cartoon of a person hearing a noise in an attic.

I walk across the floor. I walk at a normal speed with the donuts.

The woman in my room is in her forties, tall, with gray-blond hair cut very straight at her jawline and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and she's lifting a framed picture of a lighthouse out of a box like it's a birthday cake. She's wearing a navy sweater that probably has a fabric content she could tell you. I know her. Simone Achterberg. Vice President of Operations as of last May. We've been in six meetings together, maybe seven. She took notes in every one of them by hand, in a black notebook, in handwriting so small and level I once thought it was printed. In the third meeting she asked me a question about weld times on the knee frame that nobody in that company had asked me in two years, and I answered it for twenty minutes, and she wrote down all twenty minutes.

She sees me in the doorway and puts the lighthouse down on my desk, face up.

"Pip," she says. "Good morning."

"You're in my room."

"I asked for it," she says. "He said yes before I finished the sentence."

She says it the way you'd say the weather in Grand Rapids in January: not friendly, not unfriendly, a thing that is true and has consequences for your commute. I'll give her that. I've had people take things from me with a smile on their face and I'd rather have it level.

I look at the ficus. I look at her pens, which are in my drawer. I look at the photo on the windowsill of a girl in a green sweatshirt with her arm around a dog.

"That your daughter?"

Simone glances at the photo, then back at me, a small adjustment behind the eyes, like she's checking a measurement.

"Hannah," she says. "She's a sophomore at State."

"What's she taking?"

"Mechanical engineering. She wants to build bridges, or she did in October."

"Good school for bridges." I hold the box out. "Donut?"

She looks at the box for a second longer than a person normally looks at donuts. Then she takes a plain cake one, the least donut of all the donuts, and holds it without eating it.

"Thank you," she says.

"You're welcome."

"I would have asked you," Simone says. "If I had known it was yours. Personally yours."

"Who did you think it belonged to?"

"The company. It's on the asset register." She sets the donut on a napkin and squares the napkin to the edge of my desk. "Line forty-one. Furniture and fixtures, founders' office, one unit."

"It's on a lease."

"Then the register is wrong."

"The register's been wrong since 2018."

"Then I will fix it," she says, and she means it, and I believe her, which is the worst part of the morning so far.

That's when Adam comes around the end of the design studio with two coffees in a cardboard tray and his coat over his arm. He's moving fast, the way he always moves, as if every room he walks into is already a little late for him. He's wearing the blue suit he wears for flights. His hair is still wet at the back from the gym. When he sees me he doesn't stop walking, but something in his face slows down.

There are eleven years of Adam Kerrigan's faces in my head, catalogued and cross-referenced. This is the one he makes when he walks into a room and the room isn't the room he planned for. It lasts half a second. Then he puts the other face on, the one that sold three hundred million dollars of a company to people who'd never stood in a backyard in their lives.

"Pip," he says, and hands one of the coffees to Simone, over my shoulder, into my room.

Simone takes it. She has a donut in one hand and a coffee in the other now, and she stands there in my room holding both, and none of us says anything for a while.

"Adam," I say. "Why is Simone in my room?"

He looks at the room. He looks at me. He takes a sip of his coffee.

"You barely use it," he says.

Behind me, the man in sales says "Absolutely" one more time, softer, and hangs up on the dial tone.

You barely use it.

He doesn't say it mean. That's the thing I'll think about at three in the morning for the next week. He says it like a fact on a slide. Like the occupancy rate of a conference room. He says it in the same voice he'd use to tell you that the Eight is now oatmeal.

"That's interesting," I say.

Adam has been married to me for eleven years and he knows what "that's interesting" means. Everybody in my family knows. My mother says it to the parish council. My brother Marek said it to a referee in a church league game once and the referee left the gym. It means you've already lost and you're the last to know. I watch Adam hear it. His thumb goes to the plastic lid of his coffee and presses the little tab down, presses it down again.

"Simone's running integration," he says. "She needs a door that closes. You're at the plant five days out of five. You come up here for the board and the holiday party. The room sits empty, Pip. People walk past it all day and it's dark. It looks like a museum."

"Integration of what?"

He glances at Simone. It's quick. It's nothing. It's the kind of glance you give the one person in the room who already knows the answer, to check that you're both going to give the same one.

I've seen that glance before. I used to be the person who got it.

"It's on the agenda for the Monday meeting," he says. "Which is right now. Which you'd know if you came to the Monday meeting."

"I'm here."

"You're here with donuts."

"They're from Marge's."

"I know where they're from, I can see the box." He breathes out through his nose. "Listen. We'll get you something better. A real office up here, south side, under the skylights. Whatever you want. You can design it."

"I designed this one."

"I know you did."

"I built this one."

"Pip. I know."

He says it gently, and there's a version of me that hears the gentleness and wants to put the donuts down. There's another version that's been sitting at a workbench at the plant for two years watching the parking lot every morning at seven. I don't know which one is going to talk until she does.

"Is that the plan for everything?" I ask. "You find out what I barely use, and then you give it to somebody who'll use it more?"

That lands somewhere. I see it land. He sets the tray down on the corner of a desk that belongs to somebody in marketing, who rolls her chair two feet away from it as though it might go off.

"That's not fair," he says, quiet.

"Did you ask me?"

"I'm asking you now."

"You're telling me now, in front of sales. That's a different verb."

"Pip, it's a room."

"It's a room," I agree. "It's ten by twelve with a door on the east side and a corner with no post in it. I know exactly what it is. I'm asking if you asked me."

He doesn't answer that. He looks at Simone instead, and that's an answer.

"That's interesting," I say again, and now a couple of people actually look up.

Simone sets her coffee on my windowsill next to her daughter. She doesn't set the donut down. She holds it like she's going to be asked to account for it later.

"I can take the south side," she says to Adam. "It isn't important to me where I sit."

"It's done," Adam says. "Facilities moved the badge access Friday."

Friday. I was at the plant Friday until nine at night with Lolo and a jig that wouldn't hold square. He didn't call. He doesn't call Fridays. He texts. *Late one, don't wait.* I didn't wait. I haven't waited in a long time. I made eggs and ate them over the sink and went to bed, and somewhere in the building where I used to have a door, a man from facilities was reprogramming a lock.

I look at the door. My father turned that pull on a lathe in our garage in 2014 out of a piece of brass rod from a bathroom fixture somebody threw out on Bridge Street. On the inside of the door, in carpenter's pencil on the raw birch, there's writing. Three lines, two different hands. You can't see all of it from here. The banker's box is leaning against the bottom of the door, holding it open, and it covers the top line.

I can see the bottom one. *3. Coffee at seven.* That line is in my handwriting. Underneath it, an arrow, and *(not 7:15)* in his.

I married him in that room. June of 2015, in my father's garage, before it had a desk in it or a company around it. A judge from the Kent County court who bowled with my uncle, my mother in her church hat, my father in a clean shirt holding the door open with his foot because the closer wasn't hung yet. Ten minutes. Afterward we had pierogi on a sheet of plywood on two sawhorses, and Adam made a speech to four people as if it were four hundred, and my father laughed until he had to sit down on a bucket.

I don't say any of that. You can't say a thing like that on a Monday in front of ninety people and a ficus.

"Okay," I say.

Adam's shoulders come down a quarter inch. He thinks I mean *okay*. I let him think it. He picks up the coffee tray again, the meeting already pulling on him from across the floor, the glass conference room where I can see the Monday people gathering, Gordon Pyle sweating into a cardigan, somebody setting out a projector clicker like it's a gavel.

"Come to the meeting," he says. "Please. There's something we need to walk through, and I'd rather you heard it from me."

"From you and Simone."

"From me."

"I'll be in in a minute," I say. "I'm going to give these out."

I hold up the donuts.

He looks at the box, then at me, and for half a second there's a face I don't have catalogued, something that might be about to ask a question. Then his phone buzzes in his pocket and he takes it out and the face is gone.

"Chicago," he says to Simone, not to me. "The Halvard dinner's at seven. I'm on the noon flight."

"I'll send you the deck tonight," she says.

"You're the best."

He says it the way you say it to a waiter. He says it the way he used to say it to me.

I go back across the floor. I give out donuts. I give out every donut in the box, cruller, glazed, bismarck, the long johns, the paczki Marge only makes in January because she can't help herself. I stop at every desk. I ask the woman in design whose headphones are on how her dad's hip is doing, and she takes the headphones off and tells me, for some time. I ask Bridget about the oatmeal. I tell the man in sales that next time he should call somebody real.

When the box is empty I fold it flat and put it in the recycling by the elevator.

Then I don't go to the meeting. I go to the stairwell, the old iron one in the northwest corner that nobody uses because the elevator's right there and the stairwell smells like pigeons and radiator paint. I go down one flight and stop on the landing at three where there's a window, tall and narrow and filthy, looking straight down onto Monroe Avenue.

From here I can see the whole width of the street. Two lanes, a bus stop, a fire hydrant. The streetlight posts are the old kind, cast iron, set back from the curb.

Plenty of room, I think.

Then I go down the rest of the stairs to my truck.

## Chapter 3 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The Clyde Park plant sounds like a kitchen the night before a wedding, if the kitchen were the size of a hockey rink and everybody in it were holding a welding torch.

I hear it from the parking lot. I always hear it from the parking lot, the long rising whine of the panel saw, the press brake going *chunk* like a church door, the hiss and crackle from the knee line where four welders stand in a row under the yellow curtains making the hidden frames that let my corner stand up without a post. I park the truck by the dumpsters where I always park it and sit there with the engine running and listen to it for a while. Some people have a beach. I have this.

Inside, Irene Kwiatkowski looks up from the glazing table and lifts her chin at me, which from Irene is a parade. Benny Szymanski is running the forklift with a cigarette behind his ear that he will not light, because I'll fire him, and he'll pretend it's because of the rule. He swings the forks around and idles up next to me.

"Boss. Thought you were downtown today."

"I was downtown today."

"Short day downtown." He looks at my face and then at the forks. "You want me to move something heavy for you? I'm feeling strong."

"Move the Traverse City unit to finishing, Benny."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant," I say, and he backs away beeping, which is Benny's version of a hug. Somebody's radio is playing Fleetwood Mac, the song every radio in Michigan plays at least once an hour in case you've forgotten you're sad.

I walk the floor. I walk it every morning. Today it's afternoon and I walk it anyway, past the wall framing jigs and the roof cassette line and the finishing bay where three Corners sit in a row under work lights, nearly done, their glass still wearing the blue protective film. One of them is going to a retired dentist in Ada who wants to paint in it. One is going to a couple in Traverse City who are getting divorced and need a place for him to live in the backyard while they figure out who gets the house. I know because I read every order. Nobody else reads every order. Adam says reading every order doesn't scale.

At my bench in the back corner, by the big rolling door to the yard, there's a weld sample I was in the middle of cutting on Friday, a section of the knee frame clamped in a vise with its cross-section sawn clean so you can see the bead. I sit down and pick up my file and look at it.

I can't work. The file goes back on the bench.

I get up and go out through the rolling door into the yard and there's a stack of pallets against the fence, and I kick one of them. Then I kick it again. I keep kicking it until it's the pallet's fault. That's a thing I do. I've done it since I was fourteen and my brothers told me girls can't weld, which I'd have argued about more if I hadn't been fourteen and holding a stick welder upside down. My oldest brother, Joe, found me kicking the garage door that night and stood there in his socks and didn't say anything. Then he went inside and came back out with my father's welding hood and put it on my head backwards so I couldn't see, and said, "There. Now you look like a welder."

The pallet has a crack in it now and I feel a little better, though not much.

My phone buzzes in my back pocket. It's Adam.

*Missed you in the meeting. We need to talk properly — tonight? I'm in Chicago til Tues PM. Call me after 10.*

*We need to talk properly*. He's the one who started talking improperly, as a policy, around the time the company passed two hundred employees and his calendar went from a calendar to a weather system. I put the phone in my back pocket.

It buzzes again, an email this time. From Simone Achterberg. Subject line: *Project North — integration workstreams (CONFIDENTIAL — leadership only)*. There's an attachment called *North_Integration_v14.pdf*, and the preview line says *Pip, as discussed with Adam, sharing the full deck so you're*—and then it cuts off.

As discussed with Adam. Version fourteen.

I hold my thumb over it. I could open it. I could read the whole thing standing right here in the yard by my cracked pallet. I've been getting Monday decks from this company for two years and I don't open them. I tell myself it's because they're written by people who say "learnings" and "socialize." The real reason is that every time I open one, something's in it I wasn't asked about. It's easier to know a thing is happening than to see the slide where it happens.

I swipe it into a folder called LATER. LATER has four hundred and some emails in it. LATER is my professional legacy.

Then I go back inside and up the iron steps to the little mezzanine office over the paint booth that I never use either, and I open the file cabinet in the corner. Second drawer down, a hanging file labeled *UNIT 001* in my own handwriting from when my handwriting was neater.

Bill of sale, sort of. A receipt from the steel yard on Market Avenue for the original frame stock, made out to *P. Sobczak*. A receipt from Grand Rapids Glass, also *P. Sobczak*, for two pieces of insulated glass with a polished edge, and a note on it in the counterman's handwriting: *good luck w/ the corner*. A photo of my father in his welding jacket with the room behind him on sawhorses, taken with a disposable camera, his face squinting into the sun like he's about to tell the photographer to move.

And the lease, two pages, stapled crooked. *Lessor: Philippa M. Sobczak. Lessee: Twodesk, Inc.* One dollar a year. I wrote it myself in 2018, on the kitchen table, the week before we craned the room into the Lindqvist building, because our lawyer at the time said we couldn't put my personal property on the company's insurance without one.
