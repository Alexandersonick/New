## Chapter 27 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

At eleven-forty on Friday night, second shift goes home, and I'm sitting in the break room at Clyde Park under the fluorescent lights with a cup of vending-machine hot chocolate and the whiteboard.

It's quiet. It's never quiet in here. The break room at shift change is the loudest room in the building: lockers banging, the microwave going, Benny telling a story about a deer, somebody's phone playing a video of somebody else's dog. Now it's empty, the chairs pushed in crooked, a Marge's box on the counter with one cruller in it that nobody had the nerve to take. The only sound is the vending machine humming and the clock over the door, which has run four minutes slow for as long as I've worked here and which everybody uses anyway, because it's the one that counts.

*ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 7,912.*

*HOLE: 2.3*
*STATE GRANT: −0.61*
*= 1.7*

*SATURDAYS × 12 WKS + 2ND SHIFT TRAINING (KNEE) = FASTER CUT = FASTER ESCROW*
*CREW EQUITY: 5% OF CO. FROM PIP'S SHARES → TRUST → EVERYONE ON THE FLOOR*

And under that, the box. Empty. A square I drew in blue marker on Thursday afternoon and didn't put anything in.

I've been looking at it for an hour.

Lolo comes in at eleven-fifty with her coat on and her lunch cooler in one hand and her bowling bag in the other, because it's Friday and the Spare Parts have a makeup match at midnight against the Bedpans, who had a pandemic of ER shifts on Monday and forfeited. She sees me at the table. She stops in the door.

"Hon," she says.

"Hi, Lolo."

"You sleep here now too? Is this a family thing?"

"I'm thinking."

"Thinking's for the daytime. At night it's called worrying." She puts her cooler down on the counter next to the cruller. She doesn't put the bowling bag down. She stands there looking at me, and then at the board, and then at me again. "He told you."

"Last night. On the porch. He rang the doorbell."

"The doorbell." Lolo makes the noise, the ketchup-on-pierogi noise. "That man. Eleven years he's had a key."

"He said the drive felt like a thing you get asked into."

Lolo doesn't say anything for a moment. Then she sets the bowling bag down on the floor, very carefully, and pulls out a chair across from me and sits in it, sideways, with one arm over the back, the way she sat on the folding chair in the mezzanine office the first day.

"So," she says. "Nashville."

"Gordon ran it. It fills the box."

"I know. Gordon told Darnell's wife's sister. Everybody knows. Benny knows. Irene knows. The Bedpans probably know." She looks at the board. "Without Saturdays."

"Saturdays would be voluntary. Paid. Time and a half."

"And the five percent?"

"Still goes to the crew. If I still want it to."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

Lolo nods slowly. She looks at the empty box for a long time.

"So what are you going to do, hon?"

I put the hot chocolate down. It's gone cold anyway. It was never really hot. That machine's been making hot chocolate the temperature of a bath since 2017.

"I don't know," I say. "That's why I'm sitting here. I don't know. If I take it, I'm closing his plant. Sixty people. A girl named Kiana who learned to read prints at a Wendy's. If I don't, I'm asking all of you for twelve weeks of Saturdays when I don't have to. He says it's his mistake to end. He says I should get to say no to him in front of everybody. He wants to stand up in front of a hundred and forty people and say *close my plant*, and he wants me to decide right there, out loud, with everybody watching." I look at her. "What would you do?"

Lolo looks at me for a long moment.

Then she takes her glasses off, slowly, and lets them drop on their beaded chain. I wait for it. My father used to say when Lolo takes her glasses off you better put yours on. I wait for whatever she's going to say that I'll need, because she always has it. Since I was a kid on a bucket by the welders, Lolo's always had it.

"No," she says.

"No what?"

"No, I'm not telling you." She folds her arms on the back of the chair. "You don't get my advice tomorrow, hon. You get my vote. And my vote's no. I told you that. I'm voting for Florida."

"Lolo—"

"I love you. You know I love you. I've known you since you had braces and a stick welder you held upside down." Her voice is very even. "But I'm not your dad, and I'm not your priest, and I'm not your husband. I'm a second-shift lead who wants a lanai. If I sit here and tell you what to do, then tomorrow when I stand up and vote against you, everybody's going to think I'm voting against my own advice, and you're going to think I'm the one who decided. I'm not deciding this for you. You've had enough people deciding for you. Him with his slides. You with yourself." She puts her glasses back on. "I'm going to stand up tomorrow and say *Pip, I want the pool, here's why*, in front of everybody, the way you asked me to. That's what I can give you. That's all."

I sit there. The vending machine hums. The clock over the door says 11:58, which means it's 12:02.

It's the first time in my life Lolo hasn't given me the answer.

And it's right. It's exactly right. I can feel it the way you feel a square joint when you run your thumb down it, no lip, no gap. She's not my oracle. She's not the old woman at the edge of the story who tells the heroine the truth. She's sixty-three years old, her hands go numb at night, and she wants Florida, and she's going to stand up in front of everybody and ask for it. That's worth more than an answer. That's the whole thing I've been trying to learn for a month.

"Okay," I say.

"Okay like a person?"

"Okay like a person." I almost laugh. "Who taught you that?"

"Your husband. At eleven o'clock on Wednesday, with a bowling ball. He kept saying it. *Okay like a person.* I thought he'd lost his mind out there in that room." She stands up and picks up her bowling bag. "Maybe he has. He's holding it better, though. The ball. He's got a good grip for a man who's never worked a day in his life."

"He's worked."

"Not with his hands, hon."

"He's working with them now," I say.

Lolo stops at the door with her bag. She looks at me over her glasses, one long look, the one that used to make my father put his glasses on.

"Yeah," she says. "I noticed."

She goes out. The door swings shut behind her on its slow closer. I hear her boots on the floor, out through the dark plant, past the knee line under its yellow curtains, past the press brake, past the glazing table with Irene's square capitals taped to the end of it, and then the side door, and the cold, and her Buick starting in the lot, coughing twice before it catches.

I sit there a long time.

Then I get up and take the blue marker off the tray under the whiteboard. I uncap it. I stand in front of the empty box with the marker in my hand, and I think about writing something in it. *NASH*. Or *NO*. Or a question mark, like Simone, neat, in the corner.

I don't. I put the cap back on.

Tomorrow I'll stand in front of this board with a hundred and forty people and my husband in a wrinkled shirt. And somebody's going to write in that box, and for once I don't know who.

Then I take the cap back off, and leave the marker on the tray, uncapped, where anyone can reach it.
