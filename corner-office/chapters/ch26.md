## Chapter 26 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

On Friday morning at six, sitting in Pip's chair in the room in the yard with my knees up and the afghan around my shoulders, I call Odell Haynes in La Vergne, Tennessee.

He picks up on the first ring. Odell gets up at four-thirty every day of his life, including Christmas. He says the hour before anybody else is awake is the only hour a plant manager owns.

"Boss," he says. "You're up early."

"I've been up since four."

"That's not early. That's insomnia." I hear a coffee maker going in the background, and a dog. "What can I do for you?"

I've had this call in my head since about two in the morning, when I gave up on sleeping and lay on the floor under the afghan looking at the cedar ceiling. I've had it every way. I've had it with a reason first and a cushion after. I've had it as a slide. I've had it as a speech about the future of work, God help me, and I lay there in the dark and laughed out loud at myself, alone in a ten-by-twelve room in a factory yard, like a lunatic.

"Odell," I say. "I'm going to tell you something before I do it, and then I'm going to ask you something. Is that all right?"

There's a pause. The coffee maker stops.

"Go ahead," Odell says.

"Tomorrow morning at six, Pip's putting a plan to the crew at Clyde Park. Both shifts. A vote. It's the plan that keeps the company alive without Halvard, and it's a good plan, and it's got a hole in it, and the hole's about a million seven. She's filling it with Saturdays. Twelve weeks of six-day weeks, and five percent of the company from her own shares to the crew for doing it." I take a breath. "There's another way to fill the hole. She left it off the board on purpose. She left it off because it's mine."

Odell doesn't say anything. He knows. He's known since the day I sat in his glass box and he told me what happens on a Friday.

"I'm going to stand up tomorrow in front of her crew," I say, "and disagree with her. I'm going to say don't take their Saturdays. Close Nashville."

The dog barks once in the background, somewhere in Murfreesboro.

"I'm telling you before," I say. "I'm not deciding it. The crew decides tomorrow, and Pip decides if she'll take it. But if it goes that way, you'll know first. Not from a parking lot."

For a long time there's nothing on the line but Odell breathing.

"Okay," he says finally. "What's the question?"

"What do you think?"

That's it. That's the question. *What do you think.* I've owned that plant for fourteen months. I picked the site and the architect and the consultant from Detroit and the color of the floor epoxy. I gave a speech on a scissor lift. I never asked the man who runs it what he thought about whether it should be there.

Odell laughs. Not a happy laugh. A long, tired one.

"I think about time, boss," he says. "I think it should've been in October. I think you knew it in October." The coffee maker starts again. "But you're asking, so here's the rest. Kiana. Terrell. Lynette on paint. Darius, the big kid on framing. They're good. They're the best I ever had. You tell your wife if she needs thirty people to learn the knee, she could do worse than sixty people who already know how to build her rooms, just the wrong way."

I sit there in the chair with the phone against my ear.

"They'd move?" I say.

"Some would. Kiana would. She told me last week, she said, *Mr. Haynes, if they ever close us, I'm going to Michigan to learn the one with no post*. She said it like a joke. She wasn't joking." He pauses. "You want me to ask them? Before?"

"Yes," I say. "Ask them. Please."

"Huh," says Odell. "Please."

"I'm trying it out."

"Well, try it faster," Odell says. "I've got a dog to walk."

I hang up. I sit there for a long time with the phone in my hand, looking out the glass corner at the yard going from black to gray, the maples on Clyde Park coming up out of the dark one branch at a time. At 6:40 Benny's Silverado comes down the street with its one bad headlight and turns in at the gate.

I'm still in the room. I said I'd be out before Benny. I've been out before Benny every morning for a week.

I watch him park by the dumpsters. I watch him get out and stand there with his lunch cooler, looking across the yard at the room with the light on and me in it, in Pip's chair with an afghan around my shoulders like an old woman at a bus stop.

He doesn't come over. He just lifts his chin at me, once, the way Irene lifts hers, and goes into the plant.

I take the afghan off and fold it with the corners square.

At ten I'm in the glass room on four with Gordon and the model, and I can't concentrate, and Gordon can tell. He keeps looking at me over his laptop like a man watching a pot he's been told not to watch.

"You're going to do it," he says finally.

"Do what?"

"Nashville. Tomorrow." He takes his glasses off and rubs them on his cardigan. "I ran it last night. After Simone's model. I put Nashville in the empty box at the bottom. Pip's empty box." He puts the glasses back on. "It fills it. Stop the Nashville line Monday and you're above six million on the twelfth without a single Saturday. The severance and the lease don't hit till March. Saturdays still make it faster. But you wouldn't need them to make it."

"I know."

"I didn't tell her. I thought—" He stops. He looks down at the laptop. "I thought you should."

"I'm going to."

"Before tomorrow?"

"Tonight."

Gordon nods slowly. Then he does a thing I've never seen Gordon Pyle do in six years. He reaches across the table and puts his hand on my arm, briefly, awkwardly, like a man touching a stove to see if it's hot.

"My father," he says, "used to say that the hardest number in any budget is the one you put in yourself."

"Your father was an accountant for the church."

"He was very wise about money," Gordon says. "He was terrible at everything else."

At seven-thirty that night I drive to the house on College Avenue for the first time in a week.

Her truck's in the drive. The kitchen light's on. I park on the street, not in the drive, because the drive feels like a thing you're asked into. I walk up the front steps, not the back door, and stand on the porch under the light in the cold, and I ring my own doorbell.

I hear her come down the hall. The good stairs, the ninth one, then the floorboards in the front hall that we never fixed. The porch light goes brighter. The door opens.

She's in my Grand Valley sweatshirt. Her hair's down. She's holding a dish towel.

"You rang the doorbell," she says.

"I don't live here right now."

"You have a key."

"I know. I'm not using it." I stand there with my hands in my coat pockets. "I have a before."

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she steps back.

"Come in," she says. "It's cold."

"I'd like to say it out here. If that's all right. It's short."

She stops. She folds the dish towel over her arm, slowly, and leans on the door frame, and waits.

"Tomorrow," I say, "at the vote, I'm going to stand up and disagree with you. In front of everybody. I'm going to say don't take their Saturdays. Close Nashville."

She doesn't move. Her face doesn't change. That's the side-of-a-building face, and I've learned that it's not nothing behind it, it's everything, held still so it doesn't spill.

"Gordon ran it," I say. "It fills your box. All of it. You'd make the test without a single Saturday. Saturdays still go faster, but they'd be voluntary. Time and a half, like they should be. And the five percent from your shares still goes to the crew, if you still want that, because they should have it anyway. And I called Odell this morning and asked him what he thought, and he said I should've done it in October, and he said sixty people in Tennessee already know how to build your rooms the wrong way. He's asking them tonight if they'd come up and learn the knee."

I stop. My breath's going up in a cloud in the porch light.

"You left it off the board for me," I say. "Simone told me. Gordon told me. Everybody knew but me. You left Nashville off because it's the only thing in the company that's mine, and you didn't want to be the one who turned the lights off. That's the kindest thing anybody's done for me in two years and it's the wrong decision, and I'm going to say so tomorrow in front of your crew. I'm telling you tonight so you're not alone with it when I do."

Pip looks at me for a long time.

"Why in front of the crew?" she says. "Why not just tell me now, and I'll change the board, and we do it my way?"

"Because then it's your decision to close my plant. You'd carry it. You'd pick up the heavy end." I take my hands out of my pockets. "It's my plant. It should be my mistake to end. Out loud. In front of the people whose Saturdays it's saving. And you should get to say no to me in front of them, if you want to."

She doesn't say anything. Behind her, down the hall, the radiator in the kitchen knocks twice.

"Okay," she says.

"Okay how?"

"Okay like a person. I heard you. I'm not saying yes." She unfolds the dish towel and folds it again, the other way. "I'm saying you told me before. I'll decide tomorrow, in the room, with everybody. That's what I'm asking them to do. I can't ask them to decide in front of me if I've already decided in my kitchen."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's just how it is." She looks past me at the street, at my car parked at the curb instead of in the drive. "You parked on the street."

"The drive felt like a thing you get asked into."

Something happens at the corner of her mouth. There and gone.

"Go back to the room," she says. "Sleep. You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping on your floor."

"I know. You've got a sleeping bag and a toothbrush and two shirts on my coat hook." She steps back inside. Her hand's on the door. "The shirts are wrinkled, Adam. Tomorrow you're standing up in front of a hundred and forty people who iron for church."

"I'll iron."

"You don't know how to iron."

"Then I'll be wrinkled," I say, "and right."

She looks at me through the half-open door for a long moment. The porch light's on her face and the hall light's behind her.

"Six a.m.," she says. "Don't be late."
