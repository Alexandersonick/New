## Chapter 26 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

On Friday morning at six, sitting in Pip's chair in the room in the yard with my knees up and the afghan around my shoulders, I call Odell Haynes in La Vergne, Tennessee.

He picks up on the first ring, because Odell gets up at four-thirty every day of his life, Christmas included, and says the hour before anybody else is awake is the only hour a plant manager owns.

"Boss," he says. "You're up early."

"I've been up since about four."

"That's not early, that's insomnia." I hear a coffee maker going in the background, and a dog. "What can I do for you?"

I've had this call in my head since about two in the morning, when I gave up on sleeping and lay on the floor under the afghan looking at the cedar ceiling. I've had it every way there is. I've had it with a reason first and a cushion after. I've had it as a slide. I've had it as a speech about the future of work, God help me, and I lay there in the dark and laughed out loud at myself, alone in a ten-by-twelve room in a factory yard.

"Odell," I say, "I'm going to tell you something before I do it, and then I'm going to ask you something. Is that all right?"

There's a pause, and in the background the coffee maker stops.

"Go ahead," Odell says.

"Tomorrow morning at six, Pip's putting a plan to the crew at Clyde Park, both shifts, a vote. It's the plan that keeps the company alive without Halvard, and it's a good plan, and it's got a hole in it, and the hole's about a million seven. She's filling it with Saturdays: twelve weeks of six-day weeks, and five percent of the company from her own shares to the crew for doing it." I take a breath. "There's another way to fill the hole. She left it off the board on purpose, because it's mine."

Odell lets that sit. He's known since the day I sat in his glass box and he told me what happens on a Friday.

"I'm going to stand up tomorrow in front of her crew," I say, "and disagree with her. I'm going to say don't take their Saturdays. Close Nashville."

The dog barks once in the background, somewhere in Murfreesboro.

"I'm not deciding it," I say. "The crew decides tomorrow, and Pip decides if she'll take it. But if it goes that way, you'll know first, and you won't hear it in a parking lot."

There's nothing on the line for a while but Odell breathing.

"Okay," he says finally. "What's the question?"

"What do you think?"

That's the whole question. I picked the site and the architect and the consultant from Detroit and the color of the floor epoxy, and I gave a speech on a scissor lift, and in fourteen months I didn't ask the man who runs the place whether it should be there.

Odell laughs, long and tired, the laugh of somebody who has been up since four-thirty for thirty years.

"I think about time, boss," he says. "I think it should've been in October. I think you knew it in October." The coffee maker starts again. "But you're asking, so here's the rest: Kiana, Terrell, Lynette on paint, Darius, the big kid on framing. They're good, the best I ever had. You tell your wife if she needs thirty people to learn the knee, she could do worse than sixty people who already know how to build her rooms, just the wrong way."

I sit there in her chair with the phone against my ear. "They'd move?"

"Some would, and Kiana would. She told me last week, she said, *Mr. Haynes, if they ever close us, I'm going to Michigan to learn the one with no post*. She said it as a joke, and she wasn't joking." He pauses. "You want me to ask them?"

"Yes," I say. "Ask them tonight, please."

"Please," Odell says, turning the word over like he's checking the bottom for a price tag.

"I'm trying it out this week."

"Well, try it faster," Odell says. "I've got a dog to walk."

I hang up and sit with the phone in my hand, looking out the glass corner at the yard going from black to gray, the maples on Clyde Park coming up out of the dark one branch at a time. Just before seven, Benny's Silverado comes down the street with its one bad headlight and turns in at the gate.

I'm still in the room. I said I'd be out before Benny, and I've been out before Benny every morning for a week.

He parks by the dumpsters and gets out and stands there with his lunch cooler, looking across the yard at the room with the light on and me in it, in Pip's chair with an afghan around my shoulders like an old woman at a bus stop.

He doesn't come over. He lifts his chin at me, once, the way Irene lifts hers, and goes into the plant.

I take the afghan off and fold it with the corners square.

At ten I'm in the glass room on four with Gordon and the model, and I can't concentrate, and Gordon can tell. He keeps glancing at me over his laptop like a man watching a pot he's been told not to watch.

"You're going to do it," he says finally.

I keep my eyes on the spreadsheet and say, "Do what?"

"Nashville, tomorrow." He wipes his palms on his cardigan, one and then the other. "I ran it last night, after Simone's model. I put Nashville in the empty box at the bottom, Pip's empty box. Two levers. Stop the line Monday and you don't spend a week of Nashville, four hundred and twenty thousand, before the test on Friday. And the forty finished Lites sitting in their yard, the campground people in Gatlinburg have been asking about them since November; that's the rest of the hole over twelve weeks. You're above six million on the twelfth by about one week of Nashville. Without a single Saturday. Saturdays make the twelve weeks faster. You wouldn't need them for Friday."

"I know. I ran it at three this morning on a legal pad, and my version was uglier."

"I didn't tell her. I thought—" He stops and looks down at the laptop. "I thought you should."

"I'm going to tell her tonight, at the house." I close the laptop lid on my side. "And Gordon, don't tell anybody else. Not Ruth, not the board, nobody, until she's heard it from me."

"Who would I tell?" He looks genuinely wounded. "Ruth's going to send you a fruit basket when she hears, you know. She's been asking you to close it since August."

"Then Ruth can hear it Monday with everybody in La Vergne, and she can send the basket to Odell."

Gordon nods slowly, the way he nods at a column that finally reconciles. Then he does a thing I've never seen Gordon Pyle do in six years: he reaches across the table and puts his hand on my arm for about a second, as if my arm might be hot.

"My father," he says, "used to say that the hardest number in any budget is the one you put in yourself."

"Your father did the books for a church, Gordon."

"He was very wise about money," Gordon says. "He was terrible at everything else."

At seven-thirty that night I drive to the house on College Avenue, which I haven't seen in a week.

Her truck's in the drive and the kitchen light's on. I park on the street, because the drive feels like a thing you're asked into. I walk up the front steps instead of going round to the back door, and stand on the porch under the light in the cold, and I ring my own doorbell.

I hear her come down the hall: the good stairs, the ninth one, then the floorboards in the front hall that we never fixed. The porch light goes brighter and the door opens.

She's in my Grand Valley sweatshirt with her hair down, holding a dish towel.

"You rang the doorbell," she says.

"I don't live here right now."

"You have a key."

"I know, and I'm not using it." I stand there with my hands in my coat pockets. "I have a before."

She studies me from the doorway for a while, then steps back. "Come in, it's cold."

"I'd like to say it out here, if that's all right, because it's short."

She folds the dish towel over her arm, slowly, and leans on the door frame, and waits.

"Tomorrow," I say, "at the vote, I'm going to stand up and disagree with you in front of your crew. I'm going to say don't take their Saturdays, close Nashville."

Nothing moves in her face. It's the face she gives a building inspector, and I've learned there's everything behind it, held still so it doesn't spill.

"Gordon ran it," I say. "It fills your box, all of it. You'd make the test without a single Saturday. Saturdays still go faster, but they'd be voluntary, time and a half, like they should be. And the five percent from your shares still goes to the crew, if you still want that, because they should have it anyway. And I called Odell this morning and asked him what he thought, and he said I should've done it in October, and he said sixty people in Tennessee already know how to build your rooms the wrong way. He's asking them tonight if they'd come up and learn the knee."

I stop, and my breath goes up in a cloud in the porch light.

"You left it off the board for me," I say. "Simone told me, and then Gordon told me, and everybody knew but me. You left Nashville off because it's the only thing in the company that's mine, and you didn't want to be the one who turned the lights off. That's the kindest thing anybody's done for me since Nashville, and it's the wrong decision, and I'm going to say so tomorrow in front of your crew. I'm telling you tonight so you're not alone with it when I do."

Pip turns the towel over on her arm and doesn't answer right away.

"Why in front of the crew?" she says. "Why not just tell me now, and I'll change the board, and we do it my way?"

"Because then you'd be right about Nashville in private again, and I'd be wrong in private again, and nobody would ever have to hear me say it." I take my hands out of my pockets. "It's my plant, so it should be my mistake to end, out loud, in front of the people whose Saturdays it's saving. And you should get to say no to me in front of them, if you want to."

Down the hall behind her, the kitchen radiator knocks twice, and she says, "Okay."

"Okay meaning yes, or okay meaning go away?"

"Okay meaning I heard you. I'm not saying yes." She unfolds the dish towel and folds it again, the other way. "You told me first, and I'll decide tomorrow, in the room, with everybody. That's what I'm putting to them. I can't ask them to decide in front of me if I've already decided in my kitchen."

"That's fairer than I was."

"It's not fair, it's just how it is." She looks past me at the street, at my car parked at the curb instead of in the drive. "You parked on the street."

"The drive felt like a thing you get asked into."

Her nose wrinkles, once, which means she's decided not to laugh at something.

"Have you eaten?" she says.

"I had a cruller around ten."

"A cruller isn't dinner, Adam." She goes back down the hall without closing the door, and I stand on my own porch and listen to the freezer open and shut, and she comes back with a foil package the size of a brick and puts it in my hands. It's cold and heavy and it crackles. "My mother sent forty. She thinks you're living in a shed."

"I am living in a shed."

"Don't tell her that or she'll send forty more."

"There's no stove in the shed."

"Then you'll eat them cold, the way my uncles do at a wake." She steps back inside, one hand on the door. "Go back to the room and sleep, you look terrible."

"I've been sleeping on your floor."

"I know, you've got a sleeping bag and a toothbrush and two shirts on my coat hook. The shirts are wrinkled, Adam. Tomorrow you're standing up in front of a hundred and forty people who iron for church."

"Then I'll iron them tonight."

"You don't know how to iron."

"Then I'll be wrinkled," I say, "and right."

She stands in the half-open door a while longer, with the porch light on her face and the hall light behind her.

"Six a.m.," she says. "Don't be late."
