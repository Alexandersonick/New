## Chapter 17 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Simone Achterberg lives on the eleventh floor of a building on Ionia Avenue that used to be a cold-storage warehouse for a meatpacker. There's a plaque in the lobby about it. *Here, from 1911 to 1974, the Valley City Packing Company kept West Michigan supplied in ham.* Now there's a doorman named Felix and a lobby that smells like eucalyptus, and the elevator plays something with a harp in it.

She opens the door in reading glasses and a gray sweatshirt that says MSU ENGINEERING MOM, holding a glass of white wine. She looks me over the way she looked at the gauge on the test rig.

"Pip," she says, and doesn't move out of the doorway.

"Can I come in for a minute?"

"It is nine-forty at night."

"I know what time it is."

She thinks about it, then steps back and opens the door wider.

The condo is one big room with very high ceilings and old brick walls and huge factory windows looking south over the city, all the lights, the Lindqvist building's water tower lit up green for some reason, the arena, the long dark line of the river. It's a beautiful room with almost nothing in it: a couch, a table with two chairs, a bed behind a folding screen. And boxes, eleven of them, against the wall by the kitchen, banker's boxes and moving boxes and one big wardrobe box, all labeled in black marker in her small level printing: KITCHEN MISC., BOOKS (HANNAH'S?), WINTER, PHOTOS. One of them just says DO NOT.

"You haven't unpacked," I say.

"I told you that I had not."

"You said eleven, but I didn't think you meant it."

"I always mean the number." She goes into the kitchen, which is a long steel counter along the brick wall, and takes another wineglass down from a cabinet that has, as far as I can see, four glasses and two plates in it. "Will you have some wine?"

"Yes," I say, "a big one."

She pours it and hands it to me, and we stand there on either side of her steel counter, the way Adam and I stand at either end of ours, and I think, *I've been standing at counters with people since Nashville*.

"I'm not here to fight," I say.

"I didn't think you were, or you would have come in the daytime." She sips her wine and waits. "So why are you here?"

I take my phone out of my coat pocket and open the photo of the whiteboard in the break room at Clyde Park, Irene's square capitals, *1 SHIFT: 1,800. 2 SHIFTS: 3,400. 2 SHIFTS + SAT: 4,100*, Darnell's little crane drawn underneath. I set it on the steel counter and slide it across to her.

"I need seven point four million dollars in thirty days," I say, "and I can't do arithmetic on money. I can do it on steel and I can do it on glass. I can tell you how many knees four welders can make in an eleven-hour shift with one of them hungover, but money I can't do, so I need somebody who can."

Simone looks at the phone without picking it up.

"Gordon can do money," she says.

"Gordon sweats when he does money, and I need somebody who does it calm."

"Why me?"

"Because you drove out to the plant to find out why there's no post in the corner. Nobody else did that since Nashville, not Ruth, not Kurt, not him."

She doesn't answer right away. She turns her wineglass a quarter turn by the stem, and then another quarter, like somebody working a combination lock.

"You are asking me to help you stop the deal I was hired to close," she says.

"Yes," I say, "I am."

"I have a daughter at State. I have a lease on this apartment. I have eleven boxes I have not unpacked, because I did not know whether I would be staying." She looks at the boxes. "If Halvard buys the company, I am the integration lead. That is a good job. It is a very good job. If Halvard does not buy the company, then in nine weeks there may not be a company."

"I know there may not."

"So you would like me to bet against myself."

"I'm asking you to," I say. "That's all it is. If you say no, I'll finish your wine and go."

Something shifts in her face, the way it did in my room in the yard when I said *he's not the only person awake*: her mouth goes thin, and her eyes go to the boxes and come back.

"Nobody has asked me for anything since May," she says. "Do you know that? I have been at that company eight months. People bring me problems. People bring me decks. Your husband brings me numbers at one in the morning and tells me stories about a woman with a poodle in Holland. Nobody has asked me for anything that cost me something to give." She picks up her wine and puts it down without drinking. "I would have liked to be asked by him, but he would never ask me anything that mattered. I'm not his."

"No," I say, "you're not."

"I knew that, and I want you to know I knew it," she says to the counter. "I told him to tell you about Northgate. In October, on the phone, the night he signed, I said, *go upstairs and tell your wife*. I said it twice, and he said he would."

I put my glass down on the steel.

I came here braced for a lot of things, the navy sweater, the ficus, a woman who knew my husband's late-night voice better than I did. I didn't brace for the woman on the other end of that call telling him to hang up and go upstairs.

"Why are you telling me that?" I ask.

"Because you asked me something," Simone says, "so I am telling you something. That is how it works, I think, though it has been a while."

She picks up my phone and studies the whiteboard photo for a full minute. Then she pushes her reading glasses up her nose and goes to the box against the wall that says OFFICE.

"What's in DO NOT?" I say.

"My wedding album," Simone says, digging, "and a waffle iron that was a wedding present. I have not decided which one goes down the chute first."

She comes back with a legal pad and a calculator, an actual calculator with paper tape, and sets them on the counter and sits down on one of the two chairs.

"Sit," she says, and I sit.

We work until a quarter to two.

She's so fast. I've seen Adam work a room and Gordon work a spreadsheet, and I've never seen anybody work a problem the way Simone works it, quietly, in order, no wasted motion, like a surgeon with steady hands who's done this exact operation a thousand times and is a little bored. She asks me questions and writes down my answers in her small printing and runs the calculator, and the paper tape curls off the end of it onto the counter and down toward the floor.

"Who is your steel mill?" she says.

"Great Lakes Specialty, in Monroe, Gil Chamberlain. I've bought from him since the garage, net thirty."

"Can you get sixty days out of him?"

"I've never asked."

"Then ask," she says, and writes it down. "If he gives you sixty, you cut the steel before you pay for it, and when the steel is cut, the escrow releases, ten percent at steel-cut, your rule." She runs the tape. "At sixty days, your first four hundred units cut in week one release a million five, and you pay Gil in week nine out of the escrow his own steel released."

I stare at the curl of tape coming off the calculator.

"That is the money before the money," Simone says, "or some of it. Now, who does your glass?"

"Grand Rapids Glass and Holland Glazing."

"Same question for both of them."

"I'll ask them both tomorrow."

"Tell each one the other is being asked, and make them compete." She writes. "Labor. You need a second shift, which means thirty people who can weld the knee."

"There aren't thirty people in Michigan who can do that."

"Then you will have to train them. How long does that take?"

"Six weeks to make a welder I'd let near a knee. Twelve to make one I'd trust."

"Who trains them?"

"My four knee welders, and Lolo."

"Lolo is sixty-three," Simone says, without looking up. "Does she want to spend her Saturdays teaching thirty strangers to weld?"

"I've never asked her that either."

Simone writes something on the pad and doesn't read it out to me. "Your four are already welding knees eleven hours a day." She taps her pen on the pad. "You need them to train and weld, which is overtime, which is Saturdays." She runs the tape again and frowns at it. "That is about two point eight million dollars over the gap, after the steel and the glass terms. That is your hole, Pip: two point eight, not seven point four."

I look at the two point eight on the tape. It's still a number I don't have, but it's a number, and a number has an edge you can get a grinder on.

Some time after midnight the tape runs out. Simone goes to the box marked WINTER and comes back with a new roll.

"Why is the calculator paper in winter?" I say.

"Because I packed it in October," she says, threading it, "and I knew I would be doing this kind of arithmetic by February."

I laugh, and she looks up, pleased, like I've passed something.

"And there's this," she says a while later, and draws a box at the bottom of the pad, and writes in it in her small printing: *NASH*. Then she sits there with her pen still, looking at the box.

I know what's in the box: four hundred and twenty thousand a week. Seven weeks of Nashville and the hole is gone.

"No," I say.

Simone doesn't argue or ask why. She looks over her reading glasses at me, and then she draws a single line through the box, neat, the way you'd mark something *not now*, and writes a small question mark next to it.

"It's his," I say. "It's the only thing in the company that's his. He's got sixty people down there and he hired every one of them, and there's a girl who learned to read prints. I won't walk into the first thing he ever built without me and turn the lights off, not to save my plan."

"I understand," Simone says.

"You think I'm wrong, though."

"I think it is four hundred and twenty thousand dollars a week." She puts the pen down. "And I think you are being kind to him in a way he has not been kind to you. That is not my business, so I will write it down as an open item."

She does, in the margin, in printing so small I'd need a loupe.

When I finally stand up and put my coat on, the tape is three feet long again and curled on the floor like a white tail. Simone tears it off, rolls it, snaps a rubber band around it and hands it to me.

"Give that to the board," she says.

"Thank you," I say, and tuck it in my coat.

"Don't thank me yet. You have twenty-nine days." She walks me to the door. "I am still the integration lead for Project North. I will still be on Kurt's calls. I will tell you what he says, and I will tell him nothing about this." She stops with her hand on the door. "I want it in writing that I asked you not to thank me."

"Simone," I say from the hallway.

"Yes."

"I don't care about the credit. Somebody's going to say this is about whose name is on the patent. Kurt can put his name on the patent and his face on the box. I don't care who they think invented it. I care that there's no post."

She gives me the look my mother gives a priest who's run long.

"I know, Pip, I read the protocol," she says. "*The eye goes to the corner.*"

Downstairs, Felix the doorman is asleep in his chair with his mouth open, and I let myself out into the cold. Ionia Avenue is empty, the snow coming down slow and big now, the soft kind, under the streetlights. My truck is parked on the street with an inch on the windshield and a parking ticket going soft under the wiper.

I stand on the sidewalk and look up at the eleventh floor, where one window is still lit, high up, in the long row of dark factory glass.

There's a text from Adam from this afternoon that I haven't opened. I've been carrying it around since the ramp like a stone in my coat pocket.

I take it out and open it: a phone number, a name, a drawer, left side, under the Nashville lease, and a password.

*Your mother's maiden name and the year we got married.*

I stand there in the snow on Ionia Avenue reading it over and over until the screen goes dark in my hand.
