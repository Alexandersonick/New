## Chapter 17 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Simone Achterberg lives on the eleventh floor of a building on Ionia Avenue that used to be a cold-storage warehouse for a meatpacker. There's a plaque in the lobby about it. *Here, from 1911 to 1974, the Valley City Packing Company kept West Michigan supplied in ham.* Now there's a doorman named Felix and a lobby that smells like eucalyptus, and the elevator plays something with a harp in it.

She opens the door in reading glasses and a gray sweatshirt that says MSU ENGINEERING MOM, holding a glass of white wine. She looks at me for a moment the way she looked at the gauge on the test rig.

"Pip," she says.

"Can I come in?"

"It is nine-forty at night."

"I know what time it is."

She thinks about it. Then she steps back and opens the door wider.

The condo is one big room with very high ceilings and old brick walls and huge factory windows looking south over the city, all the lights, the Lindqvist building's water tower lit up green for some reason, the arena, the long dark line of the river. It's a beautiful room. There's almost nothing in it. A couch. A table with two chairs. A bed behind a folding screen. And boxes. Eleven of them, against the wall by the kitchen, banker's boxes and moving boxes and one big wardrobe box, all labeled in black marker in her small level printing. KITCHEN — MISC. BOOKS — HANNAH'S? WINTER. PHOTOS. One of them just says DO NOT.

"You haven't unpacked," I say.

"I told you."

"You said eleven. I didn't think you meant it."

"I always mean the number." She goes into the kitchen, which is a long steel counter along the brick wall, and takes another wineglass down from a cabinet that has, as far as I can see, four glasses and two plates in it. "Wine?"

"Yes."

She pours. She hands it to me. We stand there on either side of her steel counter, the way Adam and I stand at either end of ours, and I think, *I've been standing at counters with people for two years*.

"I'm not here to fight," I say.

"I didn't think you were. You would have come in the daytime." She sips her wine. "Why are you here?"

I take my phone out of my coat pocket. I open the photo of the whiteboard in the break room at Clyde Park, Irene's square capitals, *1 SHIFT: 1,800. 2 SHIFTS: 3,400. 2 SHIFTS + SAT: 4,100*, Darnell's little crane drawn underneath. I put the phone on the steel counter and slide it across to her.

"I need seven point four million dollars in thirty days," I say. "And I can't do arithmetic on money. I can do it on steel. I can do it on glass. I can tell you how many knees four welders can make in an eleven-hour shift with one of them hungover. I can't do money. I need somebody who can do money."

Simone looks at the phone. She doesn't pick it up.

"Gordon can do money," she says.

"Gordon sweats when he does money. I need somebody who does it calm."

"Why me?"

"Because you drove out to the plant to find out why there's no post in the corner." I look at her. "Nobody else did that. Not in two years. Not Ruth. Not Kurt. Not him."

She's quiet for a long time. She takes her reading glasses off and folds them and sets them on the counter next to my phone. She turns her wineglass a quarter turn by the stem.

"You are asking me to help you stop the deal I was hired to close," she says.

"Yes."

"I have a daughter at State. I have a lease on this apartment. I have eleven boxes I have not unpacked, because I did not know whether I would be staying." She looks at the boxes. "If Halvard buys the company, I am the integration lead. That is a good job. It is a very good job. If Halvard does not buy the company, then in nine weeks there may not be a company."

"I know."

"So you are asking me to bet against myself."

"I'm asking you," I say. "That's all. I'm not telling you. I'm asking."

She looks at me. Something moves behind her face, the way it did in my room in the yard when I said *he's not the only person awake*, a measurement being taken again with a better tool.

"Nobody has asked me for anything since May," she says. "Do you know that? I have been at that company eight months. People bring me problems. People bring me decks. Your husband brings me numbers at one in the morning and tells me stories about a woman with a poodle in Holland. Nobody has asked me for anything that cost me something to give." She picks up her wine. Puts it down without drinking. "I would have liked to be asked. By him. He would never ask me anything that mattered. I'm not his."

"No," I say. "You're not."

"I knew that. I want you to know I knew that." She says it to the counter. "I told him to tell you. About Northgate. In October, on the phone, the night he signed. I said, *go upstairs and tell your wife*. I said it twice. He said he would."

I put my glass down.

I didn't expect that. I came here braced for a lot of things. The navy sweater, the ficus, a woman who knew my husband's voice at one in the morning better than I did. I didn't brace for the woman on the other end of that call telling him to hang up and go upstairs.

"Why are you telling me that?" I ask.

"Because you asked me something," Simone says. "So I am telling you something. That is how it works, I think. It has been a while."

She picks up my phone off the counter and looks at the whiteboard photo. She looks at it for a full minute. Then she puts her reading glasses back on and goes to the box against the wall that says OFFICE and pulls out a legal pad and a calculator, an actual calculator with paper tape, and brings them back to the counter and sits down on one of the two chairs.

"Sit," she says.

I sit.

We work until a quarter to two.

She's fast. She's so fast. I've seen Adam work a room and I've seen Gordon work a spreadsheet and I've never seen anybody work a problem the way Simone works it, quietly, in order, no wasted motion, like a surgeon who's done this exact operation a thousand times and is a little bored. She asks me questions and writes down my answers in her small printing and runs the calculator, and the paper tape curls off the end of it onto the counter and down toward the floor.

"Steel," she says. "Who's your mill?"

"Great Lakes Specialty, in Monroe. Rob Chamberlain. I've bought from him since the garage."

"What are your terms?"

"Net thirty."

"Can you get sixty?"

"I've never asked."

"Ask." She writes. "If he gives you sixty, you cut the steel before you pay for it. When the steel's cut, the escrow releases. Your rule. Ten percent at steel-cut." She runs the tape. "At sixty days, your first four hundred units cut in week one release a million five, and you pay Rob in week nine out of the escrow his own steel released."

I stare at the tape.

"That's—"

"That's the money before the money," Simone says. "Some of it. Glass?"

"Grand Rapids Glass and Holland Glazing."

"Same question."

"I'll ask."

"Ask both. Make them compete." She writes. "Labor. You need a second shift. You need thirty people who can weld the knee."

"There aren't thirty people in Michigan who can weld the knee."

"Then train them. How long?"

"Six weeks to make a welder I'd let near a knee. Twelve to make one I'd trust."

"Who trains?"

"My four. And Lolo."

"Your four are already welding knees eleven hours a day." Simone taps her pen on the pad. "You need them to train and weld. That is overtime. That is Saturdays." She runs the tape again and frowns at it. "That is about two point eight million dollars over the gap, after the steel and the glass terms. That is your hole, Pip. Two point eight. Not seven point four."

Two point eight. I look at the number on the tape. It's still a number I don't have. But it's a number. It's not a wall.

"And there's this," Simone says, and draws a box at the bottom of the pad, and writes in it in her small printing: *NASH*. She doesn't say anything else. She just sits there, pen still, looking at the box.

I know what's in the box. Four hundred and twenty thousand a week. Seven weeks of Nashville and the hole is gone.

"No," I say.

Simone doesn't argue. She doesn't ask why. She just looks at me over her reading glasses for a long moment, and then she draws a single line through the box, neat, not crossing it out, just a line, the way you'd mark something *not now*. She writes a small question mark next to it.

"It's his," I say. "It's the only thing in the company that's his. He's got sixty people down there. He hired every one of them. There's a girl who learned to read prints. I won't walk into the first thing he ever built without me and turn the lights off. Not to save my plan."

"I understand," Simone says.

"You think I'm wrong."

"I think it is four hundred and twenty thousand dollars a week." She puts the pen down. "And I think you are being kind to him in a way he has not been kind to you. That is not my business. I will write it down as an open item."

She writes it down as an open item.

At a quarter to two I stand up and put my coat on. The paper tape is three feet long now and curled on the floor like a long white tail. Simone tears it off and rolls it up and snaps a rubber band around it and hands it to me.

"For the board," she says.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. You have twenty-nine days." She walks me to the door. "I am still the integration lead for Project North. I will still be on Kurt's calls. I will tell you what he says. I will tell him nothing about this." She stops with her hand on the door. "I want it in writing that I asked you not to thank me."

"Simone."

"Yes."

"I don't care about the credit," I say. "I want you to know that. Somebody's going to say this is about whose name is on the patent. Kurt can put his name on the patent. He can put his face on the box. I don't care who they think invented it. I care that there's no post."

She looks at me with an expression I can't read.

"I know," she says. "I read the protocol. *The eye goes to the corner*."

Downstairs, Felix the doorman is asleep in his chair with his mouth open. I let myself out into the cold. Ionia Avenue is empty, the snow coming down slow and big now, the soft kind, under the streetlights. My truck is parked on the street with an inch on the windshield.

I stand on the sidewalk and look up at the building. Eleventh floor. One window is still lit, high up, in the long row of dark factory glass.

I take my phone out. There's a text from Adam from this afternoon that I haven't opened. I've been carrying it around since the ramp like a stone in my pocket, the way you carry a letter you're afraid of.

I open it. A phone number. A name. A drawer, left side, under the Nashville lease. A password.

*Your mother's maiden name and the year we got married.*

I stand there in the snow on Ionia Avenue reading it over and over until the screen goes dark in my hand.
