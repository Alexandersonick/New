## Chapter 22 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

I sit in my car outside the gate at Clyde Park for two hours and ten minutes with the engine running, waiting for second shift to go home.

I don't want anybody to see me. That's the first thing I know about tonight, before I know anything else. I don't want Lolo at the gate with a bowling hat. I don't want Benny on the forklift saying *boss* in that voice that means *I know*. I don't want to walk across that yard in front of forty people who stood in a doorway yesterday and clapped for my wife while I found out from Tom Bielski. So I park across the street in the lot of the Reformed church with the sign that says *GOD ANSWERS KNEE-MAIL*, and I turn off the headlights, and I sit there and watch the plant through the windshield like a man on a stakeout of his own life.

At 11:32 the rolling door comes down. At 11:40 the cars start leaving. Lolo's Buick, Darnell's minivan, a dozen pickups. At 11:51 the lot lights go to their night setting, half of them off, and the plant goes dark except for the exit signs and the yard light by the fence, which stays on all night, white, over the room.

At 11:55 I text her, because she said before. *I'm going to sleep in the room in the yard. I'll be out before first shift. I'm telling you so you don't hear it from Benny.* I send it before I can make it better. Then I drive across the street and punch the gate code. Pip's birthday. The gate rolls back on its track with a long iron complaint.

I've had a key to the room since 2018. It's on my ring between the house and the Audi, a brass Schlage key with a dab of red nail polish on the bow that Pip put there so I'd stop trying it in the wrong locks. I haven't used it in two years. My hand's cold and it takes three tries. The lock's the one Stan put in, a heavy old deadbolt from a hardware store on Bridge Street that went out of business in 2012, and it turns hard, the way it always did, and then gives.

The heater's on low. She put in a better one, she said. It's warm. It smells like cedar and primer and the soap she uses, Ivory, the plain kind, she's used it since she was twelve because her mother bought it in bulk.

I don't turn on the desk lamp. The yard light comes in through the glass corner, enough to see by. Her desk. Her chair. The vise with a weld sample still in it, sawn clean. A coffee mug with a ring of dried sludge in the bottom. The graph paper, a stack of it, face down, squared to the corner of the desk.

I don't sit in my chair, the one by the radiator. I don't know why. I sit in hers.

It's the wrong height for me. She's six inches shorter and she sets it low so her feet are flat on the floor, the way her father taught her to stand at a bench. My knees come up. I sit there with my knees up like a kid at a grown-up's desk and I look out the corner, past where the post would be, at the yard and the fence and the maples on Clyde Park standing up black against the orange sky.

*Now you know.*

I said that to her. Six hours ago, at my end of the counter, with the whiskey I didn't drink. I watched it go into her. I know what it looks like when something goes into Pip, because it's so rare. She doesn't flinch. She goes still, the way the floor goes still when somebody drops a sheet of glass, and you wait, and you don't know yet if it broke.

It was true. That's the thing. It was the truest thing I've said to her in two years, and I said it like a weapon.

I sit in her chair till one. Then I get up and get the moving blanket off the shelf by the door, the quilted gray one Darnell keeps there to wrap the glass when they set a unit, and I lie down on the floor on my mother-in-law's braided rug with my coat for a pillow and the blanket over me, and I look up at the cedar ceiling.

I don't sleep. Around three, I think about coffee at seven.

Here's the true version. Not the forward-looking one from my bio.

The morning after Nashville, two years ago, a Wednesday, I came off the elevator at five to seven with my espresso and a thermos of her moka-pot coffee that I'd made at home in her pot, because I always made hers on the mornings she drove straight in from her parents' place. I walked across the floor in the dark. I opened the door with this key. I sat down in the chair by the radiator and put my feet up and waited for her.

She didn't come. I sat there till seven-thirty. I figured she was mad. I figured she was right to be mad. I'd stood on the stage at the all-hands the day before and announced a second plant in Tennessee with a slide, and she'd found out with three hundred people, and I'd watched her face from the stage, and I'd kept going, because I was in the middle of a pitch and that's what I do in the middle of a pitch. I figured she'd come the next day. I poured her coffee into the fern by the elevator and went to work.

I came the next day. And the next. Fifteen working days, three weeks, every morning at five to seven with two cups. I told myself I was being patient. I told myself she'd come when she was ready and I'd be there. I didn't call her. I didn't text. I didn't go out to Clyde Park and knock on the glass. I didn't say *I'm sorry about the slide*. I sat in a chair and waited for her to come to me, like a man who'd decided that showing up counted as asking.

On the sixteenth morning I had a flight to Nashville at eight. I didn't go up. I told myself it was one morning. And then the next morning was easier.

And somewhere in there I decided she'd stopped. That it was her. That she'd made her choice and I'd shown up for three weeks and she hadn't. I've been carrying that around for two years like a receipt.

The fern died. They replaced it with a fake one.

I'm lying on the floor of her room at three in the morning and I understand, finally, that we did the same thing. She stayed away to make me ask. I showed up to make her come. Neither of us said one word. We each decided alone what the other one meant, and then we each lived in it, in separate rooms, for two years, filing things.

*Now you know.*

I knew. I always knew. I just never said it to her face until I could use it.

At 4:40 I get up and fold the moving blanket and put it back on the shelf, corners square, the way my mother folded towels. I put her chair back at her height. I check that the graph paper's still face down and squared. I turn the heater back to low. I let myself out and lock Stan's deadbolt and walk across the yard in the dark to my car, and I'm through the gate and across the street in the church lot before Benny's Silverado comes down Clyde Park at 5:15 with its one bad headlight.

Nobody sees me. That's the point.

Saturday the plant's closed. I spend the day at a Panera in Wyoming with my laptop, answering Ruth and Diane and Gordon, telling all three of them I'm working from home. It's a lie. I don't feel good about it. I tell myself it's a lie about geography.

Saturday night I'm back in the church lot at 11:30 even though there's no second shift on Saturday and I could go in anytime. I sit there anyway. I go in at midnight. I sleep on the floor. Around two I actually sleep, for a while, and dream about the fern.

Sunday morning at ten I'm sitting in her chair with the yard light off and the real light coming in gray through the glass corner, and I call Simone.

Not at one in the morning. Ten a.m. On a Sunday. I've never called Simone at ten a.m. on a Sunday in my life.

She picks up on the fourth ring.

"Adam."

"Is this a bad time?"

"I'm in a U-Haul parking lot on Twenty-Eighth Street," Simone says. "I'm pricing trucks. So yes. But go ahead."

"You're leaving?"

"I'm pricing trucks. Pricing is not leaving. Pricing is information." I can hear traffic behind her, and a man somewhere shouting about a hitch. "What do you want?"

I sit there with the phone. I planned this. I had the whole thing planned, the way I plan things, in order, with a slide. I had an opening and a close.

"I want to stop calling you at one in the morning," I say.

There's a silence.

"Okay," Simone says.

"I mean it. It's not—you didn't do anything. You picked up. You always picked up. I'm the one who called. For eight months I called you about the things I was afraid of because you were awake and you had the numbers and you didn't look at me any way at all. And I called it work. And it was work, some of it. But some of it was me not going upstairs." I look out the corner at the yard. "I should have been calling my wife. I should have been knocking on her door. I used you so I didn't have to, and I'm sorry. I'm stopping."

Another silence. Longer. The man shouting about the hitch has stopped.

"You're telling me this," Simone says slowly, "at ten in the morning on a Sunday."

"Yes."

"Before you stop. Not after."

"Yes."

"Huh," says Simone. It's the first time I've ever heard her make a sound that isn't a word. "Well. Thank you for telling me. I would have noticed eventually. Around one-fifteen."

I almost laugh. I don't.

"There's another thing," I say. "Kurt. He's telling people you leaked his model. He's poisoning you with Brightline and Haverhill, I know he is, I know those guys. I want to call them. Today. I want to tell them the truth. That you never gave Pip a single Halvard number, that you were the most professional person in the building, that Kurt's a sore—"

"Adam."

"What?"

"Do not decide that for me."

I stop.

"Ask me," Simone says.

I sit there in Pip's chair with my knees up. Out the glass corner a crow lands on the fence, looks at the room, and decides against it.

"Simone," I say. "Can I call Brightline and Haverhill and tell them the truth about you?"

"Yes," she says. "Thank you. Use the word *exemplary*. Dave Haverhill likes words like that. He thinks they're expensive." She pauses. "Where are you?"

"In the room."

"Which room?"

"Hers. In the yard."

"On a Sunday."

"I've been here since Friday."

Simone doesn't say anything for a long time. Behind her, a truck backs up, beeping.

"You should go home," she says finally.

"She asked me to go."

"Then you are home," Simone says. "You barely used it."

And she hangs up. She hangs up first, which she has never done, not once in eight months, and I sit there with the phone in my hand and the crow on the fence, and I laugh until my eyes hurt.

When I stop, I look at the inside of the door. The light's better now. The pencil on the raw birch, three lines, two hands, eleven years old.

*1. Nobody decides alone.*

I look at it a long time. I know what I'm going to do. I've known since about four this morning, on the floor, under Darnell's moving blanket. It's going to cost me everything I put on the table in October. It's the right thing, and I'm sure of it the way you're sure of a number you've checked three times.

But I won't do it yet.

First I'm going to tell her.
