## Chapter 12 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

At ten past eleven on Friday night somebody knocks on my glass with one knuckle, and I don't even have to look up.

"It's open," I say.

The door opens. The cold comes in, and then Adam does, in his long gray coat with the collar up and a Clyde Park Lanes ball cap on his head that I have never seen in my life.

I look at the hat. He looks at me looking at the hat.

"Lolo," he says.

"Lolo gave you a hat?"

"Lolo texted me at nine-thirty. She said *your wife's in the yard*. Then she said *wear a hat, it's eleven degrees*. Then she met me at the gate with this." He takes it off and looks at it. SPARE PARTS, it says on the back, iron-on letters, half gone. "I think I'm on the team now."

"You can't bowl."

"I bowled a one-forty at the Christmas party."

"In 2019. With bumpers."

"Nobody said bumpers were cheating."

"Everybody said bumpers were cheating, Adam. Gordon cried."

He laughs. Not the Rotary one. The real one, short, surprised out of him, the one I heard through the wall at one in the morning. He stands there inside the door on my mother's rug with the hat in his hand, laughing in my room, and something in my chest goes very still, the way a room goes still when the furnace shuts off and you realize how loud it was.

"Sit down," I say. "You're letting the heat out."

He looks at the chair by the radiator. The other chair. He looks at it a long time, like it's a dog that used to be his and might not remember him.

Then he sits down in it. He puts his feet up on the radiator, crossed at the ankle, in his good leather shoes with the salt lines on them, and leans back, and the chair makes the same small creak it's made since 2014, and the whole room settles a quarter of an inch, like it's been waiting for a weight.

Neither of us says anything for a while. Outside the glass the yard light makes a white circle on the snow and the maples along Clyde Park stand up black against an orange sky. Grand Rapids at night is always orange. Sodium lights and snow.

"It's warm in here," he says.

"I put in a better heater."

"When?"

"Tuesday. Darnell and me."

"You've been out here since Tuesday?"

"Most of the time. The plant's right there." I tip my head at the rolling door, which is down now, second shift gone home, the building dark except for the exit signs. "I can see the knee line from my desk. I like it."

"You can see the knee line from the mezzanine."

"The mezzanine smells like primer."

"So does this."

"That's my sweater," I say. "That's me."

He looks at me then. It's a long look. He used to look at me like that across rooms when we were raising the A, at pitch dinners, me in the one black dress I owned and him working the table, and he'd catch my eye over somebody's shoulder and hold it for a second too long, so I'd know the deal was done before the investor did.

I look away first. I pick up the graph paper. It's been sitting face down on my desk since Wednesday.

"Lolo told you to tell me something," he says.

"Lolo tells me a lot of things."

"She said you had a plan, and she said if you didn't show it to me she'd show me herself. She said she'd draw it on my car."

"She would. She's got markers."

"So show me."

I don't, for a second. I sit there holding the paper.

Then I turn it over and hand it across the desk.

He takes his feet down off the radiator and leans forward and puts the paper in the light from my desk lamp, and he reads it. Adam reads like a fast man. He reads the way he walks into rooms, like the page is already a little late for him. But he reads this slow. He reads Irene's numbers, which I copied onto the bottom in my own hand, and Darnell's paint booth math, and my arrows, and the box that says *CP 2ND SHIFT + SAT*, and the box that says *STEEL / GLASS UP FRONT — $?*, with three question marks, and a fourth one I added this afternoon after Ruth.

He turns it over to see if there's anything on the back. There isn't.

"Four thousand a year," he says.

"Four thousand one hundred. Irene says."

"And the deposits release at steel-cut."

"Rule two."

"So as you build, the escrow pays you." He's talking to himself now. I can see the deck building behind his eyes, the slides lining up. "Ten percent at cut, the balance at delivery. You're cash-flow positive per unit from the day the knee's welded. It's—Pip. It's clean. It's very clean."

"It's not clean. Look at the box."

"I see the box."

"Money before the money." I point at it. "Steel. Glass. Six weeks of it before the first escrow comes loose. Overtime. And the second shift's a hiring problem, I need thirty people who can weld the knee and there aren't thirty people in Michigan who can weld the knee. I'd have to train them. That's months."

"So you stagger it."

"So I stagger it, and I need money for the stagger."

He looks at the box with the question marks for a long time. His thumb goes to the edge of the paper and rubs it, back and forth, the way he did with the coffee lid that morning on four.

"How much?" he says.

"I don't know. Six. Eight. Depends on steel."

He doesn't say anything. His face does the thing. The door. I watch it close. Something goes across his eyes that's heavier than the arithmetic and he puts it away somewhere I can't follow, and I think, *ask him*. Ruth said ask him. I could ask him right now. *Is the company in trouble.* Four words. I've welded harder things than four words.

"You'd sell me my own room," he says instead.

It takes me a second.

Then I laugh. I don't mean to. It comes up out of me from somewhere under my ribs, from a long way down, a bark like his at the airport, and I put my hand over my mouth. Because I said that. I said that about him, to Kurt, standing right where he's sitting. *He'd sell you your own chair.* And he heard it. Of course he heard it. And he's been carrying it around all week, turning it over, and he's just handed it back to me in a room on a Friday at eleven-thirty at night with a bowling hat on his knee.

"That's mine," I say through my hand. "That's my line."

"It's a good line. I improved it."

"You didn't improve it, you *stole* it."

"I liberated it," he says, and that does it, I'm gone, I'm laughing so hard I have to put my head down on the desk on top of the graph paper, and he's laughing too, the real one, the dog-out-of-a-car one, and the radiator ticks, and the room holds both of us, ten feet by twelve, like it was built to.

When I lift my head he's closer. He's leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, the chair tipped up on its front legs, and his face is about a foot from mine across the corner of the desk. His eyes are red at the edges. Mine probably are too. We're both too old to laugh like that at midnight without paying for it.

He's looking at my cheekbone. I know what's there. I've been drawing all day.

"You've got graphite," he says.

"Leave it."

"I know." His voice is very low. "It's structural."

He doesn't reach for it. His hand is right there on his knee, a foot from my face, and it doesn't move, and I don't move, and the room is so quiet I can hear the yard light humming outside in the cold.

His phone buzzes.

It's in the breast pocket of his coat, against his chest. It buzzes once, twice. He doesn't move. On the third one he takes it out, slow, and the screen lights up his face from underneath, blue, and lights my desk, and lights the graph paper, and I can read it upside down because I've been reading things upside down across desks my whole life.

SIMONE.

It's one minute past midnight. Not one in the morning. Midnight. She's early tonight.

He looks at it. I watch him look at it. It buzzes a fourth time in his hand.

Then he presses the side button, and the screen goes black, and he puts the phone face down on my desk, on top of my graph paper, on top of the box with the four question marks.

He doesn't say anything about it. He doesn't say *strike that* or *it's work* or *sorry*. He just puts it face down and takes his hand away from it, like it's something hot he put down on a counter.

It's the nicest thing he's done in two years.

And it's gone anyway. The foot of air between us has closed up like water. We both know it. He sits back. The chair comes down on all four legs with its little creak. I sit back too. I look at the phone face down on my plan.

"You should go home," I say.

"Pip—"

"It's late. You've got Nashville Monday."

"How do you know I've got Nashville Monday?"

"I opened the Monday deck," I say.

He looks at me. Then he nods, slowly, and picks up the phone, and puts it back in his breast pocket without turning it over, and stands up and puts on the bowling hat, which looks ridiculous on him, which looks so ridiculous on him I have to look at the floor.

At the door he stops.

"It's a good plan," he says. "The money before the money. I'll think about it."

"Don't think about it alone."

He holds still for half a second, one hand on the brass pull my father turned. Then he goes out into the cold.

The chair by the radiator is still warm. I don't sit in it.
