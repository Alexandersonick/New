## Chapter 12 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

At ten past eleven on Friday night somebody knocks on my glass with one knuckle, and I don't even have to look up.

"It's open," I say.

The door opens, the cold comes in, and then Adam does, in his long gray coat with the collar up and a Clyde Park Lanes ball cap on his head that I have never seen in my life.

I stare at the hat. He stands there and lets me.

"Lolo," he says, as if that covers it.

"Lolo gave you a hat?"

"Lolo texted me tonight. She said *your wife's in the yard*. Then she said *wear a hat, it's eleven degrees*. Then she met me at the gate with this." He takes it off and turns it over in his hands. SPARE PARTS, it says on the back, in iron-on letters, half gone. "She also asked me my average. I think I'm on the team now."

"You can't bowl."

"I bowled a one-forty at the Christmas party."

"In 2019, with bumpers."

"Nobody said bumpers were cheating."

"Everybody said bumpers were cheating, Adam. Gordon cried."

He laughs the real laugh, short, surprised out of him, the one I used to hear through the wall, nothing like the one he does for investors. He stands there inside the door on my mother's rug with the hat in his hand, laughing in my room, and something in my chest goes very still, the way a room goes still when the furnace shuts off and you realize how loud it was.

"Sit down," I say. "You're letting the heat out."

He considers the other chair, the one by the radiator, for a long second, with his hat still in his hand.

Then he sits down in it. He puts his feet up on the radiator, crossed at the ankle, in his good leather shoes with the salt lines on them, and leans back, and the chair makes the same small creak it's made since 2014, and the whole room settles a quarter of an inch, like it's been waiting for a weight.

Neither of us talks for a while. Outside the glass the yard light makes a white circle on the snow, and the maples along Clyde Park stand up black against an orange sky. Grand Rapids at night is always orange, sodium lights and snow.

"It's warm in here," he says, and undoes the top button of his coat but leaves it on.

"I put in a better heater."

"When?"

"Tuesday, with Darnell, before his wife started having false alarms."

The heater kicks on under the desk with a thunk, and my desk lamp dims to brown and comes back. Adam tips his head at the lamp.

"Your heater's fighting your lamp," he says.

"The heater's winning. Everything in here's on one circuit. Darnell says it's fine."

"Darnell's at the hospital."

"Darnell says it's fine from the hospital," I say. "There's coffee, if you want it. It's from the break room and it's been on the burner since four."

He wants it. I pour it into the Spare Parts mug Lolo left on my shelf, and he holds it in both hands and drinks, and makes a face like a man taking a punch he paid for.

"This is terrible."

"Second shift likes it that way. It keeps them up."

"Lolo wants me Monday night." He turns the hat around on his knee so the iron-on letters face me. "Darnell's wife is due any minute. I told her I'm busy Monday, and she said that's what they all say."

"That is what they all say. She'll find somebody."

"She said if she has to she'll take the intern."

"You've been out here since Tuesday?"

"Most of the time. The plant's right there." I tip my head at the rolling door, which is down now, second shift gone home, the building dark except for the exit signs. "I can see the knee line from my desk. I like it."

"You can see the knee line from the mezzanine."

"The mezzanine smells like primer."

"So does this room, Pip."

"That's my sweater," I say, "and that's me."

He gives me a long look over the mug. He used to look at me like that across rooms when we were raising the A, at pitch dinners, me in the one black dress I owned and him working the table, and he'd catch my eye over somebody's shoulder and hold it a second too long, so I'd know the deal was done before the investor did.

I look away first and pick up the graph paper. It's been lying upside down on my desk since Wednesday.

"Lolo told you to tell me something," he says.

"Lolo tells me a lot of things."

"She said you had a plan, and she said if you didn't show it to me she'd show me herself. She said she'd draw it on my car."

"She would, too, she's got a whole box of markers."

"So show me, then." He sets the mug on the radiator and holds his hand out.

I don't, for a second. I sit there holding the paper against my chest.

Then I turn it over and hand it across the desk.

He takes his feet down off the radiator and leans forward and puts the paper in the light from my desk lamp, and he reads it. Adam usually reads the way he walks into rooms, like the page is already a little late for him, but he reads this slow. He reads Irene's numbers, which I copied onto the bottom in my own hand, and Darnell's paint booth math, and my arrows, and the box that says *CP 2ND SHIFT + SAT*, and the box that says *STEEL / GLASS UP FRONT: $?* with three question marks, and a fourth one I added on Wednesday after Ruth.

He turns it over to see if there's anything on the back, and there isn't.

"Four thousand a year," he says.

"Four thousand one hundred, Irene says, and Irene's never wrong about glass."

"Who else has seen this?"

"Lolo and Irene, and Benny, and the vending machine in the break room, which hasn't weighed in."

He keeps his eyes on the paper. "So the crew saw it before I did."

"The crew did half the math, Adam."

For a second he doesn't move. Then he nods at the paper, once. "Okay," he says to it. "That's fair. What does Simone say?"

"Simone hasn't seen it."

"Simone's going to want a model."

"Simone can have a model when there's money for one," I say. "Keep reading."

He reads, and stops at the margin. "You put Benny's raise in the plan."

"Benny put Benny's raise in the plan. That's his handwriting, not mine."

He reads on, slower. "And the deposits release at steel-cut."

"That's rule two, on the door since 2014."

"So as you build, the escrow pays you." He's talking to himself now. I can see the deck building behind his eyes, the slides lining up. "Ten percent at cut, the balance at delivery. You're cash-flow positive per unit from the day the knee's welded. It's—Pip, it's clean, it's very clean."

"It isn't clean," I say. "Look at the box."

"I see the box. Three of the question marks are pencil and one's pen."

"The pen one's from Wednesday. Money before the money." I put my finger on it. "Steel and glass, six weeks of it, before the first escrow comes loose. Overtime. And the second shift's a hiring problem. I need thirty people who can weld the knee, and there aren't thirty people in Michigan who can weld the knee, so I'd have to train them, and that's months."

"So you stagger it."

"So I stagger it, and I need money for the stagger."

"If I were pitching this," he says, "I'd lead with the three thousand orders."

"You're not pitching it."

"If I were. You lead with the demand, and then you show them the plant can meet it."

"Lead with the knee," I say. "The knee is why there's demand."

"Nobody buys a knee, Pip."

"Three thousand people just did. They just don't know what it's called."

He stays on the box with the question marks. His thumb goes to the edge of the paper and rubs it, back and forth, the way he did with the coffee lid that morning on four.

"How much?" he says.

"I don't know yet. Six million, maybe eight, depending on steel."

His face does the thing, the door, and I watch it close. Something goes across his eyes that's heavier than the arithmetic, and he puts it away somewhere I can't follow, and I think, *ask him*. Ruth said ask him. I could ask him right now. *Are we in trouble.* Four words. I've welded harder things than four words.

"You'd sell me my own room," he says instead.

It takes me a second, and then I laugh. I don't mean to. It starts somewhere under my ribs, a long way down, a bark like his at the airport, and I put my hand over my mouth. I said that. I said it about him, to Kurt, standing right where he's sitting: *He'd sell you your own chair.* He heard it, of course he heard it, and he's been carrying it around all week, turning it over, and now he's handed it back to me in a room on a Friday near midnight with a bowling hat on his knee.

"That's mine," I say through my hand. "That's my line."

"It's a good line. I improved it."

"You didn't improve it, you *stole* it."

"I liberated it," he says, and that does it, I'm gone, I'm laughing so hard I have to put my head down on the desk on top of the graph paper, and he's laughing too, the real one, the dog-out-of-a-car one, and the radiator ticks, and the room holds both of us, ten feet by twelve, like it was built to.

When I lift my head he's closer. He's leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, the chair tipped up on its front legs, and his face is about a foot from mine across the corner of the desk. His eyes are red at the edges. Mine probably are too. We're both too old to laugh like that at midnight without paying for it.

He's looking at my cheekbone, and I know what's there, because I've been drawing all day.

"You've got graphite on your face," he says.

"Leave it."

"I know," he says, very low. "It's structural."

He doesn't reach for it. His hand is right there on his knee, a foot from my face, and it doesn't move, and I don't move, and the room is so quiet I can hear the yard light humming outside in the cold.

His phone buzzes in the breast pocket of his coat, against his chest. It buzzes once and then again, and he doesn't move. On the third one he takes it out, slow, and the screen lights up his face from underneath, blue, and lights my desk and the graph paper, and I can read it upside down because I've been reading things upside down across desks my whole life.

SIMONE.

It's a minute past midnight. She's early tonight.

I watch him look at it while it buzzes a fourth time in his hand.

Then he presses the side button, and the screen goes black, and he puts the phone face down on my desk, on top of my graph paper, on top of the box with the four question marks.

He doesn't say *strike that* or *it's work* or *sorry*. He just takes his hand away from it, like it's something hot he put down on a counter.

It's the nicest thing he's done since Nashville.

It's gone anyway, the foot of air between us closed up like water, and we both know it. He sits back, and the chair comes down on all four legs with its little creak. I sit back too and look at his phone lying dark on my plan.

"You should go home," I say.

"Pip—"

"It's late. You've got Nashville Monday."

"How do you know I've got Nashville Monday?"

"I opened the Monday deck," I say.

He holds my eyes for a second. Then he nods, slowly, and picks up the phone, and puts it back in his breast pocket without turning it over, and stands up and puts on the bowling hat, which looks ridiculous on him, so ridiculous I have to look at the floor.

"Wear it in Nashville," I say to the floor.

"Odell will think I've been demoted."

"Tell him it's a promotion. Lolo doesn't give out hats to just anybody."

At the door he stops.

"It's a good plan," he says. "The money before the money. I'll think about it."

"Don't think about it alone."

He holds still for half a second, one hand on the brass pull my father turned. Then he goes out into the cold.

The chair by the radiator is still warm. I don't sit in it.
