## Chapter 9 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Simone Achterberg arrives at the plant on Monday at eight-fifteen in a gray Volvo with a ski rack and no skis, parks it between the lines, straight, and walks across the yard in a pair of lined rubber boots that are exactly right for the yard. I'll give her the boots. Nobody from downtown ever wears the right boots out here. They come out in loafers and stand in the slush with their faces getting longer and longer, like men being slowly lowered into a lake.

I'm at the test rig on the far side of the yard, outside the rolling door, with Irene and Darnell. The rig is a steel frame the size of a garage door, painted yellow, with a hydraulic ram on one side and a big analog gauge on a stand. Bolted into it is a corner. Not mine. A Lite corner, two short sections of wall meeting at a square steel post, four inches by four, painted the oatmeal color that came down from the integration team.

Darnell's working the pump. Irene's got the clipboard. I'm standing with my arms crossed, watching the post, because I can't help it, the way you can't help watching somebody chew with their mouth open.

Simone walks up to the gauge. She doesn't say good morning. She takes her reading glasses off her head, puts them on, leans in close to the dial, and taps the glass with one fingernail.

"Your zero's drifted," she says. "You're reading high. About two percent."

Darnell stops pumping. Irene looks at the gauge, then at Simone, then at me.

"How do you know?" Darnell asks.

"The needle's resting a hair past the pin. You've got no load on it yet." She steps back. "It will make the Lite look stiffer than it is. Which I would prefer it not do, because I would like to know how stiff it is."

I look at the gauge. She's right. I hate that she's right. It's the kind of thing I'd catch if I weren't busy watching a post.

"Darnell," I say. "Re-zero it."

He does. The needle drops back onto the pin with a tiny click, like it's embarrassed.

Simone turns to me. "Good morning, Pip."

"You drove out here to fix my gauge?"

"I drove out here to ask you to sign off on the Lite test protocol. The gauge was a bonus." She has a folder under her arm. Of course she has a folder. "I have the protocol, the load schedule, and the acceptance criteria. Irene has already reviewed them. Irene has notes."

I look at Irene. Irene shrugs one shoulder, which means *she's not wrong about the notes*.

"Inside," I say. "It's cold."

We go into the room. Simone wipes her boots on my mother's bread-bag rug, both feet, twice, which my mother would like. She stops just inside and looks around the way people look around a church in a town they're passing through, polite, a little curious about what everybody's praying for. Her eyes go to the desk. To the glass corner. To the chair by the radiator. Then to the inside of the door.

She reads it. I watch her read it. She reads it the way she reads everything, all the way through, and then she reads it again, slower.

"Rule two," she says.

"What about it?"

"*Never spend a customer's money before you've cut their steel.*" She looks at me. "Gordon showed me the escrow balance on Friday. It's nine point two million dollars. It's the most money this company has had in one place since the Series D, and nobody can touch it."

"That's the idea."

"It is the most expensive sentence in this company."

"It's the cheapest one," I say. "It just bills us at the wrong time."

Something happens at the corner of her mouth. I don't know her well enough to read it. It might be a smile. It might be a woman doing the arithmetic on a sentence.

I take the folder and sit down at the desk. She doesn't sit in the other chair. She stays standing by the door with her hands folded in front of her, like a witness who's been told she can sit and has decided not to.

The protocol is good. It's very good. Load schedules, deflection limits, cycle counts, and a section on how the posted corner behaves under snow load that's honestly better than the one I wrote for the original in 2014. Irene's notes are in red pen down the margins, in her square capitals: *GOOD. GOOD. NO — RACKING NEEDS 3 CYCLES NOT 2. GOOD.* At the bottom of the last page there's a line for my signature, under the words *Product architecture approval (Founder, Design)*.

"No," I say.

"You have not read page six."

"I don't need to read page six. It's a good test. It's a better test than mine. I'm not signing it."

"Why?"

"Because you'll use it to build a room with a post in it."

"That is what the Lite is."

"I know what it is."

"Pip." She says it carefully, the way you'd say the name of a dog you're not sure about. "I have spent four months trying to take the post out of the Lite. Four months. I had two consultants. I had a structural engineer from Purdue. I had Darnell on weekends." She nods out the window, toward the rig, where Darnell is now pumping again and watching the needle like it owes him money. "I cannot do it at that price. Nobody can. The only way to get a corner with no post is your knee, and your knee is eleven hours of welding by four of the best people in Michigan, and Halvard's cost model allows four."

"So don't sell it to Halvard."

"That is not my decision."

"It's not *mine* either, apparently. It's on a slide."

She doesn't flinch. I'll give her that too. Most people flinch when I do that voice. Even Adam flinches.

"Most customers will not notice the post," she says.

"Two thousand four hundred people noticed there isn't one."

"Two thousand six hundred and twelve, as of this morning." She glances at her watch, which is an actual watch, with hands. "Tavi messages me the number. I did not ask him to. He seems to need someone to tell."

I put the folder down on the desk. I put my hand flat on it. I look out at the yard through the glass corner, past the place where the post would be, at the fence and the snow and the line of maples along Clyde Park that somebody planted in the seventies and then forgot about, so they grew up crooked and huge and full of crows.

"You know why the corner has no post?" I say.

"Load path. The moment frame—"

"No. I mean why."

She waits. She's good at waiting. That's two things I'll give her now, and I'm starting to resent the count.

"Because when you sit in a small room," I say, "the corner's where your eye goes. Everybody. Kids, old people, everybody. You sit down in a little room, you look out the corner. It's the farthest point. It's where the room opens. And if there's a post there, then every time you look out, the first thing you see is a post." I tap the folder. "People buy these to get out of their houses. Their marriages. Their kids. They go out to the backyard and sit in a little room so they can look at something that isn't the thing they're stuck in. I wasn't going to make them look at a post."

Simone's quiet for a long time. Out in the yard, the ram hisses, and the needle climbs, and Darnell calls out a number to Irene.

"I did not know that," she says finally.

"It's not in the protocol."

"It should be." She holds her hand out for the folder. I give it back to her. She opens it to page one and takes a pen out of her coat, a black one, fine point, and writes something across the top in her small, level handwriting. I lean over to see.

*Design intent: the eye goes to the corner.*

"I'm still not signing it," I say.

"I did not expect you to." She closes the folder. "I expected to learn why. I have learned why."

That's not what I thought she'd say. I've had Simone Achterberg in my head for a week as a woman in a navy sweater putting a ficus on my desk, and I didn't make room for a woman who'd drive out to Clyde Park in the right boots to learn why.

She doesn't leave. She stands there with the folder against her chest, and I can see her deciding something.

"Lolo told me you were married in this room," she says.

"Lolo talks."

"Lolo was standing at the gate when I parked. She asked me if I was the one with the ficus. I said yes. She said, and I am quoting, *You know they got married in that thing, hon? In her daddy's garage. Ten minutes. I made the pierogi.*" Simone's voice doesn't change at all doing Lolo, which somehow makes it funnier. "I would like you to know that if I had known, I would not have asked for it."

"You'd have asked for something else."

"I would have asked for a door that closes. That is what I wanted." She looks at the door, the real one, the birch one with the writing on the inside. "I was taking calls in the stairwell. It smells like pigeons."

"It does."

"And radiator paint."

"Since 1922."

We look at each other. It's strange. It's like looking at somebody across a fence you put up yourself.

"Can I ask you something?" I say.

"Yes."

"The calls. At one in the morning."

I watch it land. I watch her decide whether to pretend she doesn't know what I mean, and decide not to. Her hands tighten on the folder, just a little, the knuckles going pale along the edge.

"He cannot sleep," she says. "I cannot sleep. My daughter is in East Lansing and my ex-husband is in Phoenix with a dental hygienist named Courtney, and I live in a rented condo on Ionia Avenue with eleven boxes I have not unpacked since May. At one in the morning he calls and we go over the numbers. Then we talk about other things. He tells me stories. He's very funny at one in the morning."

"I know."

"I know you know." She says it gently. "I have not touched him, and he has not touched me. I want that to be clear, because you are entitled to it. What else I feel at one in the morning, I have not decided is any of your business." She turns the folder a quarter turn against her chest. "He is the only person awake."

"He's not the only person awake," I say.

It comes out of me before I can stop it. It comes out in my mother's voice. Simone hears it. She looks at me for a long moment, and something in her face shifts, like a measurement being taken again with a better instrument.

"No," she says. "I suppose he isn't."

Outside, Darnell yells something, and Irene yells something back, and the ram goes quiet. Simone turns to look through the glass. The needle on the gauge has stopped. The oatmeal-colored post is standing there in the yellow frame, holding its corner up, steady as a fence post, doing its job. It passes. I can tell from Darnell's face that it passes.

"It's a good post," I say. "I want you to know that. It's not a bad post. It's just a post."

Simone puts the folder under her arm and opens the door. The cold comes in. She stops on the step, half in and half out, and looks back at the gauge across the yard.

"Have them re-zero it after every run," she says. "Your zero's going to keep drifting. It's the cold."
