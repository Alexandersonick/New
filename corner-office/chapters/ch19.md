## Chapter 19 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

My wife and my work wife have taken over the transparency room, and everybody on the fourth floor can watch them do it.

It's the following Wednesday, a week and a day since the board. I'm standing by the coffee station at the end of the design studio with a cup of the office drip, which is worse than gas-station coffee and slightly better than Pip's, watching them through the glass. Pip's at the long white table in her plant jacket with her sleeves pushed up and a mechanical pencil behind her ear. Simone's across from her in a navy sweater with her calculator and its paper tape curling off the edge of the table onto the floor, a long white tongue of it, a week's worth, that nobody's allowed to throw away. Between them there's a whiteboard on an easel with Irene's numbers from the break room copied onto it in Pip's handwriting, and under that a column in Simone's, and they've been there since seven, and every so often one of them says something and the other one writes it down.

The whole floor watches them, sales included. Marketing has stopped pretending to watch their monitors and has turned their chairs. Bridget from accounts payable comes up from three twice a day with a question about invoices she could ask by email, just to walk past the glass.

"It's like a nature documentary," says Tavi, who's appeared at my elbow with a laptop under his arm. He's in his Carhartt indoors now and doesn't take it off anymore. "Two apex predators sharing a kill."

"That's my wife you're talking about, Tavi."

"I know, I meant it nicely." He gives me a sideways look. "Seven thousand two hundred and eight."

"I know, you text me every morning."

"I text everybody, it's the only number I've ever been in charge of." He shifts the laptop to his other arm. "Are you going in there?"

"No."

"You could bring them coffee," Tavi says. "That's the classic way in."

"They have coffee."

"They have Simone's coffee. I've had it. It's hot water that's heard about coffee." He waits. "So why not?"

Because nobody asked me in. Since the ramp I've been telling her things before I act on them, eight days of it, small things, a call from Kurt, a question from Gordon, an email from the guy at Northgate, texted to her the minute they happen like a teenager, and she's been answering *ok* and *thanks* and once, near midnight, *go to sleep*. She hasn't asked me into the glass room, though, and at forty I'm finding out that you don't walk into a room just because you can see through the walls.

"Because it's their meeting," I say.

Tavi gives me the face of a twenty-three-year-old who has just watched an adult say something unexpected.

"Huh," he says, and goes away.

The thing nobody tells you about getting what you want is that it moves the fear to a new place in your chest.

Since Nashville the fear has lived in one place, payroll, Nashville, the number at the bottom of Gordon's report on Wednesdays, and I could put my hand on it like a sore tooth. Then last Thursday, on I-94 coming home from Monroe in the snow, with my wife driving like a nun and Gil Chamberlain's net sixty in her coat pocket, I looked over at her and she was humming. Pip doesn't hum, ever, but she hummed for twenty miles, under her breath, something Polish her mother used to sing, and I sat there in the passenger seat and something turned over under my ribs that scared me worse than payroll.

Because now there's something to lose that isn't money.

Late in the morning, Kurt Lindahl calls my cell.

"Adam! How's my favorite seller?"

"Kurt," I say, and wait.

"I'm hearing wonderful things, just wonderful. Supplier terms, net sixty from your steel mill, I hear. Very clever, very scrappy." He says *scrappy* like a man admiring a child's drawing. "I hear your wife and our integration lead are working very closely together."

Through the glass, Simone's writing something and Pip's laughing at it, actually laughing, with her head back.

"Who'd you hear that from?"

"Oh, furniture's a small world," he says, laughing. "Listen, I just want to say, as a friend: I think it's admirable. I think it's a great story. And I think you should remember that supplier terms don't move a covenant date. Halvard can be patient, and Northgate can't, and you know that."

"I know what Northgate can do."

"I'm only saying it because I care about the outcome, for everybody. And give Simone my best, would you? I'll see her on Thursday's call. I always look forward to Thursday."

"I'll pass that on," I say, and he laughs again like I've told a joke, and hangs up.

I take out my phone and text Pip right there by the coffee station: *Kurt called, knows about Gil's terms, didn't say how, says Northgate can't wait. Sent Simone his best and I didn't like the way he said it.* Through the glass, I watch her pick up her phone off the white table and read it, and frown, and look up, and find me at the coffee station, and hold my eyes for one second across the floor.

Then she nods, once, and goes back to the whiteboard.

That's the whole conversation, and it's the best one I've had at work since Nashville.

Near the end of the day, Neil Pruitt from Northgate calls.

I take it in the stairwell, the iron one that smells like pigeons. Neil is a short, pleasant man from Naperville with a voice like a fax machine. I met him once, in October, in an office on Wacker Drive with a view of the river where they dye it green on St. Patrick's Day. He told me about his kids' hockey. He had me initial forty-one pages, and I took the last one home and signed it at the kitchen counter at two in the morning.

"Adam, it's Neil, just a courtesy call."

Nobody from a lender has ever called anyone in history out of courtesy.

"Go ahead, Neil," I say.

"We've been reviewing the facility in light of the, ah, the developments, the media coverage, the board minutes, which Gordon was kind enough to send, the founder situation. Our credit committee met Monday. They've asked me to let you know we'll be requiring weekly cash reporting going forward, rather than monthly, under section eleven. And they've asked me to note that the covenant is a floor, not a date. If the company's cash falls below six on any reporting date, not just the test date, we have the right to call."

I sit down on the iron step.

"That's not how I read section eleven," I say.

"I understand, but our counsel reads it differently. You're welcome to have your counsel call our counsel." There's a pause, and some paper moves on his end. "Adam, I'll be honest with you, because I like you. If this company's cash dips under the floor, I don't think my committee waits. I think they call. And I think the shares are worth more to us in a sale than in a fight." He lets that sit. "I'm told Halvard is a very motivated buyer."

The stairwell is very quiet, and somewhere below me, on three, a door opens and closes.

"Thanks for the courtesy, Neil."

"Anytime, and say hi to your wife. We all saw the video, and my daughter wants one."

I sit on the iron step after he hangs up until the motion light over the landing decides I've died and goes out.

Here's the old me: he makes a plan. He calls Diane and gets her on with Northgate's counsel and fights section eleven for two weeks, and calls Kurt and finds out exactly how motivated, and calls Simone after midnight and runs the numbers, and by the time anybody else finds out, he's already decided what to do and has a slide for it. Strike that. He has three slides, and a backup deck.

I wave the light back on and get up off the step and go back up to four. I walk across the floor past sales and marketing and the design studio, past the coffee station and the cone somebody still keeps at the bolt plates in the northwest corner, and I stop outside the glass door of the transparency room, and I knock.

Pip and Simone look up together, like a jury.

Pip studies me through the glass for a second, then gets up and opens the door.

"Can I come in?" I say.

"Yes."

"I have a bad part."

She steps back to let me in.

I tell it badly, in the wrong order, with the worst part in the middle, standing at the end of the long white table with my hands in my pockets because I don't know what else to do with them: weekly reporting, section eleven, *a floor, not a date*, *I think they call*, *the shares are worth more to us in a sale*. I forget Neil's last name halfway through and have to start the sentence over. I say *um*, which I never say, more than once.

Simone writes it all down. Pip doesn't write anything; she stands by the whiteboard with the pencil behind her ear and her arms crossed, and listens with her eyes on my face.

When I'm done, the only sound in the room is the calculator.

"So it's not thirty days," Pip says.

"It's however many days until the cash dips under six."

"What does the tape say, Simone?"

Simone's already running it. "At current burn, with the new supplier terms and nothing else changing, the balance crosses six million in—" She runs it again. "Twenty-three days. Possibly twenty-one, depending on when the Nashville payroll clears."

Pip turns to the whiteboard, to Irene's numbers and Simone's column and the box at the bottom of Simone's column that says *NASH*, with a single neat line through it and a question mark. She stands looking at that box long enough for Simone to take her finger off the calculator, and then she turns back to me.

"Thank you for telling me first," she says.

"I'm bad at it, though."

"You're terrible at it," Pip says. "You said um four times and you forgot the man's name." She uncrosses her arms and holds out a green marker. "Keep doing it. And put it on the board, in your handwriting, under Simone's."

I take the marker and write *NORTHGATE: FLOOR 6, WEEKLY* under Simone's column, and my capitals come out leaning downhill like they're trying to leave the room.

"Your handwriting is terrible," Simone says.

"I'm in sales. We type."

"Every call," Pip says. "Every time one of them calls you, it goes up here with everything else."

Simone hooks the third chair out from under the table with her foot, without looking up from the tape. "Sit down, Adam. Somebody needs to call Grand Rapids Glass about delivery dates and be charming, and neither of us is willing."

I sit down in the third chair and make the call, with the whole fourth floor watching me through the glass.

The dispatcher at Grand Rapids Glass says the earliest she can cut tempered panels in that size is three weeks.

"She needs them in two," I say.

"Everybody needs them in two."

I tell her the worst of it up front, that we're short on cash and long on orders, and that Holland already matched her terms and would love to go first on the glass too. There's a pause on the line.

"Holland," she says, the way you'd name a rash. "I can do two and a half."

Pip, without turning around from the whiteboard, holds up two fingers over her shoulder, like somebody bidding at a church auction.

"Two," I say.

"Fine, two. Tell her she owes me a softball game."

That night I don't go upstairs to the room across the hall.

I stand on the landing at the top of the good stairs, with the ninth step creaking behind me, and look at two doors, mine open, hers shut with light under it.

I've never knocked on that door since Nashville. I've stood here a hundred times in the dark with my hand up and then turned around. I knock now, once, with one knuckle, like I'm at a drive-through.

The light under the door moves, and the door opens. She's in my old Grand Valley sweatshirt and her wool socks, with her hair down and her reading glasses on, holding a pad of graph paper.

"I don't have a bad part," I say. "I just wanted to knock."

She considers me over her reading glasses, holding the graph paper against her chest.

"Come in if you want to," she says.

"I want to."

"Then ask me the whole way through."

"Pip," I say, "can I come in?"

She sets the graph paper on the dresser by the door, face down.

"Yes," she says, and takes my hand, and pulls me in, and closes the door behind us.

Later, much later, lying on my back in her bed with her asleep against my shoulder and her hair in my mouth, I look up at her ceiling in the gray light from the window. There's a water stain up there, over the bed, shaped a bit like Lake Michigan.

Two years across the hall. I never knew she had a lake.
