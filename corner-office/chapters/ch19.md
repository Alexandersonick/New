## Chapter 19 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

My wife and my work wife have taken over the transparency room, and everybody on the fourth floor can watch them do it.

It's the following Wednesday, a week and a day since the board. I'm standing by the coffee station at the end of the design studio with a cup of the office drip, which is worse than gas-station coffee and slightly better than Pip's, watching them through the glass. Pip's at the long white table in her plant jacket with her sleeves pushed up and a mechanical pencil behind her ear. Simone's across from her in a navy sweater with her calculator and its paper tape curling off the edge of the table onto the floor, a long white tongue of it, a week's worth, that nobody's allowed to throw away. Between them there's a whiteboard on an easel with Irene's numbers from the break room copied onto it in Pip's handwriting, and under that a column in Simone's, and they've been there since seven, and every so often one of them says something and the other one writes it down.

The whole floor watches them. Sales watches them. Marketing has stopped pretending to watch their monitors and has turned its chairs. Bridget from accounts payable comes up from three twice a day with a question about invoices she could ask by email, just to walk past the glass.

"It's like a nature documentary," says Tavi, who's appeared at my elbow with a laptop under his arm. He's in his Carhartt indoors now. He doesn't take it off anymore. "Two apex predators sharing a kill."

"That's my wife, Tavi."

"I know. I meant it nicely." He looks at me sideways. "Seven thousand two hundred and eight."

"I know. You text me."

"I text everybody. It's the only number I've ever been in charge of." He shifts the laptop. "Are you going in?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Because I wasn't asked. Because Pip said *before, not after*, and for nine days I've been telling her things before, small things, a call from Kurt, a question from Gordon, an email from the guy at Northgate, I've been texting them to her the minute they happen like a teenager, and she's been answering *ok* and *thanks* and once, at eleven at night, *go to sleep*. But she hasn't asked me into the glass room. And I'm learning, very slowly, at forty, that you don't walk into a room just because you can see through the walls.

"Because it's their meeting," I say.

Tavi looks at me for a long time with the face of a twenty-three-year-old who has just watched an adult say something unexpected.

"Huh," he says, and goes away.

The thing nobody tells you about getting what you want is that it makes you afraid in a new place.

For two years I've been afraid in one place. Payroll. Nashville. The number at the bottom of Gordon's report on Wednesdays. I knew where the fear was. I could put my hand on it, like a sore tooth. And then last Thursday, on I-94 coming home from Monroe in the snow, with my wife driving like a nun and Rob Chamberlain's net sixty in her coat pocket, I looked over at her and she was humming. Pip doesn't hum. She hummed for twenty miles, under her breath, something Polish her mother used to sing, and I sat there in the passenger seat and felt something I haven't felt in a long time, and it scared me worse than payroll.

Because now there's something to lose that isn't money.

At ten-forty, Kurt Lindahl calls my cell.

"Adam! How's my favorite seller?"

"Kurt."

"I'm hearing wonderful things. Wonderful. Supplier terms. Net sixty from your steel mill, I hear. Very clever. Very scrappy." He says *scrappy* like a man admiring a child's drawing. "I hear your wife and our integration lead are working very closely together."

I look through the glass. Simone's writing something. Pip's laughing at it. Pip's actually laughing at something Simone wrote, with her head back.

"Who'd you hear that from?"

"Oh, it's a small world. Furniture's a small world." He laughs. "Listen, I just want to say, as a friend: I think it's admirable. I think it's a great story. And I think you should remember that supplier terms don't move a covenant date. Halvard can be patient. Northgate can't. You know that."

"I know that."

"I'm only saying it because I care about the outcome. For everybody."

"I'll pass that on," I say, and he laughs again like I've told a joke, and hangs up.

I stand there. I take out my phone and text Pip: *Kurt called. Knows about Rob's terms. Didn't say how. Said "Northgate can't wait."* I send it. Through the glass, I watch her pick up her phone off the white table and read it, and frown, and look up, and find me at the coffee station, and hold my eyes for one second across the floor.

Then she nods, once, and goes back to the whiteboard.

That's it. That's the whole conversation. It's the best conversation I've had at work in two years.

At four-fifteen Dennis Pruitt from Northgate calls.

I take it in the stairwell, the iron one that smells like pigeons. Dennis is a short, pleasant man from Naperville with a voice like a fax machine. I met him once, in October, in an office on Wacker Drive with a view of the river where they dye it green on St. Patrick's Day. He told me about his kids' hockey. He had me sign forty-one pages.

"Adam. Dennis. Just a courtesy call."

Nobody from a lender has ever called anyone in history out of courtesy.

"Go ahead, Dennis."

"We've been reviewing the facility in light of the, ah, the developments. The media coverage. The board minutes, which Gordon was kind enough to send. The founder situation." Fax machine. "Our credit committee met Monday. They've asked me to let you know we'll be requiring weekly cash reporting going forward, rather than monthly. Under section eleven. And they've asked me to note that the covenant is a floor, not a date. If the company's cash falls below six on any reporting date, not just the test date, we have the right to call."

I sit down on the iron step.

"That's not how I read section eleven," I say.

"I understand. Our counsel reads it differently. You're welcome to have your counsel call our counsel." A pause. "Adam, I'll be honest with you, because I like you. If this company's cash dips under the floor, I don't think my committee waits. I think they call. And I think the shares are worth more to us in a sale than in a fight." Another pause. "I'm told Halvard is a very motivated buyer."

The stairwell is very quiet. Somewhere below me, on three, a door opens and closes.

"Thanks for the courtesy, Dennis."

"Anytime. Say hi to your wife. We all saw the video. My daughter wants one."

I sit on the iron step for a long time after he hangs up.

Here's the old me. The old me makes a plan. The old me calls Diane and gets her on with Northgate's counsel and fights section eleven for two weeks, and calls Kurt and finds out exactly how motivated, and calls Simone at one in the morning and runs the numbers, and by the time anybody else finds out, the old me has already decided what to do and has a slide for it.

I get up off the step. I go back up to four. I walk across the floor past sales and marketing and the design studio, past the coffee station and the cone that somebody still keeps at the bolt plates in the northwest corner, and I stop outside the glass door of the transparency room, and I knock.

Pip looks up. Simone looks up.

Pip looks at me for a moment through the glass. Then she gets up and opens the door.

"Can I come in?" I say.

"Yes."

"I have a bad part."

She steps back.

I tell it badly. I tell it in the wrong order, with the worst part in the middle, standing at the end of the long white table with my hands in my pockets because I don't know what else to do with them. Weekly reporting. Section eleven. *A floor, not a date.* *I think they call.* *The shares are worth more to us in a sale.* I forget Dennis's last name halfway through and have to start the sentence over. I say *um*. I don't say *um*. I say it four times.

Simone writes it all down. Pip doesn't write anything. She stands by the whiteboard with the pencil behind her ear and listens, with her arms crossed, looking at my face.

When I'm done there's a silence.

"So it's not thirty days," Pip says.

"It's however many days until the cash dips under six."

"Simone?"

Simone's already running the tape. "At current burn, with the new supplier terms and nothing else changing, the balance crosses six million in—" She runs it again. "Twenty-three days. Possibly twenty-one, depending on when the Nashville payroll clears."

Pip turns and looks at the whiteboard. At Irene's numbers. At Simone's column. At a box at the bottom of Simone's column that says *NASH*, with a single neat line through it and a question mark.

She looks at that box for a long time.

Then she turns back to me.

"Thank you for telling me," she says. "Before."

"I'm bad at it."

"You're terrible at it," Pip says. "You said um four times. You forgot the man's name." She uncrosses her arms. "Keep doing it."

That night I don't go upstairs to the room across the hall.

I stand on the landing at the top of the good stairs, with the ninth step creaking behind me, and look at two doors. Mine is open. Hers is shut, with light under it.

I knock.

I've never knocked on that door. Not in two years. I've stood here a hundred times in the dark with my hand up and then turned around. I knock now, once, with one knuckle, like I'm at a drive-through.

The light under the door moves. The door opens. She's in my old Grand Valley sweatshirt and her wool socks, with her hair down and her reading glasses on, holding a pad of graph paper.

"Hi," I say.

"Hi."

"I don't have a bad part," I say. "I just wanted to knock."

She looks at me for a long time over her reading glasses.

"Come in," she says. "If you want to."

"I want to."

"Then say it like a person."

"Pip," I say. "Can I come in?"

She takes her glasses off. She puts them on the dresser by the door. She puts the graph paper on top of them, face down.

"Yes," she says, and takes my hand, and pulls me in, and closes the door behind us.

Later, much later, lying on my back in her bed with her asleep against my shoulder and her hair in my mouth, I look up at her ceiling in the gray light from the window. There's a water stain up there, over the bed, shaped a bit like Lake Michigan.

Two years across the hall. I never knew she had a lake.
