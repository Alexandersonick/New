## Chapter 24 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Monday night a little after ten, sitting in Pip's chair in the room in the yard with the heater ticking, I write her a text and don't send it for forty minutes.

I keep trying to make it better. That's the problem. I keep trying to make it a pitch. I write it with a reason in front, *because I love you*, which is true, and delete it, because it sounds like a man putting a bow on something he's about to hand over. I write it with an apology in front and delete that too. I write it with a list. I write it as a question, *would it be okay if*, and that one I stare at the longest, because it's closest. But I've already decided, and if I phrase it as a question I'm pretending she gets a vote in something I've already done in my head, and she'll know, because she always knows.

At 10:52 I send the plain one.

*Tomorrow I'm driving to Chicago to see Northgate. I'm going to tell them the cash crisis was mine, Nashville, all of it. I'm going to sign a personal guarantee on top of the pledge, so if the company misses the test, my shares go first and everything else I own goes after them. In exchange I'm going to ask them to measure the covenant on the test date only, not every week, and to give the company first right to buy my shares if they call them, so they can't go to Halvard. I'm telling you before, not after. I'll be back tomorrow night.*

It's read a minute later.

Nothing comes for nine minutes. I sit there with the phone face up on her desk on top of her graph paper and watch the three dots come up and go away and come back. I can picture her doing it, at the counter or on the couch in the front room where the wall is thicker, typing something and deleting it, then typing it again. She doesn't type long messages. She types like she tack-welds: short, and on to the next one.

Then it comes, two letters, *OK*, and that's all.

I don't know what kind of OK it is. Pip's got as many kinds of OK as her mother has kinds of pierogi, her brother Marek says, and you only find out which one you got when you bite into it.

A minute after that, a second one comes in.

*Drive safe.*

I set the phone down on the graph paper and put my hand over my eyes for a while.

The emergency board was at two that afternoon. I'd sat at the far end of the walnut, across from her, the way I always do now. Ruth had wanted to call Kurt back, not to restart the deal but to *reopen a dialogue*. She'd said it in the public-radio voice. Kurt would come back, she said, at a lower number, seven hundred maybe. It was still a life-changing number, and it was a floor under all of us, under Northgate, under the crew, under her fund's firefighters in Toledo. *Pip*, she said, *I'm asking you to let me make one call.*

And Pip said, very quietly, "No."

And I said, a half-second after her, "No."

Ruth had looked from one of us to the other, two people at opposite ends of a slab of walnut who hadn't spoken to each other in three days, saying the same word in the same voice. Her eyebrows went up and stayed there, and she put her tablet down flat on the table.

"Well," she said. "At least you're aligned."

I drive to Chicago on Tuesday at five in the morning in snow that turns to rain at Benton Harbor and back to snow at the Indiana line. I don't listen to anything. I usually listen to podcasts about leadership, men with good microphones explaining how they built things, and I leave them off. I want to hear myself think, and what I get is the wipers and the trucks going by, and every few miles, her voice saying *drive safe*.

Diane calls at seven, just past Benton Harbor. I emailed her the two pages at midnight.

"Adam, you can't do this," she says, with no hello. "This is insane. This is an unlimited personal guarantee. Do you know what unlimited means? It's the opposite of limited."

"I'm telling you before I do it, Diane."

"Before *what*? Before you hand a bank in Chicago your car keys?" I can hear her kitchen behind her, a kid asking for something, a cupboard. "Fine. If you're going to do something insane, you're going to do it in the right language. Section two is garbage. Pull over at the next exit and I'll read you section two."

I pull over at a Speedway in Bridgman and she reads me section two, and fixes it, and then section four, while a man in a Carhartt jacket fills a pickup with diesel two feet from my window and looks at me in my suit like I'm lost.

"There," Diane says, when section four's done. "Now it's insane in proper English. Drive safe."

That's two people this morning telling me to drive safe, and only one of them is a lawyer billing me for it.

Northgate Capital has the thirty-first floor of a black glass building on Wacker Drive, across the river from the Merchandise Mart. The river's gray today and full of ice. They're not dyeing it green for another six weeks. Neil Pruitt meets me at the elevator in a gray suit with a pin on his lapel shaped like a hockey stick. He shakes my hand with genuine warmth, a man glad to see a borrower he's going to have to hurt.

"Adam, you drove? In this weather?"

"I wanted to think."

"Well, I hope you thought of something." He laughs nervously. "Margaret's joining us, Margaret Oyelaran. She chairs credit committee. She doesn't usually come to these, but she wanted to see you."

Margaret Oyelaran is about sixty, Nigerian by way of Evanston, in a camel suit and glasses on a chain, with a legal pad in front of her and a fountain pen she doesn't uncap for the first ten minutes. We sit in a conference room with a view of the river. Neil offers coffee. I say no, and Margaret says nothing at all. Neil pours himself a cup anyway and never touches it, and by the end of the meeting it's grown a skin. She looks at me the way Simone looks at a spreadsheet she thinks is lying to her, patiently, waiting for the line that gives it away.

"Mr. Kerrigan," she says. "Neil tells me you asked for this meeting. You have counsel?"

"My counsel's on the phone in Grand Rapids, if you want her. She already fixed section two."

"You came without counsel in the room."

"I came to sign something, and I wanted to be in the room when I did."

Margaret uncaps the pen.

I don't give them the history. They have Gordon's reports; they can read. I tell them one thing they can't get from a spreadsheet: that Nashville was my idea, over my co-founder's objection, and the nine million went into it, and the plan that keeps this company alive is hers, not mine. Steel at net sixty, escrow at cut. That's all I say about it. Margaret watches me say it without blinking.

When I'm done, the room stays quiet long enough for a barge to go by on the river below, pushing ice.

"Why come in person?" Margaret says.

"Because I'm about to ask you to let me owe you more," I say, "and that's not a thing you ask for in an email."

"More," Margaret repeats, as if she's tasting the word. "And what do you want for it, Mr. Kerrigan?"

I take the papers out of my bag. I spent an hour last night on the floor of her room with my laptop, writing it out like a letter instead of a deck, and Diane fixed the language at a gas station in Bridgman. Two pages. I slide them across the table.

"I'm offering a personal guarantee," I say. "On top of the pledge. Unlimited. If the company misses the test on the twelfth and you call the loan, you take my shares first, and then you can come after everything else I own. My accounts, my car, my half of anything I've got. I won't contest section eleven. I won't fight you on the reading. I'll sign it today."

Neil makes a small sound, as if he's bitten his tongue.

"In exchange," I say, "two things. You measure the covenant on the twelfth, once, and not every week or on any other reporting date. And if you call the loan and take my shares, the company gets a right of first refusal to buy them back at your price within ninety days, before you sell them to anybody else. Anybody, including Minneapolis."

Margaret reads the two pages all the way through, and then she reads them again, slower. Her pen doesn't move.

"Your wife," she says without looking up. "The co-founder. Does she know you're here?"

"Yes, ma'am, she does."

"Does she know what you're offering?"

"Yes. I told her last night."

"What did she say?"

"She said *OK*."

Margaret looks up at me over her glasses, and one of her eyebrows goes up a sixteenth of an inch.

"And then?"

"Then she said *drive safe*."

Margaret Oyelaran puts the cap back on her fountain pen. She sets it down on the legal pad, parallel to the edge. She looks out the window at the barge and the ice and the Merchandise Mart, and then she turns to Neil.

"Neil," she says. "How many borrowers in twenty-two years have come into this room and asked to owe us more?"

Neil thinks about it seriously.

"None that I can think of," he says.

"None." She turns back to me. "Mr. Kerrigan, this doesn't help you very much. Your shares are worth a great deal more than anything else you own. If the company fails, a personal guarantee from a man whose net worth is mostly in that company is a gesture."

"I know it is."

"Then why make it?"

"Because it's all I've got that's mine," I say. "Everything else was hers too. Or the crew's. Or the company's. This is just me. I don't have anything else to put on the table that I didn't take from somebody without asking."

Nobody in the room moves.

"The test date only," Margaret says finally. "Measured once, on the twelfth. I can do that. The right of first refusal, I can do that too, with a ninety-day window, at our price. Neil will paper it this afternoon." She uncaps the pen again and makes a note. "I'm not doing it because of your guarantee. I want that on the record. Your guarantee is a gesture."

"Then why are you doing it?"

"Because Gordon's report says your first escrow release, one point five million dollars, lands on Thursday, when your steel is cut. A lender who calls a loan the week its collateral starts producing cash is a lender who deserves what happens next." She caps the pen. "And because my committee would rather lend to a company where the founders say *no* to the same man in the same voice. Neil sent me the minutes from yesterday. Ruth Baskin's note at the bottom." Margaret almost smiles. "*At least they're aligned.*"

I sign a little after three-thirty with Neil's pen, which says NORTHGATE on the side and skips on every capital letter. Forty-one pages in October; nine today. Neil walks me to the elevator. He keeps patting me on the shoulder like I've had a death in the family, which in a way I have. It's me, the man who had a plan for everything and a slide for the plan.

"Say hi to your wife," he says at the elevator. "And good luck with the knees."

I drive back in the dark. The snow's stopped. Michigan comes up out of Indiana flat and black and starry, and I don't turn on the radio, and somewhere around Paw Paw I look down and I'm doing eighty-five and humming. I don't know the song. It's something Polish. I've heard it through a wall.

I get to Clyde Park at 10:40 and text her from the church lot with the engine running.

*Signed. Test date only, Friday the 12th. First refusal if they call. My shares go first. Everything else after.*

I sit there while the dots come and go.

*OK*, she writes. Then: *You're in the room?*

*I'm going in now. I'll be out before Benny.*

The dots come and go for a while, and then nothing, and then the dots again.

*There's a blanket on the shelf*, she writes. *The good one. Not Darnell's. I brought it from home.*

I read it three times in the dark car. Then I drive across the street and punch in her birthday, and the gate rolls back, and I cross the yard in the dark to the room with the light on.

On the shelf, on top of Darnell's blanket, sits her grandmother's afghan, every color of yarn Goodwill had in 1981, folded with the corners square.
