## Chapter 24 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Monday night at 10:12, sitting in Pip's chair in the room in the yard with the heater ticking, I write her a text and don't send it for forty minutes.

I keep trying to make it better. That's the problem. I keep trying to make it a pitch. I write it with a reason in front, *because I love you*, which is true, and delete it, because it sounds like a man putting a bow on something before he hands it over. I write it with an apology in front and delete that too. I write it with a list. I write it as a question, *would it be okay if*, and look at that one for a long time, because it's closest. But it isn't a question. I've already decided, and if I phrase it as a question I'm pretending she gets a vote in something I've already done in my head, and she'll know, because she always knows.

At 10:52 I send the plain one.

*Tomorrow I'm driving to Chicago to see Northgate. I'm going to tell them the cash crisis was mine, Nashville, all of it. I'm going to sign a personal guarantee on top of the pledge, so if the company misses the test, my shares go first and everything else I own goes after them. In exchange I'm going to ask them to measure the covenant on the test date only, not every week, and to give the company first right to buy my shares if they call them, so they can't go to Halvard. I'm telling you before, not after. I'll be back tomorrow night.*

It's delivered at 10:52. Read at 10:53.

Nothing for nine minutes. I sit there with the phone face up on her desk on top of her graph paper and watch the three dots appear and disappear four times. Four times. I can picture her doing it. At her end of the counter, or on the couch in the front room where the wall is thicker, typing something and deleting it, typing and deleting. She doesn't type long messages. She types like she welds, short and tacked.

At 11:01:

*OK.*

That's it. Two letters. I look at it a long time. I don't know what kind of OK it is. Pip's got as many kinds of OK as her mother has kinds of pierogi, her brother Marek says, and you only find out which one you got when you bite into it.

Then, at 11:02, a second one.

*Drive safe.*

I put the phone down. I put my hand over my eyes for a while.

The emergency board was at two that afternoon. I'd sat at the far end of the walnut, across from her, the way I always do now. Ruth had wanted to call Kurt back. Not to restart the deal. To *reopen a dialogue*. She'd said it in the public-radio voice. Kurt would come back, she said, at a lower number. Seven hundred, maybe. It was still a life-changing number, and it was a floor under all of us, under Northgate, under the crew, under her fund's firefighters in Toledo. *Pip*, she said, *I'm asking you to let me make one call.*

And Pip said, very quietly: "No."

And I said, a half-second after her: "No."

Ruth had looked from one of us to the other, two people at opposite ends of a slab of walnut who hadn't spoken to each other in three days, saying the same word in the same voice, and something happened in her face I'd never seen before. She put her tablet down.

"Well," she said. "At least you're aligned."

I drive to Chicago on Tuesday at five in the morning in snow that turns to rain at Benton Harbor and back to snow at the Indiana line. I don't listen to anything. I usually listen to podcasts about leadership, men with good microphones explaining how they built things. I turn them off at the state line. I want to hear myself think, and what I hear is the wipers, and the trucks going by, and every few miles, her voice saying *drive safe*.

Northgate Capital has the thirty-first floor of a black glass building on Wacker Drive, across the river from the Merchandise Mart. The river's gray today and full of ice. They're not dyeing it green for another six weeks. Neil Pruitt meets me at the elevator in a gray suit with a pin on his lapel shaped like a hockey stick. He shakes my hand with genuine warmth, a man glad to see a borrower he's going to have to hurt.

"Adam. You drove? In this?"

"I wanted to think."

"Well, I hope you thought of something." He laughs nervously. "Margaret's joining us. Margaret Oyelaran. She chairs credit committee. She doesn't usually come to these. She wanted to see you."

Margaret Oyelaran is about sixty, Nigerian by way of Evanston, in a camel suit and glasses on a chain, with a legal pad in front of her and a fountain pen she doesn't uncap for the first ten minutes. We sit in a conference room with a view of the river. Neil offers coffee. I say no. Margaret says nothing at all. She looks at me the way Simone looks at a spreadsheet she thinks is lying to her, patiently, waiting for the line that gives it away.

"Mr. Kerrigan," she says. "Neil tells me you asked for this meeting. You have counsel?"

"No."

"You came without counsel."

"I came to tell you some things before you find them out."

Margaret uncaps the pen.

I tell them the bad part first.

It's easier the third time. It's still horrible. It's like the cold shower still, but you know where the water's coming from now. I tell them about Nashville, that it was my idea, over my co-founder's objection, without asking her, and that it's been losing four hundred and twenty thousand dollars a week for a year and building a room nobody wants. I tell them about October, that the reason the company needed their nine million wasn't the market or the economy or supply chains, it was me. I tell them Halvard walked because my wife went on television and said no, and that she was right to say no, and that she was wrong not to call me first, and that I'd taught her that by not calling her first for two years. I tell them about her plan. Steel at net sixty. Escrow at cut. A state training grant. Six days a week. A crew vote on Saturday.

I say *um* twice. I forget the name of the state fund and have to look it up on my phone. Margaret watches me do it without blinking.

When I'm done, nobody says anything for a while. On the river below, a barge goes by, pushing ice.

"Why are you telling us this?" Margaret says.

"Because you're going to find out. From the reports. From the minutes. From Channel 6. You've already found out half of it. I'd rather you heard it from me, in the right order, before you decide anything."

"In the right order," Margaret repeats.

"Mine's the wrong order. I'm working on it."

"And what do you want, Mr. Kerrigan?"

I take the papers out of my bag. Not a deck. I spent an hour last night on the floor of the room in the yard with my laptop, writing it out like a letter, and Diane looked at it at seven this morning on the phone and said *Adam, you can't do this, this is insane*, and I said *Diane, I'm telling you before*, and she said *before WHAT* and then she fixed the language. Two pages. I slide them across the table.

"I'm offering a personal guarantee," I say. "On top of the pledge. Unlimited. If the company misses the test on the eighth and you call the loan, you take my shares first, and then you can come after everything else I own. My accounts. My car. My half of anything I've got. I won't contest section eleven. I won't fight you on the reading. I'll sign it today."

Neil makes a small sound, like a man who's bitten his tongue.

"In exchange," I say, "two things. You measure the covenant on the twelfth. Once. Not every week. Not any reporting date. The eighth. And if you call the loan and take my shares, the company gets a right of first refusal to buy them back at your price within ninety days, before you sell them to anybody else. Anybody. Including Minneapolis."

Margaret reads the two pages. She reads them all the way through, and then she reads them again, slower. Her pen doesn't move.

"Your wife," she says without looking up. "The co-founder. Does she know you're here?"

"Yes."

"Does she know what you're offering?"

"Yes. I told her last night."

"What did she say?"

"She said *OK*."

Margaret looks up at me over her glasses. Something happens at the corner of her mouth.

"And then?"

"She said *drive safe*."

Margaret Oyelaran puts the cap back on her fountain pen. She sets it down on the legal pad, parallel to the edge. She looks out the window at the river for a long moment, at the barge and the ice and the Merchandise Mart, and then she turns to Neil.

"Neil," she says. "How many borrowers in twenty-two years have come into this room and asked to owe us more?"

Neil thinks about it seriously.

"None," he says.

"None." She turns back to me. "Mr. Kerrigan, I'm going to be honest with you. This doesn't help you very much. Your shares are worth a great deal more than anything else you own. If the company fails, a personal guarantee from a man whose net worth is mostly in that company is a gesture."

"I know."

"Then why make it?"

"Because it's all I've got that's mine," I say. "Everything else was hers too. Or the crew's. Or the company's. This is just me. I don't have anything else to put on the table that I didn't take from somebody without asking."

The room's very quiet.

"The test date only," Margaret says finally. "Measured once, on the twelfth. I can do that. The right of first refusal, I can do that too, with a ninety-day window, at our price. Neil will paper it this afternoon." She uncaps the pen again and makes a note. "I'm not doing it because of your guarantee. I want to be clear about that. Your guarantee is a gesture."

"Then why?"

"Because Gordon's report says your escrow released one point five million dollars on Thursday, and a lender who calls a loan the week its collateral starts producing cash is a lender who deserves what happens next." She looks at me. "And because my committee would rather lend to a company where the founders say *no* to the same man in the same voice. Neil sent me the minutes from yesterday. Ruth Baskin's note at the bottom." Margaret almost smiles. "*At least they're aligned.*"

I sign at 3:40 in the afternoon. Forty-one pages in October; nine today. Neil walks me to the elevator. He keeps patting me on the shoulder like I've had a death in the family, which in a way I have. It's me, the man who had a plan for everything and a slide for the plan.

"Say hi to your wife," he says at the elevator. "And good luck Saturday. With the vote."

"How do you know about the vote?"

"You told us," Neil says. "About an hour ago. You said *um*."

I drive back in the dark. The snow's stopped. Michigan comes up out of Indiana flat and black and starry, and I don't turn on the radio, and somewhere around Paw Paw I realize I'm doing eighty-five and humming. I don't know the song. It's something Polish. I've heard it through a wall.

I get to Clyde Park at 10:40. I text her from the church lot before I go in.

*Signed. Test date only, Friday the 12th. Right of first refusal if they call. My shares go first. Everything else after.*

I sit there. The dots come and go. Come and go.

*OK*, she writes. Then: *You're in the room?*

*I'm going in now. I'll be out before Benny.*

A long pause. The dots. Nothing. The dots again.

*There's a blanket on the shelf*, she writes. *The good one. Not Darnell's. I brought it from home.*

I look at the screen a long time. Then I drive across the street and punch in her birthday, and the gate rolls back, and I cross the yard in the dark to the room with the light on.

On the shelf by the door, on top of Darnell's moving blanket, there's her grandmother's afghan. Every color of yarn they had at Goodwill in 1981, folded with the corners square.
