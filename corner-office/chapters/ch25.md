## Chapter 25 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

I don't call Northgate.

That's the first thing I do on Wednesday morning: not call. I'm sitting at my end of the counter at six with the moka pot going *blup* on the stove and my phone face up next to my mug, and Neil Pruitt's number is right there in the text from the parking ramp, and I could call it. I could call and say *this is Philippa Sobczak, I'm the wife, tell me exactly what he signed*. I could ask Diane to send me the nine pages. I could check.

I don't. He told me before. He told me what he was going to do, and then he did it, and then he told me he'd done it. That's the rule. My father says that's the whole rule. If I call to check, I'm saying it isn't enough.

It's enough. It's so much more than enough I can't look at it straight on. *My shares go first. Everything else after.* I read it standing at the stove, and I have to turn the burner off and hold onto the edge of the counter for a minute, the river one, the one he wanted.

Then I drink my coffee and go to work.

Wednesday I sign Simone's forty pages for the state, at the kitchen table on Garfield Avenue with my father watching over his reading glasses like a notary. Thursday morning Gil Chamberlain's first trailer of tube comes in at six-forty, two hours early, because Gil's driver left Monroe at four to beat the snow. The knee line cuts steel for the first four hundred units by noon. Gordon calls at three, out of breath, from the stairwell where he takes calls he's afraid to have in the glass room.

"It released," he says. "The escrow. The first tranche. One point five two. It's in the operating account. It's—Pip, it's *there*. I can see it."

"Good."

"I've been in finance for twenty-eight years and I've never—" He stops. "My father would've liked your rule."

"Your father was an accountant for the church."

"That's what I mean," Gordon says, and hangs up, and I think he might be crying again, and I think it might be the good kind.

By Thursday afternoon the math looks like this. I write it on the whiteboard in the break room myself, in my own handwriting, under Darnell's count, *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 7,804*:

*HOLE: 2.3*
*STATE GRANT: −0.61*
*= 1.7*

Under that, in a box:

*SATURDAYS × 12 WKS + 2ND SHIFT TRAINING (KNEE) = FASTER CUT = FASTER ESCROW*
*CREW EQUITY: 5% OF CO. FROM PIP'S SHARES → TRUST → EVERYONE ON THE FLOOR*

Under that, a smaller box, with nothing in it. I don't write *NASH* in it. I don't write anything in it. I just draw the box and leave it empty, and I stand back and look at the board for a long time, and Lolo comes in for second shift and stands next to me and looks at it too.

"Five percent," she says.

"From my third."

"That's a lot of company, hon."

"It's a lot of Saturdays."

Lolo looks at the board. She doesn't take her glasses off. "You going to ask them?"

"Saturday. Six a.m. Shift change. Everybody on the floor. Both shifts. I'm going to ask."

"Ask, or tell?"

"Ask," I say. "It's a vote. If they say no, I don't have a plan."

Lolo nods slowly. "I'm going to vote no, hon. I told you."

"I know."

"I'm going to stand up and say why."

"I want you to."

She looks at me sideways for a while. Then she reaches over and squeezes my arm, once, hard, the way she used to squeeze my father's arm at Christmas when he did his impression of her, and goes off to punch in.

I stand there and look at the empty box.

At nine o'clock Thursday night I walk across the yard in the dark to my own room and knock on the glass with one knuckle.

The light's on inside. He's at my desk with his laptop and Simone's model open on the screen and a legal pad covered in his handwriting, which is terrible, big and slanted and full of arrows, like a man drawing a play on a napkin at a bar. He looks up. He sees me through the glass corner.

He gets up and opens the door.

"Hi," he says.

"Hi. Can I come in?"

"It's your room."

"I know it's my room," I say. "You're in it. I'm asking."

He steps back. "Yes. Come in."

I come in. It's warm. It smells like cedar and the afghan, which is folded on the shelf, and like him, the soap he's used since 2013 and something under it that's just him, the way the inside of a truck smells like the person who drives it. There's a sleeping bag rolled up in the corner under my desk. A toothbrush in a coffee mug on the windowsill. Two clean shirts on hangers on the coat hook by the door, the hook I screwed into the cedar in 2014, which has never held a shirt in its life.

"You moved in," I say.

"I'm camping."

"You've got a toothbrush."

"I've got a toothbrush," he agrees.

I sit down in the chair by the radiator. His chair. He doesn't say anything about it. He sits down in my chair, at my desk, set at my height, and his knees come up, and we look at each other across the corner of the desk in the light from the lamp.

"I'm asking the crew on Saturday," I say. "Six a.m. I'm telling you before."

"I know. Lolo told me."

"Lolo told you?"

"Lolo came by at eleven last night. With a bowling ball. She said she was going to show me how to hold it." He rubs his face. "She talked for an hour. Mostly about Florida. She's going to vote no."

"She told me."

"She told me you told her you wanted her to say why."

"I do."

He looks at me for a long moment. "That's—Pip. That's a good thing to want."

"I'm learning."

"Me too," he says.

We sit there. The heater ticks. Outside, the yard light hums, and the snow's coming down again, slow and big, the soft kind.

And then, before I can think about it, before I can plan anything, he gets up out of my chair and comes around the desk. He doesn't stand over me. He gets down on the floor, on my mother's braided rug, on his knees, like a man about to fix the radiator, except he's not looking at the radiator. He's looking at me.

"I'm sorry," he says.

I don't say anything. I can't.

"I'm sorry about Nashville. I'm sorry about the slide. I'm sorry I stood up in front of three hundred people and decided our life without you and then kept talking because I was in the middle of a pitch. I'm sorry about the room. *You barely use it.* I said that to you in front of the whole floor, and I said it like a fact on a slide, and I knew what the room was. I was at the wedding. I made the speech." His voice doesn't break. It just gets very quiet, quiet the way Simone's gets, every word set down one at a time. "I'm sorry about Northgate. I'm sorry I didn't knock. I'm sorry I called her at one in the morning and told her the things I should have told you, and called it work. I'm sorry about what I said at the counter. I'm sorry I knew you that well and used it."

He stops. He's still on his knees on the rug.

"I'm not asking you for anything," he says. "I'm not pitching. I'm not going to tell you what to do with it. I don't have a slide. I just wanted you to hear it in one place, all of it, before Saturday, in case Saturday goes wrong. I wanted you to know I know. Please. That's all. Please just know I know."

The room's so quiet I can hear the snow landing on the roof.

I've been waiting two years for this. I didn't know that's what I was waiting for until he's on the floor in front of me saying it. I always thought when it came it would feel like winning. It doesn't feel like winning. It feels like when you take a weld you've been holding with a clamp for an hour and finally open the clamp, and the two pieces stay together, and your hand doesn't know what to do now that it's not squeezing.

I don't say *I forgive you*. I don't know if I do yet. I don't say anything about it at all.

I ask him something instead.

"Were you ever there?"

He looks at me. "Where?"

"Coffee at seven. After I stopped. After Nashville." My voice comes out strange. "Were you ever there? In the room. At seven. After I stopped coming."

He doesn't answer right away. He looks at me with an expression I don't have catalogued, and I understand that he's deciding whether to tell me, and that he's going to, and that it's going to cost him something.

"Fifteen mornings," he says.

The room goes away. Not dark. Just away, the way a room goes away when you stand up too fast.

"Three weeks," he says. "Every working day. I got there at five to seven. I made yours at home, in your pot, and brought it in the green thermos. I sat in this chair—" he touches the radiator chair, the one I'm sitting in "—and put my feet up and waited. Till seven-thirty. Then I poured yours in the fern by the elevator and went to work."

"Fifteen."

"The sixteenth morning I had a flight. I didn't go up. And the next morning was easier." He looks down at the rug. "I told myself you'd stopped. That you'd made your choice and I'd shown up for three weeks and you hadn't. I've been carrying that around for two years like a receipt."

I was at my bench. Six-thirty, every morning, filing things, with the knee line coming on around me, thinking *he's stopped, he doesn't care, he's on a plane*, thinking it so hard I made it true. And five miles north, on the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building, he was sitting in this chair with my terrible coffee going cold, waiting for me.

Lolo was right. I wasn't there. How would I know if he stopped?

I'm crying. I didn't know I was going to. It comes up out of me with no sound at all, my face just wet, both hands flat on my knees, the way I sat at the walnut table when he said *hers*. I don't wipe it. I let it go. He doesn't reach for me. He stays on the rug on his knees and looks at me and lets me, which is the right thing, which is the thing nobody but him would know to do.

"I stopped so you'd come find me," I say, when I can. "I wanted you to come out to the plant. Knock on the glass. Say *I'm sorry about the slide*. I sat at my bench for three weeks waiting for you to walk across that floor."

"I sat in this chair for three weeks waiting for you to walk across that floor."

"We were waiting at the wrong ends."

"We were waiting at the wrong ends," he agrees, very quietly.

I look at him. On his knees on my mother's rug, in a shirt he's worn two days, with his hair sticking up in the back where he slept on it on my floor.

"What happened to the fern?" I ask.

He almost laughs. His face does something complicated, and his eyes are wet now too.

"It died," he says. "They replaced it with a fake one."
