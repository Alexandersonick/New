## Chapter 25 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

I don't call Northgate.

That's the first thing I do on Wednesday morning: not call. I'm sitting at the counter at six with the moka pot going *blup* on the stove and my phone face up next to my mug, and Neil Pruitt's number is right there in the text from the parking ramp, and I could call it. I could call and say *this is Philippa Sobczak, I'm the wife, tell me exactly what he signed*. I could ask Diane to send me the nine pages. I could check up on my own husband.

I don't, because he told me what he was going to do, and then he did it, and then he told me he'd done it. My father says that's the whole rule. If I call to check, I'm saying it isn't enough.

It's enough, and it's so much more than enough I can't look at it straight on. *My shares go first. Everything else after.* I read it again standing at the stove, and I have to turn the burner off and hold onto the edge of the counter for a minute, the river one, the one he wanted.

Then I drink my coffee and go to work.

Wednesday I sign Simone's forty pages for the state at the kitchen table on Garfield Avenue, with my father watching over his reading glasses like a notary.

"You read it?" he says.

"Simone read it, Tato, and Simone wrote it."

"Read it anyway, Philippa." He slides the pen back across the oilcloth to me. "You don't sign what you didn't read. You don't need a door for that one. It's plain sense."

So I read all forty pages while my mother irons in the next room and says *so* through the doorway every few minutes, at nothing, and then I sign.

Thursday morning Gil Chamberlain's first trailer of tube comes in at six-forty, two hours early, because Gil's driver left Monroe at four to beat the snow. The knee line cuts steel for the first four hundred units by noon. Gordon calls at three, out of breath, from the stairwell where he takes calls he's afraid to have in the glass room.

"It released, Pip," he says, without hello. "The escrow, the first tranche, one point five two. It's in the operating account. It's actually *there*. I can see it on the screen."

"That's good, Gordon. That's really good."

"I've been in finance for twenty-eight years, and I've never had a number do what it said it would on the day it said it would." He breathes for a second. "My father would've liked your rule."

"Your father was an accountant for the church."

"That's what I mean," Gordon says, and hangs up, and I'm fairly sure the stairwell's getting the rest of it.

By Thursday afternoon the math looks like this. I write it on the whiteboard in the break room myself, in my own handwriting, under Darnell's count, *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 7,804*:

*HOLE: 2.3*
*STATE GRANT: −0.61*
*= 1.7*

Under that, in a box:

*SATURDAYS × 12 WKS + 2ND SHIFT TRAINING (KNEE) = FASTER CUT = FASTER ESCROW*
*CREW EQUITY: 5% OF CO. FROM PIP'S SHARES → TRUST → EVERYONE ON THE FLOOR*

Under that, a smaller box, with nothing in it. I don't write *NASH* in it. I don't write anything in it. I just draw the box and leave it empty and step back with the marker still uncapped in my hand, and Lolo comes in for second shift and stands next to me and reads it too.

"Five percent of the company," she says.

"From my third, Lolo, out of my own end."

"That's a lot of company, hon."

"It's a lot of Saturdays."

She keeps reading the board. "You going to ask them?"

"Saturday at six, at shift change, with everybody on the floor from both shifts, and I'm going to put it to them."

"Ask, or tell?"

"I'm going to ask, Lolo," I say. "It's a real vote, and if they say no, I don't have a plan."

Lolo nods slowly at the board. "I'm going to vote no, hon, like I told you."

"I know you are, Lolo."

"I'm going to stand up and say why."

"I want you to stand up and say why."

She looks at me sideways for a while. Then she reaches over and squeezes my arm, once, hard, the way she used to squeeze my father's arm at Christmas when he did his impression of her, and goes off to punch in.

I stay and look at the empty box until the marker dries out in my hand.

At nine o'clock Thursday night I walk across the yard in the dark to my own room and knock on the glass with one knuckle.

The light's on inside, and he's at my desk with his laptop and Simone's model open on the screen and a legal pad covered in his handwriting, which is terrible, big and slanted and full of arrows, a play drawn on a bar napkin. He looks up and finds me through the glass.

He gets up and opens the door. "Hi," he says, and holds it.

"Hi. Can I come in, or is this a bad time?"

"It's your room, Pip. You don't have to ask."

"I know it's my room," I say. "You're in it, so I'm asking."

He steps back out of the doorway. "Then yes, come in."

I come in, and it's warm, and it smells like cedar and the afghan, which is folded on the shelf, and like him, the soap he's used since 2013 and something under it that's just him, the way the inside of a truck smells like whoever drives it. There's a sleeping bag rolled up in the corner under my desk, a toothbrush in a coffee mug on the windowsill, and two clean shirts on hangers on the coat hook by the door, the hook I screwed into the cedar in 2014, which has never held a shirt in its life.

"You moved in," I say.

"I'm camping, which is different."

"You've got a toothbrush in a mug."

"I've got a toothbrush," he agrees.

"Who ironed those?" I nod at the shirts.

"Nobody. They're from the cleaner on Clyde Park, by the Wendy's. The woman at the counter thinks I'm in witness protection, and I haven't corrected her."

I sit down in the radiator chair, his chair, and he lets it go by. He sits down in my chair, at my desk, set at my height, and his knees come up, and we face each other across the corner of the desk in the light from the lamp.

"I'm putting it to the crew on Saturday at six," I say. "I'm telling you before I do it."

"I know, Lolo told me last night."

"Lolo came out here to tell you?"

"Lolo came by at eleven with a bowling ball. She said she was going to show me how to hold it." He rubs his face with both hands. "She talked for an hour, mostly about Florida. She's going to vote no."

"She told me that too."

"She told me you told her you wanted her to say why."

"I do want that," I say.

He sits with that, with his knees up and his hands hanging between them. "That's a good thing to want, Pip."

"Don't say that to her, she'll want it in writing."

That gets most of a smile out of him, and then it goes.

We sit there while the heater ticks. Outside, the yard light hums, and the snow's coming down again, slow and big, the soft kind.

And then, before I can think about it, before I can plan anything, he gets up out of my chair and comes around the desk. He doesn't stand over me. He gets down on the floor, on my mother's braided rug, on his knees, like a man about to fix the radiator, except he's facing me and not the radiator.

"I'm sorry," he says.

My hands are flat on my knees, and I keep them there.

"I'm sorry about Nashville. I'm sorry about the slide. I'm sorry I stood up in front of three hundred people and decided our life without you and then kept talking because I was in the middle of a pitch. I'm sorry about the room. *You barely use it.* I said that to you in front of the whole floor, and I said it like a fact on a slide, and I knew what the room was. I was at the wedding. I made the speech." His voice doesn't break. It just gets very quiet, every word set down one at a time, like Simone's. "I'm sorry about Northgate. I'm sorry I didn't knock. I'm sorry I called her at one in the morning and told her the things I should have told you, and called it work. I'm sorry about what I said at the counter. I'm sorry I knew you that well and used it."

He stops, still on his knees on the rug.

"I'm not asking you for anything," he says, "and I'm not pitching. What you do with it is yours. I don't have a slide. I just wanted you to hear it in one place, all of it, before Saturday, in case Saturday goes wrong. Please. Please just know I know."

The room's so quiet I can hear the snow landing on the roof.

I've been waiting for this longer than I'd ever admit, and I didn't know it until he's on the floor in front of me saying it. I always thought when it came it would feel like winning. What it feels like is when you take a weld you've been holding with a clamp for an hour and finally open the clamp, and the two pieces stay together, and your hand doesn't know what to do now that it's not squeezing.

I don't say *I forgive you*. I don't know if I do yet. I leave it on the rug between us where he put it.

I ask him something instead. It's the thing I've wanted to ask since the elevator doors opened on four, with the ficus on my desk.

"Why did you give her the room?"

He doesn't answer right away. He looks past me at the door, at the three lines in pencil, and I watch him decide to tell me the true version and not the one he's practised.

"Because I couldn't walk past it," he says. "We got married in it. And then I stopped going in, after Nashville, and every time I came off the elevator it was sitting there in the corner with the lights off, and it was proof. Of what I'd done. Of what we'd turned into. So when Simone asked for a door that closes, I said yes before she finished, because then it wouldn't be dark anymore. Somebody would be in it. I'd walk past and the light would be on, and I could pretend that meant something." He swallows. "I told you you barely used it. I barely could look at it. I gave it away so I'd stop having to."

The room goes away. Not dark. Just away, as a room goes when you stand up too fast.

I'm crying. I didn't know I was going to. It comes up out of me with no sound at all, my face just wet, both hands flat on my knees, the way I sat at the walnut table with the receipts between them. I don't wipe it, and he doesn't reach for me. He stays on the rug on his knees and lets me.

"You gave away the room where we got married," I say, when I can, "so you wouldn't have to look at it."

"Yes."

"To a woman you called at one in the morning."

"Yes."

He doesn't add anything to either one. He doesn't explain, or soften it, or tell me she's a good person, which she is. He just kneels there on my mother's braided rug in a shirt he's worn two days, with his hair sticking up in the back where he slept on it on my floor, and lets both of those sit in the room with us, ugly and true and his.

When I can talk again, I say the only practical thing I can find.

"Get up off my mother's rug. You'll wreck your knees."

He laughs once, through his nose, and wipes his eyes with the heel of his hand, and doesn't get up yet.

"It's a good rug," he says.
