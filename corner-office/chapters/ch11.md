## Chapter 11 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Ruth Baskin drinks an oat milk cortado with an extra shot, standing up, at a tall table by the window of a coffee place on Fulton Street that used to be a bank. The vault door is still there in the back, propped open, with a bookshelf inside it. Everything downtown used to be something else. That's the whole economy now, my brother Marek says: you take a building that did one thing and you make it do a cuter thing.

"Pip." She puts her hand out across the table. "Thank you for coming in."

"You drove from Ann Arbor for this?"

"I drive from Ann Arbor every week. It's an hour and ten if you don't respect the speed limit." She shakes my hand, firm, once, like she's testing a railing. "I listen to audiobooks about the Roman Empire on the way. I'm on the fall, and it's very relaxing."

Ruth is fifty-eight, small and wiry, with short gray hair that she gets cut every three weeks by somebody very expensive, a quilted vest, and running shoes that cost more than my boots. She's been on our board since the Series B. She's smart and she isn't mean. She's what my father would call a person who's never had to lift the heavy end of anything, which isn't the same as mean but rhymes with it.

I go up to the counter and order a regular coffee. The girl asks what size and I say *coffee size*, and she looks at me like I've spoken Latin at her, and then she rings me up for a large that costs six dollars and comes in a cardboard sleeve that says *Hello, Neighbor*.

"I'll get to it," Ruth says when I come back. "You don't like being warmed up. I've read the file."

"There's a file on me?"

"There's always a file, Pip." She sips. "I want you to vote yes on Halvard."

"I know you do."

"I'd like to tell you why, and then I'd like you to tell me why not, and then I'd like us to see if there's a version where we both get a little of what we want. That's what I do all day. That's the job."

"Go ahead, then."

She tells me why, and she does it well. She has a voice like a public radio host, and she doesn't use slides, and she doesn't do the nose voice. She tells me about the multiple, which is good. She tells me about the market, which is soft, and about a company in Denver that did tiny houses and a company in Austin that did backyard studios with a solar roof, both gone now, one into bankruptcy and the other into a much larger company that shut it down in a year and kept the logo. She tells me that founders always think they're the exception. She tells me it's very rare to get an offer like this at a time like this, and that she has a fiduciary duty, and that so do I.

"I know I do," I say. "To the company."

"To the shareholders, Pip."

"The company's the shareholders."

"The company's a lot of things." She smiles a little and counts them off on the table with one fingertip. "Your crew, your customers, two thousand-odd people with their deposits in escrow in Cleveland. Me, you, Adam."

"Halvard will close my plant."

"In eighteen months, yes."

"Kurt told Adam thirty-six."

Ruth tilts her head. That's news to her, and I watch her file it somewhere behind her eyes.

"In writing?" she says.

"I don't know. I get told things in kitchens at midnight."

"Well," Ruth says, "thirty-six is better than eighteen."

"Zero's better than thirty-six."

"Zero's not on the table, Pip."

"Then I'm a no."

She doesn't get angry; she lays one hand flat on the table next to her cup, and that's the thing about Ruth. She takes the no and holds it up to the light like a dentist with an X-ray, turning it, looking for the cavity.

"You know the arithmetic," she says. "Adam and Lakeshore together are sixty-seven. A sale needs seventy-five. Without you it doesn't happen, and everybody at that table on the nineteenth knows it, which is why I'm buying you coffee."

"You didn't buy me coffee. I bought my coffee."

"Then I owe you six dollars." She doesn't reach for her wallet. "Do you know what my phone looked like the morning after your video? My limited partners called me thirty-one times. Not one of them asked about the room. They asked if the founders were getting divorced."

"What did you tell them?"

"That we'd take it offline." She looks at me over the rim of her cup. "Are you?"

"I just put a better heater in my office," I say.

Ruth waits a second to see if there's more, and there isn't, and she nods like I've answered her. "Tell me your why not."

"Here's my why not," I say. "The corner. You can't build it with a post. If you put a post in it you've got the Lite, and the Lite's a good room, Simone's done good work on it, but it isn't what three thousand people just put money down on. They put money down on a room you can sit in and look out the corner. Halvard's going to take their money and give them a post. That's my why not."

"Three thousand?"

"Three thousand one hundred and forty, as of nine o'clock." I take out my phone and read the newest text from Tavi. "Make that one hundred and forty-four."

Ruth looks at the phone in my hand. Then she turns to the window, where a bus goes by on Fulton with an ad on the side for a personal injury lawyer whose face is on every bus in West Michigan, smiling, with his thumbs up, like a man who can't believe you got hurt either.

"Pip," she says. "I'm going to say something to you, and then I'm going to finish my cortado, and then I'm going to drive back to Ann Arbor and listen to the Visigoths sack Rome."

"Okay," I say. "Say it."

"There are things about the company's current position that you'd want to understand before the nineteenth. Things that would make Halvard look different to you. I didn't say better."

The coffee in my cup is too hot to drink and I hold it anyway, because my hands want something to do. Then I put it down on the table between us. "What things?"

Ruth drinks the rest of her cortado. She puts the little glass down on the table and lines it up with the edge, the way Simone squared her napkin, the way people do when they're about to leave a room on purpose.

"Ask your husband," she says.

She puts on her coat, a long black puffer that makes her look like a very determined sleeping bag, and shakes my hand again, once, the railing test.

"I like you," she says. "I liked the video. My daughter sent it to me from Oberlin, and my daughter has never sent me anything about my work in her life. She asked me if you were hiring." She pulls her gloves on. "She's a philosophy major."

"Tell her we need welders."

"I'll tell her. She'll think you're being metaphorical." Ruth picks up her bag. "I'll see you on the nineteenth."

She goes out into the cold, and I sit there at the tall table by the window of the bank that isn't a bank anymore, holding a cup I can't drink.

*Ask your husband.*

I could call him right now. He's on four, in the glass room. If I lean I can almost make out the top of the Lindqvist building two blocks down over the roofs, with its water tower and its painted sign. I could call and say *Ruth says there are things*, and he'd pick up, he always picks up, and he'd say *what things*, and I'd know from the half second before *what things* whether there were things.

I take my phone out and look at his name: ADAM, with the photo I put on his contact in 2016 of him asleep in the truck at a rest stop outside Toledo, mouth open, a bag of pretzels on his chest.

I text Tavi instead. *How many now.* He answers in four seconds with *3,151* and a little crane.

I tell myself it's because Adam will pitch me. That he'll get on the phone and do the voice and I'll lose an hour of a Wednesday listening to the Rotary version of whatever this is. That if there are things, they'll be on the agenda on the nineteenth, in a gray box, and I can read them there like everybody else.

That's not the reason. The reason is that if I ask him and he tells me, then it's a thing we know together, and I've gotten very used to not knowing things together.

I put my phone in my coat and drive to Clyde Park.

Lolo's on early because Darnell's at the hospital with his wife for her second false alarm this week. She's in the break room with Irene and a box of Marge's, standing at the whiteboard by the time clock where Darnell keeps the count. *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 3,161.* Under that, somebody's drawn a little crane, worse than the marketing one.

"Hon," Lolo says when she sees me. "Irene and me did some math."

"Since when do you do math?"

"Since you started sitting in a room in the yard drawing on graph paper and not telling anybody why." She uncaps a marker and holds it out sideways without looking. "Irene."

Irene steps up and takes it. Irene's fifty, from Bay City, a glazing lead for nine years, and she talks maybe twelve sentences a day, eleven of them about glass. She writes on the whiteboard in her square capitals.

*1 SHIFT: 1,800/YR.*
*2 SHIFTS: 3,400.*
*2 SHIFTS + SAT: 4,100.*

She underlines the last one twice and hands the marker back to Lolo.

"That's knee capacity," Lolo says. "That's how many knees we can weld if everybody works Saturdays. Darnell figured the paint booth can keep up if we run it two shifts. Benny says the forklift can keep up if Benny gets a raise."

"Benny always says that."

"Benny's always right." Lolo caps the marker. "So you've got three thousand people wanting a room with no post in it, and you've got a plant that can make four thousand a year if you ask nice. That's what's on your graph paper."

Irene turns around from the board with her arms folded. "Saturdays I go to my sister's," she says. "I'll go Sundays. She won't like it. She'll live."

"Write that down, hon," Lolo says to me. "Irene just gave up a sister for you."

"I'll put it in the plan," I say. "Line item, one sister, Bay City."

"Put it above the steel," says Irene, and turns back to the board, which is more sentences than I've heard from her since Thanksgiving.

I look at the board. Four thousand one hundred, against the four thousand and twenty on my graph paper. Irene's number is better because Irene knows the glass line better than I do.

"It's not enough by itself," I say. "You need steel up front, and glass. You need money before the money."

"So get money."

"From where, Lolo?"

"I don't know, hon. You're the unicorn." She slides the marker into her hair over one ear. "I'm a second shift lead. I make knees. You tell me how many knees."

The door from the floor bangs open and Benny comes in for coffee, peeling off one glove with his teeth. He stops in front of the whiteboard and reads it with his chin up, the way he reads a pick ticket.

"Four thousand rooms," he says. "Where are you putting them? The pad holds twelve, and one of them's her office."

"We ship faster," I say.

"Then the forklift goes faster." He takes a Marge's out of the box and holds it up at me like evidence. "Which means the forklift driver needs a raise."

"Told you," says Lolo.

I hold my phone up and photograph the whiteboard, and Benny leans into the frame with his doughnut, so I take it again.

"Nobody erases that," Lolo tells the room, which is Irene and Benny and a vending machine. "Anybody erases that board, they answer to me."

I stand there and look at the board, at Irene's numbers and Darnell's paint booth and Benny's forklift, at the crew already doing my arithmetic in the break room on their own, because they saw me drawing on graph paper and figured whatever I was drawing, they'd better be in on it.

*Nobody decides alone.*

Okay, Tato.

"I'm taking it to the board on the nineteenth," I say. "A plan. We build them here, every one of them."

Irene nods once, like that's settled, and goes back to the glazing line. Lolo looks at me over her glasses, then takes them off and lets them drop on their beaded chain.

"About time," she says. "Now go tell your husband."
