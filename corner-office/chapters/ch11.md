## Chapter 11 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Ruth Baskin drinks an oat milk cortado with an extra shot, standing up, at a tall table by the window of a coffee place on Fulton Street that used to be a bank. The vault door is still there in the back, propped open, with a bookshelf inside it. Everything downtown used to be something else. That's the whole economy now, my brother Marek says. Taking a building that did one thing and making it do a cuter thing.

"Pip." She puts her hand out across the table. "Thank you for coming in."

"You drove from Ann Arbor."

"I drive from Ann Arbor every week. It's an hour and ten if you don't respect the speed limit." She shakes my hand, firm, once, like she's testing a railing. "I listen to audiobooks about the Roman Empire. I'm on the fall. It's very relaxing."

Ruth is fifty-eight, small and wiry, with short gray hair that she gets cut every three weeks by somebody very expensive, and a quilted vest, and running shoes that cost more than my boots. She's been on our board since the Series B. She's smart. She's not mean. She's what my father would call a person who's never had to lift the heavy end of anything, which isn't the same as mean but rhymes with it.

I order a regular coffee. The girl at the counter asks what size and I say *coffee size* and she looks at me like I've spoken Latin.

"I'll get to it," Ruth says when I come back. "You don't like being warmed up. I've read the file."

"There's a file?"

"There's always a file." She sips. "I want you to vote yes on Halvard."

"I know."

"I'd like to tell you why, and then I'd like you to tell me why not, and then I'd like us to see if there's a version where we both get a little of what we want. That's what I do all day. That's the job."

"Go ahead."

She tells me why. She does it well. She has a voice like a public radio host, and she doesn't use slides, and she doesn't do the nose voice. She tells me about the multiple, which is good. She tells me about the market, which is soft, and about a company in Denver that did tiny houses and a company in Austin that did backyard studios with a solar roof, both of which are gone now, one into bankruptcy and the other into a much larger company that shut it down in a year and kept the logo. She tells me that founders always think they're the exception. She tells me that it's very rare to get an offer like this at a time like this, and that she has a fiduciary duty, and that so do I.

"I know I do," I say. "To the company."

"To the shareholders."

"The company's the shareholders."

"The company's a lot of things." She smiles a little. "Your crew. Your customers. Two thousand-odd people with their deposits in escrow in Cleveland. Me. You. Adam."

"Halvard will close my plant."

"In eighteen months."

"Kurt told Adam thirty-six."

Ruth tilts her head. That's news to her. I watch her file it.

"In writing?" she says.

"I don't know. I'm told things in kitchens at midnight."

"Well," Ruth says. "Thirty-six is better than eighteen."

"Zero's better than thirty-six."

"Zero's not on the table, Pip."

"Then I'm a no."

She doesn't get angry. That's the thing about Ruth. She takes the no and holds it up to the light like a dentist with an X-ray, turning it, looking for the cavity.

"Here's my why not," I say, because she asked. "The corner. You can't build it with a post. If you put a post in it you've got the Lite, and the Lite's a good room, Simone's done good work on it, but it's not what three thousand people just put money down on. They put money down on a room you can sit in and look out the corner. Halvard's going to take their money and give them a post. That's my why not."

"Three thousand?"

"Three thousand one hundred and forty, as of nine o'clock." I take my phone out and look at the text from Tavi. "One hundred and forty-four."

Ruth looks at the phone. Then she looks out the window at Fulton Street, where a bus goes by with an ad on the side for a personal injury lawyer whose face is on every bus in West Michigan, smiling, with his thumbs up, like a man who can't believe you got hurt either.

"Pip," she says. "I'm going to say something to you, and then I'm going to finish my cortado, and then I'm going to drive back to Ann Arbor and listen to the Visigoths sack Rome."

"Okay."

"There are things about the company's current position that you'd want to understand before the nineteenth. Things that would make Halvard look different to you. Not better. Different."

The coffee in my cup is too hot to drink and I hold it anyway. My hands want something to do.

"What things?" I say.

Ruth drinks the rest of her cortado. She puts the little glass down on the table and lines it up with the edge, the way Simone squared her napkin, the way people do when they're about to leave a room on purpose.

"Ask your husband," she says.

She puts on her coat. It's a long black puffer that makes her look like a very determined sleeping bag. She shakes my hand again, once, the railing test.

"I like you," she says. "I want you to know that. I liked the video. My daughter sent it to me from Oberlin. My daughter has never sent me anything about my work in her life." She pulls her gloves on. "I'll see you on the nineteenth."

She goes out into the cold, and I sit there at the tall table by the window of the bank that's not a bank anymore, holding a cup I can't drink.

*Ask your husband.*

I could. I could call him right now. He's on four, in the glass room, I can see it from here if I lean, the top of the Lindqvist building two blocks down over the roofs with its water tower and its painted sign. I could call and say *Ruth says there are things*, and he'd pick up, he always picks up, and he'd say *what things*, and I'd know from the half second before *what things* whether there were things.

I take my phone out. I look at his name. ADAM, and the photo I put on his contact in 2016 of him asleep in the truck at a rest stop outside Toledo with his mouth open and a bag of pretzels on his chest.

I don't call him.

I tell myself it's because he'll pitch me. That he'll get on the phone and do the voice and I'll lose an hour of a Wednesday listening to the Rotary version of whatever this is. That if there are things, they'll be on the agenda on the nineteenth, in a gray box, and I can read them there like everybody else.

That's not the reason. The reason is that if I ask him and he tells me, then it's a thing we know together. And I've gotten very used to not knowing things together.

I put the phone away and drive to Clyde Park.

Lolo's on early because Darnell's wife is having a baby. She's in the break room with Irene and a box of Marge's, a whiteboard marker in her hand, standing at the whiteboard by the time clock where Darnell keeps the count. *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 3,161.* Under that, somebody's drawn a little crane, worse than the marketing one.

"Hon," Lolo says when she sees me. "Irene and me did some math."

"Since when do you do math?"

"Since you started sitting in a room in the yard drawing on graph paper and not telling anybody why." She uncaps the marker. "Irene."

Irene steps up. Irene's fifty, from Bay City, a glazing lead for nine years. She talks maybe twelve sentences a day, and eleven of them are about glass. She takes the marker from Lolo and writes on the whiteboard in her square capitals.

*1 SHIFT: 1,800/YR.*
*2 SHIFTS: 3,400.*
*2 SHIFTS + SAT: 4,100.*

She underlines the last one twice and hands the marker back to Lolo.

"That's knee capacity," Lolo says. "That's how many knees we can weld if everybody works Saturdays. Darnell figured the paint booth can keep up if we run it two shifts. Benny says the forklift can keep up if Benny gets a raise."

"Benny always says that."

"Benny's always right." Lolo caps the marker. "So you've got three thousand people wanting a room with no post in it, and you've got a plant that can make four thousand a year if you ask nice. That's what's on your graph paper."

I look at the whiteboard. Four thousand one hundred. I had four thousand and twenty on my graph paper. Irene's number is better because Irene knows the glass line better than I do.

"It's not enough by itself," I say. "You need steel up front. Glass. You need money before the money."

"So get money."

"From where?"

"I don't know, hon. You're the unicorn." Lolo puts the cap on the marker and puts the marker behind her ear, like Benny's cigarette. "I'm a second shift lead. I make knees. You tell me how many knees."

I stand there and look at the board. Irene's numbers. Darnell's paint booth. Benny's forklift. The crew already doing my arithmetic in the break room, on their own, because they saw me drawing on graph paper and figured whatever I was drawing they'd better be in on.

*Nobody decides alone.*

Okay, Tato.

"I'm taking it to the board," I say. "On the nineteenth. A plan. Build them here. Every one."

Irene nods once, like that's settled, and goes back to the glazing line. Lolo looks at me over her glasses, then takes them off and lets them drop on their beaded chain.

"About time," she says. "Now go tell your husband."
