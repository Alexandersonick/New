## Chapter 4 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

"Tell me it's a prank," I say. "Tell me Darnell's got it behind the building on a forklift and he's going to bring it back around and everybody's going to laugh."

"It is not behind the building," Simone says. "I walked around the building."

It's just after six in the morning at gate C17 at O'Hare, and I'm standing at the window in my coat with my roller bag between my feet, watching a de-icing truck spray a regional jet orange. On the other end of the line, a hundred and eighty miles away, Simone Achterberg is standing in the northwest corner of the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building, where there is no longer a room. She has been there since five. She's told me this calmly, in order, as if she's reading out the manifest of a ship that sank.

"Describe it," I say.

"There are four steel bolt plates in the floor, and they have been cleaned. There is no debris. The window bay has been reinstalled and sealed, and you would not know from outside." A pause. "My boxes are stacked against the wall with the labels facing out. The ficus is on top."

"On top," I say.

"And someone watered it."

I laugh before I can stop it, a single bark at gate C17 in front of a family from Ohio in matching Buckeyes sweatshirts, and the dad turns around to see what's funny about the de-icing truck.

"I'm not laughing," I tell her.

"You laughed."

"I'm laughing at the ficus. The ficus is a nice touch. You have to admit the ficus is a nice touch."

"I do not have to admit that," Simone says, but there's something in her voice that might be a smile if you held it up to a light.

Here's the thing about me. I can sell anything to anybody, which is how I know the exact sound of a room deciding not to buy. I sold a backyard shed to a Canadian teachers' pension fund as the future of work, which, to be fair, it is. When a room decides against you it makes a kind of soft settling, like snow sliding off a roof, and I have learned that the first thing you do when a deal goes sideways is find out what's still true.

"What's still true?" I ask.

"Kurt Lindahl is on your flight. You drive him from the airport, and he tours the building at eleven. His diligence team has your founders' room on page nine of their brand assessment, with a photo, under the heading *Heritage Assets*." She lets that sit. "Gordon has called me twice. Ruth Baskin has not called yet. Elvin from the lobby says Pip told him she would mail you a dollar."

"She's mailing me a dollar?"

"For the lease, he said. He was not clear on the details, and he seemed to enjoy it."

I close my eyes. Behind them is the lease, two pages on the kitchen table in 2018, Pip typing with two fingers and her tongue between her teeth and me reading over her shoulder. *Terminable at will by Lessor.* I told her it was a terrible lease. I told her nobody would ever read it.

I've read a thousand leases in my life, and I never read hers.

"Is there video?" I ask.

"There is a video, forty seconds, filmed from a moving vehicle. It is on a Facebook group called *Grand Rapids Uncensored*. It has nine thousand views and a comment that says, I am quoting, *this city is so back*."

"Who posted it?"

"A man who drives a salt truck for Kent County. His profile picture is a walleye."

"Of course it's a walleye. Can we get it taken down?"

"It is a man's video of a public street at four in the morning. You could ask the walleye."

"Find out who he is."

"I know who he is. His name is on his profile." Her pen taps something, twice. "I will not call a salt truck driver before sunrise and ask him to delete the best thing that has happened to him all winter."

She stops, and then she says, "Adam."

Simone almost never says my name on the phone. She says it now in a particular tone, the tone she used in August walking me through the Nashville numbers at one in the morning, when I couldn't sleep and she couldn't either and she was the only person I knew who'd pick up.

"I know," I say.

"Halvard bought a story. The story has two founders and a garage and a room. You are going to walk their CEO onto a floor where one of the founders has removed the room with a crane in the night."

"I know, Simone."

"What do you want me to do?"

They're calling boarding, group one. A guy in a fleece vest pushes past me with the face of a man who has never let anyone board in front of him in his life.

What do I want her to do. That's the question I've been asking Simone for eight months, one way or another, and she always has an answer, in order, with numbers. I like that about her. I'm good with people and bad with silence, and Simone is good with numbers and fine with silence. She can sit in the glass conference room for an hour at the end of a long day with her notebook closed, not checking her phone. I can't do that. Pip can sit at a workbench for six hours and never say a word. I used to sit next to her while she did it, back in her father's garage, and talk the whole time, and she'd let me.

"Get facilities to cover the bolt plates," I say. "Put a rug down. Move your things into the glass room so it looks like a staging area. I'll tell Kurt it's being refurbished."

"Refurbished," Simone says, slowly, as if it's a word in a language she doesn't speak.

"Scheduled refurbishment, heritage asset maintenance, it's on page nine. He'll love it. He'll think we're taking care of our heritage."

"And when he sees the video?"

"He's not going to see a video with nine thousand views on a Facebook group with a walleye."

"It had six thousand when I picked up the phone," Simone says.

On the plane, Kurt Lindahl has the window, of course. He folds himself into it, long and lean, and hands his camel coat up to the flight attendant without looking, as though somebody has always been there to take it. The man across the aisle asks him what Halvard makes. "Eleven furniture brands you've heard of," Kurt tells him, "and nine you've sat on without knowing it." He's charming the way I'm charming, which is to say on purpose, and we both know it, and that makes us get along like two magicians at a party.

"Big day," he says, when I sit down. "I'm excited to see the room. Your deck on that room is my favorite deck in the deal. Two kids, a garage, a dream. It's going to test through the roof."

"It's a special room," I say.

"I want to stand in it. My comms team wants a photo of you and me standing in it, shaking hands. We'll hold the photo till close, obviously."

"Obviously."

"Is Pip going to be there?"

There it is. Kurt has asked about Pip in every meeting since October, sincerely, which is worse than slyly, because Pip owns thirty-three percent of my company and the deal needs seventy-five, and I haven't told her there's a deal. I've told her there's a *process*. I've sent her the Monday decks. She doesn't open the Monday decks. I know she doesn't, because the deck software tells me who opens what, and since the spring before last her name has sat in the *not viewed* column like a headstone.

That last part is a pitch. Strike it.

I haven't told her because if I tell her, she'll say no, and if she says no, I have to tell her why we need to sell, and if I tell her why we need to sell, it's real.

"Pip's at the plant today," I say. "Pip's always at the plant. She's our heart. She's the hands."

"I'd love to meet the hands," Kurt says.

"You will. Absolutely. She's going to love you."

He nods and takes a folder out of his bag, an actual paper folder, which I respect in a man his age. "Help me with Nashville while I have you. My ops people say your Tennessee line can't build the corner."

"Nashville builds the Lite."

"The one with the post."

"A small post," I say. It's the line we give the phones.

"Don't apologize for the post, Adam. The post is the plan." He taps the folder with one finger. "The room with no post is what we put in the ads. The room with the post is what we put in the backyards. Every brand I own works that way, and every one of them makes money."

I laugh, because he wants me to, and look out past him at the wing so he can't tell whether I mean it.

Somewhere over Lake Michigan, the flight attendant comes by with the coffee cart, and Kurt asks for hot water with lemon, and I ask for coffee, black, and I drink it and look at my phone in airplane mode where I can't get any messages, and I think: this is the last quiet I'm going to have today.

The last good meetings Pip and I had in that room were coffee at seven, which was less a meeting than a rule, written on the inside of the door, number three, in her handwriting. For six years in the Lindqvist building, every working day I was in town, we sat in that room at seven in the morning, her at her desk and me in the other chair with my feet on the radiator, and we decided the day, just us, before anybody else came up the elevator and the company woke up.

I don't think about how it ended. I'm a forward-looking guy. It's in my bio.

We land a couple of minutes early in blowing snow. My phone comes back on in the jet bridge and won't stop buzzing: Gordon, three times, and Ruth Baskin, once, a voicemail I won't be listening to in front of Kurt. Simone, a text: *Rug down. Staging area done. Video at 41k. It has been shared by the Grand Rapids Ledger.* And under that, a minute later: *Second video now, from the street, a different angle and much better quality. It is very beautiful.*

Very beautiful. Simone doesn't use adjectives she can't defend.

I put Kurt in the car with the heat on full and tell him I need two minutes to call the office about the tour. I stand outside the car in the snow with my back to him, and I don't call the office. I open the second video.

It's from the sidewalk, low and steady, Monroe Avenue at night with the snow coming down through the streetlights. And there, coming out of the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building, slow, turning a little, ten by twelve, is our room. Her room. It swings out into the air, and for a second it hangs there by itself, and the light from the streetlight goes straight through the glass corner where there's no post and comes out the other side, and somebody off-camera says, very quietly, *pretty*, and somebody else says *don't*.

I know that second voice. I'd know it in a stadium.

I play it three times. I'm standing in the snow at the Gerald R. Ford International Airport in my travel suit with Kurt Lindahl waiting in a warm car, and I play my wife's room flying out of my building three times, and the third time, for no reason I can explain to anyone, I have to put my hand on the roof of the car.

Then I get in and tell Kurt we're going to swing by the plant first.

"The plant?" he says, delighted. "Love it. The hands."

"The hands," I say.

I call Simone from the highway, on speaker, while Kurt pretends to read email.

"The rug," I say. "At the bolt plates. Take it up."

"You told me to put it down."

"I know I did. Take it up and leave the plates."

There's a pause long enough for her to write something down. I can hear the pen.

"Why?" she says.

I don't have an answer I can say with Kurt Lindahl two feet away pretending to read email. The true one is something like *because I don't want anybody standing there*, which isn't a business reason, and which I didn't know was true until I said *take it up*.

"Heritage," I say. "Let them see it. It's part of the story."

"That is not why," Simone says, and hangs up.

Kurt doesn't look up from his phone. "Rug situation?"

"Rug situation."

"I had a rug situation in Milwaukee once," he says. "We bought the company anyway." He goes back to his email with a little smile, as if he's already read the last page of this one.

The Clyde Park plant is twenty minutes from the airport if you drive the way I drive. I drive the way I drive. Kurt holds the handle above his window and keeps saying *love it* at intervals, and means about half of them. We go down Clyde Park Avenue past the tire place and the taqueria and the Reformed church with the sign that says *GOD ANSWERS KNEE-MAIL*, and I turn in at our gate, and I see it.

It's sitting in the yard by the fence on a gravel pad, up on cribbing, the snow already settling on its roof, exactly where the old Eight prototype used to sit before we scrapped it. The light's on inside.

My wife is sitting in it at her desk, with a welding sample in the vise in front of her.

She's filing it like it's any Tuesday in the world.
