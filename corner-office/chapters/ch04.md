## Chapter 4 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

"Tell me it's a prank," I say. "Tell me Darnell's got it behind the building on a forklift and he's going to bring it back around and everybody's going to laugh."

"It is not behind the building," Simone says. "I walked around the building."

It's ten after six in the morning at gate C17 at O'Hare, and I'm standing at the window in my coat with my roller bag between my feet, watching a de-icing truck spray a regional jet orange. On the other end of the line, a hundred and eighty miles away, Simone Achterberg is standing in the northwest corner of the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building, where there is no longer a room. She has been there since five. She's told me this calmly, in order, the way she tells me everything, like a person reading the manifest of a ship that sank.

"Describe it," I say.

"There are four steel bolt plates in the floor. They have been cleaned. There's no debris. The window bay has been reinstalled and sealed. You would not know from outside." A pause. "My boxes are stacked against the wall. Labels facing out. The ficus is on top."

"On top."

"And someone watered it."

I laugh. I don't mean to. It comes out of me at gate C17 in front of a family from Ohio in matching Buckeyes sweatshirts, a single bark, and the dad turns around to see what's funny about the de-icing truck.

"Strike that," I say. "I'm not laughing."

"You laughed."

"I'm laughing at the ficus. The ficus is a nice touch. You have to admit the ficus is a nice touch."

"I do not have to admit that," Simone says, but there's something in her voice that might be a smile if you held it up to a light.

Here's the thing about me. I can sell anything to anybody. That's not a brag, it's a job description. I sold a backyard shed to a Canadian teachers' pension fund. I sold it to them as the future of work, which, to be fair, it is. I have stood in front of rooms of people who didn't want to buy and I have heard the exact sound a room makes when it decides not to, a kind of soft settling, like snow sliding off a roof. And I have learned that the first thing you do when a deal goes sideways is find out what's still true.

"What's still true?" I ask.

"Kurt Lindahl lands with you at seven-forty. You drive him from the airport. He tours the building at eleven. His diligence team has your founders' room on page nine of their brand assessment, with a photo, under the heading *Heritage Assets*." She lets that sit. "Gordon has called me twice. Ruth Baskin has not called yet. Elvin from the lobby says Pip told him she would mail you a dollar."

"A dollar."

"For the lease. He was not clear. He seemed to enjoy it."

I close my eyes. Behind them is the lease, two pages on the kitchen table in 2018, Pip typing with two fingers and her tongue in the corner of her mouth and me reading over her shoulder. *Terminable at will by Lessor.* I told her it was a terrible lease. I told her nobody would ever read it.

I'm a man who has read a lot of leases. I didn't read hers.

"Is there video?" I ask.

"There is a video. Forty seconds, filmed from a moving vehicle. It's on a Facebook group called *Grand Rapids Uncensored*. It has nine thousand views and a comment that says, I am quoting, *this city is so back*."

"Who posted it?"

"A man who drives a salt truck for Kent County. His profile picture is a walleye."

"Of course it is."

"Adam."

Simone almost never says my name on the phone. She says it now in a particular tone, the tone she used the first time she walked me through the Nashville numbers in August, at one in the morning, when I couldn't sleep and she couldn't either and she was the only person I knew who'd pick up.

"I know," I say.

"Halvard bought a story. The story has two founders and a garage and a room. You are going to walk their CEO onto a floor where one of the founders has removed the room with a crane in the night."

"I know."

"What do you want me to do?"

They're calling boarding. Group one. A guy in a fleece vest pushes past me with the face of a man who has never let anyone board in front of him in his life.

What do I want her to do. That's the question I've been asking Simone for eight months, one way or another, and she always has an answer, in order, with numbers. I like that about her. I'm good with people and bad with silence, and Simone is good with numbers and fine with silence. She can sit in a quiet room for an hour. I've watched her do it, at the end of a long day in the glass conference room, just sitting with her notebook closed, not checking her phone. I can't do that. Pip can do that. Pip can sit at a workbench for six hours and never say a word. I used to sit next to her while she did it, a long time ago, and talk the whole time, and she'd let me.

"Get facilities to cover the bolt plates," I say. "Put a rug down. Move your boxes into the glass room so it looks like a staging area. I'll tell Kurt it's being refurbished."

"Refurbished."

"Scheduled refurbishment. Heritage asset maintenance. It's on page nine, he'll love it, he'll think we're taking care of our heritage."

"And when he sees the video?"

"He's not going to see a video with nine thousand views on a Facebook group with a walleye."

"It had six thousand when I picked up the phone," Simone says.

On the plane, Kurt Lindahl has the window. Of course he has the window. Kurt is fifty, Minneapolis, tall in a lean, smooth, expensive way, like a man who was born in a good coat. He runs Halvard Group, which owns eleven furniture brands you've heard of and nine you've sat on without knowing it. He's charming. He's charming the way I'm charming, which is to say on purpose, and we both know it, and that makes us get along the way two magicians get along at a party.

"Big day," he says, when I sit down. "I'm excited to see the room. Your deck on that room is my favorite deck in the deal. Two kids, a garage, a dream. It's going to test through the roof."

"It's a special room," I say.

"I want to stand in it. My comms team wants a photo of you and me standing in it, shaking hands. We'll hold the photo till close, obviously."

"Obviously."

"Is Pip going to be there?"

There it is. Kurt has asked about Pip in every meeting since October. Not in a sly way. In a sincere way, which is worse, because Pip owns thirty-three percent of my company and the deal needs seventy-five, and I haven't told her there's a deal. I've told her there's a *process*. I've sent her the Monday decks. She doesn't open the Monday decks. I know she doesn't, because the deck software tells me who opens what, and for two years her name has sat in the *not viewed* column like a headstone.

That's a pitch. That last part. Strike it.

I haven't told her because if I tell her, she'll say no, and if she says no, I have to tell her why we need to sell, and if I tell her why we need to sell, it's real.

"Pip's at the plant today," I say. "Pip's always at the plant. She's our heart. She's the hands."

"I'd love to meet the hands," Kurt says.

"You will. Absolutely. She's going to love you."

Somewhere over Lake Michigan, the flight attendant comes by with the coffee cart, and Kurt asks for hot water with lemon, and I ask for coffee, black, and I drink it and look at my phone in airplane mode where I can't get any messages, and I think: this is the last quiet I'm going to have today.

The last good meeting Pip and I had in that room was coffee at seven. Not a meeting, technically. Coffee at seven was a rule, written on the inside of the door, number three, in her handwriting. For four years in the Lindqvist building, every working day I was in town, we sat in that room at seven in the morning, her at her desk and me in the other chair with my feet on the radiator, and we decided the day. Just us. Before anybody else came up the elevator. Before the company woke up.

I don't think about how it ended. I'm a forward-looking guy. It's in my bio.

We land at seven-thirty-eight in blowing snow. My phone comes back on in the jet bridge and goes off like a slot machine. Gordon, three times. Ruth Baskin, once, a voicemail I won't be listening to in front of Kurt. Simone, a text: *Rug down. Boxes moved. Video at 41k. It has been shared by the Grand Rapids Ledger.* And under that, a minute later: *Second video now. Different angle. Much better quality. From the street. It is very beautiful.*

Very beautiful. Simone doesn't use adjectives she can't defend.

I put Kurt in the car with the heat on full and tell him I need two minutes to call the office about the tour. I stand outside the car in the snow with my back to him. I don't call the office. I open the second video.

It's from the sidewalk, low, the camera steady. Monroe Avenue at night. Snow. The streetlights. And there, coming out of the fourth floor of the Lindqvist building, slow, turning a little, ten by twelve, is our room. Her room. It swings out into the air, and for a second it hangs there by itself, and the light from the streetlight goes straight through the glass corner where there's no post and comes out the other side, and somebody off-camera says, very quietly, *pretty*, and somebody else says *don't*.

I know that second voice. I'd know it in a stadium.

I watch it three times. I'm standing in the snow at the Gerald R. Ford International Airport in my flight suit with Kurt Lindahl waiting in a warm car and I watch my wife's room fly out of my building three times, and the third time, for no reason I can explain to anyone, I have to put my hand on the roof of the car.

Then I get in and tell Kurt we're going to swing by the plant first.

"The plant?" he says, delighted. "Love it. The hands."

"The hands," I say.

I call Simone from the highway, on speaker. Kurt is pretending to read email.

"The rug," I say. "At the bolt plates. Take it up."

"You told me to put it down."

"I know. Take it up. Leave the plates."

A pause. A long one. She's writing something down. I can hear the pen.

"Why?" she says.

I don't have an answer I can say with Kurt Lindahl two feet away pretending to read email. The real one is something like *because I don't want anybody standing there*, which isn't a business reason, and which I didn't know was true until I said *take it up*.

"Heritage," I say. "Let them see it. It's part of the story."

"That is not why," Simone says, and hangs up.

The Clyde Park plant is twenty minutes from the airport if you drive the way I drive. I drive the way I drive. Kurt holds the handle above his window and keeps saying *love it* at intervals, like a man on a rollercoaster who's decided to be a good sport. We go down Clyde Park Avenue past the tire place and the taqueria and the Reformed church with the sign that says *GOD ANSWERS KNEE-MAIL*, and I turn in at our gate, and I see it.

It's sitting in the yard by the fence on a gravel pad, up on cribbing, the snow already settling on its roof. Exactly where the old Eight prototype used to sit before we scrapped it. The light's on inside.

My wife is sitting in it at her desk, with a welding sample in the vise in front of her.

She's filing it like it's any Tuesday in the world.
