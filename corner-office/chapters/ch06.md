## Chapter 6 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

We have two coffee makers.

Mine is a stovetop moka pot, aluminum, eight-sided, dented on one shoulder from the time my brother Marek threw it at my brother Joe in 1999. My mother gave it to me when I moved into my first apartment, along with a crucifix and a can of Raid. His is an Italian espresso machine the size of a small car engine, with a pressure gauge and a steam wand and a little brass eagle on top, which a man from his old fraternity sent him when we closed the Series C. It cost more than my first truck. It makes very good coffee. I've used it twice.

They sit at opposite ends of the kitchen counter in our house on Heritage Hill, like two cats who've agreed to share a house but not a room.

At ten-forty that night I'm standing at my end, washing a pan, when his headlights come up the drive.

The house is an 1890s brick Italianate on College Avenue with eleven-foot ceilings and radiators that knock like they want to be let in. We bought it in 2019 to fix up. We fixed up the kitchen. The rest of the house is still somewhat 1890s. There's a room on the second floor we call the room with the ladder, because there's been a ladder in it since 2020. Adam says we'll get to it. He says it the way you'd say it about a country you're going to visit when you retire.

He comes in through the back with his bag and his phone and the cold, and stands in the kitchen doorway in his coat.

"Hi."

"Hi."

"Did you eat?"

"Yes," I say. I had half a sleeve of saltines in the truck in the plant lot. Saltines are food. They're the food of the sick and the righteous.

"I didn't." He puts the bag down. "Is there—"

"There's pierogi in the freezer. My mother's. Walt gets half."

"Why does Walt get half?"

"Walt's invoice."

He opens the freezer and stands there in the light of it, looking at the bags of pierogi labeled in my mother's handwriting, *ruskie* and *kapusta* and *śliwki — FOR PIP ONLY*. He takes a bag of ruskie. He doesn't take the plum ones. Eleven years married, he still knows which ones are mine.

I finish the pan. He puts water on to boil. We stand at our two ends of the counter.

"Gordon called me six times today," he says to the pot.

"How's his stroke?"

"Ongoing." He opens the bag. "The site went down at three. Too much traffic. It came back at three-twenty and went down again. Tavi stayed and helped the web guys. He's a good kid."

"He's a great kid."

"Do you want to know how many?"

"How many what?"

"Deposits." He's looking at the water. "Since noon. People going to the site, clicking *reserve*, putting down ten percent."

"No."

"Four hundred and eleven."

I dry my hands. I dry them very carefully.

"For which?" I say.

"The Corner." He drops the pierogi in, one at a time, gentle, the way my mother does it. He learned that from my mother. He used to stand in her kitchen on Sundays and watch her like it was a seminar. "Your Corner. The real one. Not the Lite. They're writing in the notes field. *The one from the video. The one with no post.* Somebody in Duluth wrote *the one that can leave*."

The water comes back to a boil. The pierogi rise to the top one by one and float there, and neither of us says anything, and I look at the pierogi because I can't look at him.

"Kurt called it a demand signal," he says.

"Of course he did."

"He's thrilled. He thinks it's the best thing that could've happened to the brand. He wants to get you in the photo."

"I'm not getting in the photo."

"I know."

"I won't *be* in the photo, Adam, I'm going to be a no in the photo."

"I *know*, Pip." He turns around. In the kitchen light he looks tired the way he looked tired when we were thirty and slept on the floor of the garage two nights a week. Except then the tiredness was a thing we were doing together. "I know. Ruth called. The nineteenth is on. You're agenda item four."

"What are items one through three?"

"Minutes. Q4 numbers. Halvard."

"I'm after Halvard."

"You're after Halvard."

"That's a good spot," I say. "Everybody'll be awake."

He scoops the pierogi out with a slotted spoon onto a plate and stands there with the plate and doesn't sit down. We have a table. We have six chairs around it, and on four of them are boxes of samples and a pile of mail and a bicycle helmet nobody wears. He eats standing up at his end of the counter. I lean on mine. The radiator under the window knocks twice and goes quiet.

"You did a good job," he says. "Last night. The lift. Walt said you checked every ratchet."

"Walt talked to you?"

"Walt sent me an invoice. With a note."

"What did the note say?"

"It said *she checked every ratchet*." He eats a pierogi. "And it said he wants pierogi. It said that twice."

"He's getting them."

"I told him I'd pay for it."

"I'm paying for it."

"Pip, the city's going to fine the company—"

"The company's not paying for it. *I'm* paying for it. It's my room."

"I know it's your room!"

He puts his fork down. We look at each other across twelve feet of quartz counter that we picked out together in 2019 at a stone yard in Wyoming, standing in a warehouse for two hours with our hands flat on slabs, arguing about veining. I wanted the one with no veining. He wanted the one that looked like a river. We got the river.

"I'm going to bed," I say.

"Okay."

"Goodnight."

"Night."

I go up. I go up the front stairs, the good ones with the walnut banister, past the room with the ladder, to our bedroom at the back of the house, which is my bedroom. He sleeps in the room across the hall. He's slept there since the spring before last, because of the flights. That's what we said. He gets in at one, he leaves at five, he doesn't want to wake me. It's very considerate. Everybody in my family thinks it's very considerate. My mother says *at least he's considerate* the way you'd say *at least the car still runs*.

I brush my teeth. I get into bed. I lie on my back in the dark with the radiator ticking and look at the ceiling where there's a water stain shaped a bit like Lake Michigan, if Lake Michigan had been in a bar fight.

At twelve fifty I hear him come up. The good stairs, the creak on the ninth step, the door across the hall. The shower. The door again.

At one-ten, his voice. Low, through the wall, through two layers of 1890s plaster and a closet full of my winter coats. I can't make out words. I can make out the shape of it, the rhythm, the long easy run of a man telling a story to somebody who wants to hear it. He used to tell me stories like that, in bed, in the garage, in the cab of the truck on the way to a delivery in Kalamazoo, whole stories with voices, about investors and flight attendants and a man at the DMV. I'd fall asleep in the middle and he'd keep going.

Then he laughs. Not the Rotary laugh. The real one, the one that comes out of him like a dog getting out of a car. I haven't heard that laugh in a long time. I know exactly how long. I could tell you to the day.

I don't know for certain who's on the other end. I could get up and stand in the hall. I could put my ear against the door like a kid at Christmas. I don't. I lie there and do the arithmetic instead, which is the only thing I've ever been any good at. If he laughs like that with her at one in the morning, and he hasn't laughed like that with me in two years, then the number of people he laughs like that with is one, and it isn't me.

*You barely use it*, I think. And I think, *neither do you*.

At one-forty the voice stops. The light under his door goes out.

I get up. I take my pillow and the afghan my grandmother crocheted out of every color of yarn she could find at Goodwill in 1981, and I go down the good stairs.

The couch in the front room has the thickest wall in the house. I lie down on it and put the pillow over my head.
