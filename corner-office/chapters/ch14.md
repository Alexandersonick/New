## Chapter 14 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Clyde Park Lanes has twenty-four lanes, a bar called the Gutter that serves Stroh's in a can and a fish fry on Fridays that people drive in for from Hudsonville, and carpet so old the pattern has worn down to a theory. On Monday nights it belongs to the West Michigan Industrial League: tool-and-die shops, a commercial bakery, two plumbing supply houses, a team of ER nurses from Blodgett who call themselves the Bedpans and are terrifying, and on lane seventeen, the Spare Parts.

Lolo's been captain since 2003. The roster has changed over the years as people retired, moved to Florida, died, or got knees. Right now it's Lolo, Irene, Benny, Darnell when his wife lets him, and me when I can't think of a reason not to. Tonight Darnell can't come because his wife had the baby at four in the afternoon. So we've got a fifth.

"You're up, European," Lolo says.

Tavi stands at the top of the approach in a pair of rented shoes, red and blue, holding a ten-pound ball the color of a grape Popsicle with both hands against his chest, like it's a baby someone's handed him at a christening.

"I've never done this," he says.

"We know, hon," says Lolo. "We can tell from here."

"Do I throw it?"

"You roll it."

"Like a—" He makes a vague gesture.

"Like you're mad at it," says Benny, from the scorer's table, where he has a can of Stroh's and a basket of fries and the dignity of a man who's bowled a two-eighty in this building and will tell you the date. "Like it owes you money."

Tavi walks up to the line, very serious, and swings the ball back, and lets go of it at the top of the swing, so that it goes up instead of forward, and comes down with a sound like a dropped dresser, and rolls, slowly, with great deliberation, into the left gutter, where it continues on its way with total peace, like a ball that's made its choice.

The Bedpans on lane sixteen applaud.

"That's okay," says Irene. Irene almost never says anything at bowling. She bowls like she glazes, every throw the same, a one-sixty-five average for nine years without one strike or one gutter, as far as anybody can remember. "That was good form."

"It went in the gutter."

"The form was good. The ball made a decision."

Tavi sits down next to me on the molded plastic bench. He's pink to the ears. He's having the best night of his life. I can tell because he hasn't looked at his phone once, which for Tavi is like a normal person not breathing.

"Three thousand eight hundred and two," he says to me, low.

"I thought you weren't looking at your phone."

"I checked in the bathroom." He looks at me sideways. "Are you nervous? About tomorrow?"

"I'm never nervous."

"You're holding your shoe."

I look down. I'm holding my bowling shoe in my lap, the left one, with both hands, the way he was holding the ball. I put it on.

"I'm presenting a plan," I say. "To the board. That's all."

"Does Adam know about the plan?"

Lolo, who's been writing *Tavi — 0* on the score sheet with the satisfaction of a woman carving a headstone, looks up.

"Answer the kid," she says.

"Adam's seen the plan."

"Seen it or been asked about it?"

"He saw it Friday. In the room. He said it was clean."

"Did you tell him you were taking it to the board?"

I don't answer. I tie my shoe. I tie it very carefully, a double knot, the way my father taught me to tie them before we went out on the floor at Lindqvist, because a loose lace by a press brake will take your foot.

"Hon," Lolo says.

"I'll tell him at the meeting."

"That's not telling him. That's an ambush with a PowerPoint."

"I don't have a PowerPoint. I have a whiteboard photo."

"It's an ambush with a whiteboard photo," Lolo says, and hands Irene the ball, and Irene goes up and throws her one-sixty-five frame, a seven and a two, same as always, and comes back and sits down, and Lolo stands up and points at me with her pencil. "You're up. Throw it like you're mad at it."

I'm mad at it. I throw a strike. It doesn't help.

We bowl three games. Benny bowls a two-ten and tells us about the two-eighty. Irene bowls exactly one-sixty-five three times in a row and doesn't seem to notice. Tavi gets his first pin in the second frame of the second game, the ten pin, alone, wobbling, and when it falls over he turns around with both arms in the air, and the Bedpans on sixteen give him a standing ovation, and one of them, a big redheaded ER nurse named Colleen, comes over and kisses him on the forehead. Tavi goes so red he looks like a stop sign.

Between the second and third game, Benny goes to the Gutter for another round, and Irene goes to call her sister in Bay City, and Tavi goes to the bathroom to check his phone again, and it's just Lolo and me on the plastic bench at lane seventeen, watching the Bedpans.

"Darnell's baby's a girl," Lolo says. "Seven pounds four. They named her Philippa."

I turn and look at her.

"I'm kidding, hon. They named her Nevaeh. It's heaven backwards. Darnell's wife likes it." She drinks her Stroh's. "Your face, though."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is like your mother when the band plays the wrong polka."

"You keep saying that."

"You keep making it." She sets the can down on the scorer's table. She takes her glasses off and lets them drop on their chain. That's the signal. My father used to say that when Lolo takes her glasses off you better put yours on, because something's about to get looked at. "Can I ask you something?"

"You're going to anyway."

"Coffee at seven."

I don't say anything. On sixteen, Colleen throws a strike and does a little dance with her hands in the air.

"Your dad told me about it," Lolo says. "Years ago. He thought it was the cutest thing. His girl and her fella, up in that room every morning at seven, deciding the whole day. He used to say it like it was a TV show he watched."

"It was a long time ago."

"It was two years ago." She looks at me. "It stopped after Nashville, right? He did that thing at the all-hands, with the slide. And then it stopped."

"Then it stopped," I agree.

"Who stopped first?"

"He did."

It comes out fast. Too fast. It comes out like a thing I've been saying to myself for two years in the truck on the way to work, and I hear how smooth it is, like a stair you've walked down so many times it's worn in the middle.

Lolo doesn't say anything. She picks up the pencil and taps it on the score sheet. Tap, tap. On sixteen, the Bedpans are high-fiving.

"You sure?" she says.

"Yes."

"'Cause I remember that winter," Lolo says. "Right after Nashville. I remember because I was running a double for three weeks while Dave Wierenga was out with his gallbladder. I'd come off second shift and stay for first. And every morning that winter, you were in the plant by six-thirty."

"I'm always early."

"Not before eight, you weren't. Never in your life. You were at the Lindqvist building every morning at seven having coffee with your husband, and then you'd come out to Clyde Park at eight-thirty with a travel mug, and Benny would say *here comes the boss*." She taps the pencil again. "And then for three weeks you were at your bench at six-thirty. Every day. I know 'cause I was there. You'd come in and sit down and you wouldn't say anything to anybody, and you'd file things."

I look at the lane.

"So I'm thinking," Lolo says, gently, which is the worst way Lolo says anything, "if you were here at six-thirty, hon, you weren't there at seven. So how would you know if he stopped?"

There's a sound in my ears like the moment between a pin wobbling and falling over. I don't say anything. I don't have anything. I've got two years of a story I tell myself in the truck, smooth as a worn stair, and one sentence from a sixty-three-year-old woman in a bowling shirt that just put a crack in it.

"He stopped," I say. "He was in Nashville. He was always in Nashville that spring, he was on planes—"

"That spring, sure. I'm asking about the three weeks."

"I don't—" I stop.

*I don't know.* That's what I was going to say. I don't know, because I wasn't there, because I was here at six-thirty filing things, and the first morning I didn't go up, I told myself it was one morning, a protest, a message he'd get, and then the second morning was easier, and by the end of the week it was the way things were. And somewhere in there I decided he'd stopped too. I decided it the way you decide a noise in the house is the furnace. I never went to check.

Benny comes back from the Gutter with four cans of Stroh's in his hands, pressed together, and a fresh basket of fries balanced on top, and looks at us.

"What'd I miss?" he says.

"Nothing," says Lolo. She puts her glasses back on. "Pip's up."

I get up. I pick up my ball, a fourteen-pound black one with my initials engraved in it that my brothers bought me for my thirtieth birthday as a joke, because they thought I'd never use it. I walk to the line. I stand there with the ball against my chest the way Tavi held his, and I look down the lane at the ten white pins standing in their triangle under the lights, and the arrows on the boards, and my hands are shaking.

My hands don't shake. I've welded upside down in a crawlspace in Holland in August. My hands don't shake.

I throw it. It goes straight into the right gutter.

Behind me, very quietly, Tavi says, "Good form."
