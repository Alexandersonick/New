## Chapter 30 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The second desk comes across the yard at six-forty on the first Monday in March on Darnell's pallet jack, wrapped in the moving blanket, with my husband walking beside it holding one corner like a pallbearer.

It's a door. I want that on the record. A six-panel fir door from a house in Eastown that somebody gutted in 2013, which I bought at the Habitat ReStore on Division for eleven dollars and carried home in my father's Buick with the trunk tied shut with a bungee cord. I laid it across two sawhorses I welded out of scrap angle in the garage on Garfield Avenue, and that was Adam's desk for four years, until we moved into the Lindqvist building and bought real furniture, and the door went into the basement, and then into the room with the ladder, under the tax returns.

He found it there the Saturday after the vote. I came home from the plant and he was in the room with the ladder in an old Western Michigan sweatshirt with a sander in his hand and fir dust in his hair, and the tax returns in a pile in the hall, and the ladder, finally, gone.

"I'm refinishing it," he said.

"You don't know how to refinish things."

"I watched a video."

"You watched one video?"

"I watched four videos. One of them was a man in Vermont with a beard." He held up the sander. "He said to go with the grain."

He did not go with the grain. You can tell. There are swirl marks all over the top in the low light like somebody skated on it. He spent three weekends on it. He asked me twice how to do the edges and I told him, and he did it his own way anyway, and the edges are wrong, and it's the best piece of furniture in the building.

"Watch the step," I tell him now, from the door of the room. "Darnell, watch the—"

"I see the step," says Darnell, who's been moving things over that step for six weeks.

They get it inside. It just fits. Ten by twelve doesn't leave a lot of room for a second desk, and I measured it four times, and so did Adam, separately, and then we measured it together at the kitchen counter on a Sunday night, both of us at my end with a tape measure and a pencil and a glass of wine, arguing about whether to set it against the north wall or push it against mine.

Against mine. We agreed. It took two hours.

Darnell and Adam set the door on its sawhorses, which I sandblasted and repainted, the only part of this I was allowed to touch. They slide it across the floor until its long edge meets the long edge of my desk, the one I built in 2014 out of white oak my father found in a barn in Sparta, and the two of them come together with a small wooden sound, like a knock.

Two desks. A room ten by twelve. No post in the corner.

Darnell stands back and looks at it with his hands on his hips. He's been sleeping maybe four hours a night since Nevaeh came home. He looks like a man made of coffee.

"That's ugly," he says, admiring it. "That's a real ugly desk, boss."

"It's a door, Darnell."

"I know. It's a beautiful door. It's an ugly desk."

"Thank you, Darnell," says Adam.

"Anytime." Darnell grabs the pallet jack. At the door, he stops. "Hey. Kiana's asking for you. Lolo made her cry again."

"Lolo made her cry?"

"Lolo makes everybody cry the first week. It's a sign of respect." He shrugs. "Kiana told her to go to Florida already. Lolo laughed so hard she had to sit down on a bucket. They're best friends now. It's terrifying."

He goes out across the yard, pushing the jack, whistling.

Twenty-three people came up from Tennessee. Twenty-two of them are still here. They're living in a motel on 28th Street that Gordon got a rate on and in two rented duplexes off Clyde Park that Benny's cousin owns, and they're on second shift learning the knee from Lolo, who has never trained anybody in her life without making them cry, and who is paid, as she reminds everyone, time and a half. Kiana's the fastest. Lolo says she welds like she's mad at it. Lolo says that's a compliment. Kiana's son Jaylen is in kindergarten at the elementary school on Burton Street and has told his whole class that his mother builds the rooms that can leave.

Lolo still has her bucket. She still wants Florida. She has the trust now too, five percent of the company spread across a hundred and sixty-three people, which isn't eighteen months of base pay and isn't a lanai yet. She told me last week, sitting on her bucket at the end of second shift, that she's going in eighteen months anyway, trust or no trust, the day after Kiana can run the knee line alone. *I'm not waiting for you to make me rich, hon. I'm waiting for that girl to make me unnecessary.*

I didn't argue. I wrote it on the whiteboard in the break room, under the vote. *LOLO: 18 MONTHS. KIANA: ?* Darnell keeps a count. It's at seventeen months and three weeks.

Adam stands in the room looking at his desk.

He's in a white shirt. It's ironed. Badly. There's a crease down the left sleeve that's in the wrong place entirely, like a fold in a road map. He's been ironing his own shirts for three weeks at the board in the kitchen with the radio on, and every morning he holds one up for me to look at and I say *better* and it isn't, and he knows it isn't, and he wears it anyway.

"It fits," he says.

"I measured it."

"We measured it."

"We measured it," I agree.

He runs his hand along the seam where his desk meets mine. His swirl marks and my white oak. The edge of the door is a quarter inch higher than the edge of my desk, because his sawhorses are a quarter inch taller, because I welded them in 2014 for a man who sat up straighter than I did. I never fixed it. I'm not going to.

"There's a step," he says, feeling it.

"There's always a step."

"Should I shim it?"

"Leave it," I say. "It's structural."

He looks at me. Then he laughs, short and surprised, the real one, and leans against his ugly desk in his badly ironed shirt, and I lean against mine, and the yard comes up gray outside the glass corner, and neither of us says anything for a while.

My phone buzzes on the desk. It's a photo from a number with a 312 area code. A room with brick walls and tall windows over a river I don't recognize. Chicago. Bookshelves full. A couch. A framed print of a lighthouse on the wall, hung, straight, on a hook. And on the floor, flattened and stacked and tied with twine, fourteen cardboard boxes.

Under it: *Unpacked. All fourteen. Haverhill gave me a door that closes. It does not have a corner. I find I do not miss the corner. — S.*

And then, a second later: *Tell him I said the shirt is wrong.*

I show it to Adam. He reads it twice. He looks down at his left sleeve, at the crease in the wrong place, and then back at the phone, and he doesn't say anything for a moment.

"How does she know about the shirt?" he says.

"Tavi sends her photos."

"Tavi sends *everybody* photos."

"Tavi's lonely. He needs someone to tell." I take the phone back. I type: *He knows. He's wearing it anyway.* I send it.

Tavi's still here. Ruth tried to move him back downtown when his internship ended, and he asked if he could stay at Clyde Park instead, on the floor, and Ruth asked me, and I asked him why, and he said, *Because out here people say what they're going to do before they do it, and I like knowing.* He's on the payroll now. He runs the dashboard. The dots are at nine thousand four hundred and something. He doesn't text me the number every morning anymore. He writes it on the whiteboard, under the vote, under Lolo's count, in green.

The corner on four at the Lindqvist building is still empty. The four bolt plates are still in the maple floor, cleaned, bright. Ruth wanted to put a plaque there. *Heritage asset site*, she said, in the public-radio voice. I said no plaque. She said, *A small one?* I said, *Ruth*. She said, *Fine*. Somebody keeps putting the orange cone back on the plates every morning so nobody trips. Nobody admits it's them. I think it's Elvin. Adam thinks it's Gordon. Gordon says he doesn't know what we're talking about and goes red to the ears.

At six fifty-five, Adam takes his paper cup with the brass eagle on the sleeve and sits down in the chair by the radiator. He puts his feet up, crossed at the ankle. The chair creaks. The room settles its quarter inch.

I pour the moka pot into the Polish Falcons mug and sit down at my desk. My feet flat on the floor.

The yard light clicks off on its timer. The real light comes in through the glass corner, low and gray and then gold, the way it does in March when the sun comes up over the maples on Clyde Park. It lies across both desks, mine and his, in one long bar, over the seam.

"Okay," he says. "Before you start. I'm going to tell you something before I do it."

"Go."

"I'm going to call Kurt Lindahl this morning."

I look at him.

"Not about the deal," he says. "There's no deal. About the knee. Halvard's got a residential line in Holland that's putting posts in corners because nobody there can weld it. I'm going to call him and offer to license the knee to them. For the high-end line. Built here. By Kiana and Lolo and the second shift. Halvard sells it under their name, we weld every frame, and every frame says *Clyde Park* on the steel where nobody sees it." He drinks his coffee. "I think he'll say yes. He's an admirer of the product."

I sit there with my mug.

"That's a terrible idea," I say.

"I know."

"He'll try to steal the weld schedule."

"He'll try."

"Lolo will hate it."

"Lolo hates everything until she's training it."

"I don't agree with you," I say.

"I know." He sets the cup on the radiator. "I'm telling you before. You can say no. Or you can say no and let me call him anyway, and then tell me I was wrong at seven tomorrow. Like the thermostat."

I look at him for a long time. In the chair by the radiator, in his wrong shirt, with his feet up and the light across his knees.

"Call him at nine," I say. "Not before. I want to talk to Irene about glass first. And I'm going to put the weld schedule in the safe at my mother's house, behind the pierogi."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's just how it is."

"Okay," he says. "Like a person."

I pick up my pencil. He picks up his phone and doesn't look at it; he puts it face down on his desk and leaves it there. I start drawing. He watches me draw. Outside the glass, the first arcs come on behind the yellow curtains, and Benny's forklift backs out of the steel rack beeping, and somebody on the knee line laughs, loud, and somebody tells them to shut up.

Coffee at seven. Rule three. In my handwriting, on the inside of the door. And under it, in his, *(not 7:15)*.

It's six fifty-nine.

Two desks stand pushed together, a door and a slab of white oak, with a quarter-inch step between them that nobody's going to fix.
