## Chapter 30 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

On Friday, the day the bank in Chicago counts our money, my husband comes through the door of my room at five to seven with snow on his shoulders and an espresso in a paper cup, and the first thing he says is, "I'm going to call Kurt Lindahl this morning."

I'm at my desk with the moka pot going on the hot plate, and I lift my head.

"Good morning to you too," I say.

"Good morning. I'm going to call Kurt Lindahl." He hangs his coat on the hook by the door, sits down in the radiator chair and puts his feet up, crossed at the ankle, his good shoes with the salt lines. The chair creaks and the room settles. It's the fourth morning, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, and four is not a lot of mornings, and I find I'm counting them anyway.

I pour the moka pot into the Polish Falcons mug, which survived Tuesday, and hand it across to him. He drinks it and makes the face.

"God," he says. "Every time."

"You could stop drinking it."

"No," he says, "I can't," and drinks more, and I let that go by without picking it up, because it's seven in the morning and some things are better left on the bench where you can see them.

"Why are you calling Kurt?"

"About the knee." He sets the mug on the radiator. "Not the company. There's no deal. Halvard's got a line in Holland that puts posts in corners on their good rooms because nobody there can weld a moment frame. I want to offer to license the knee to them, for the high end. Welded here, by Lolo's second shift, every frame stamped *Clyde Park* on the steel where nobody sees it. They sell it under their name, and we get paid for every one." He tilts the mug at me. "I think he'll say yes. He's an admirer of the product."

I sit there with my own coffee going cold in my hands.

"That's a terrible idea," I say.

"I know it is."

"He'll try to steal the weld schedule."

"He'll try, and he won't get it."

"Lolo will hate it."

"Lolo hates everything until she's training it."

"I don't agree with you, Adam."

"I know. That's why I'm telling you now and not calling him till nine." He folds his hands on his stomach. "You can say no. Or you can say no and I call him anyway, and you tell me I was wrong at seven on Monday. Your father says that's how a thermostat works."

"My father turned it to sixty-eight on Sunday and my mother turned it back on Sunday night and now they're not speaking."

"He told me," Adam says, and wipes his mouth. "He's very happy about it."

Out the glass corner the yard's going gray, and the maples on Clyde Park are coming up out of the dark. Benny's Silverado isn't in yet; it's early, even for Benny.

"Call him at ten," I say. "Not nine. I want to talk to Irene about glass first, and you don't say the word *knee* to a man in a turtleneck until the weld schedule's in the safe at my mother's house, behind the pierogi."

"Ten," he says, and nods.

"And if he asks for the schedule, you hang up."

"I hang up on him."

"You don't do the nose voice."

"I've lost it, Pip. Gordon says I sound like a man reading a weather report."

"Good," I say. "Weather's honest."

He laughs, the real one, short, into the mug. Then he checks his watch, which he never used to wear, a cheap steel one from the Meijer on Twenty-Eighth Street.

"You're wearing a watch," I say.

"Eleven dollars. I decided I look at my phone too much." He holds his wrist out so I can see it. "Gordon says it makes me look like a substitute teacher."

"You look like a substitute teacher who sold the school."

"I'll take it." When the mug's empty he gets up and takes his coat off the hook. "Gordon and I are in the glass room all day. Neil said they'll measure at close of business and call by five. I'll text you when I know."

"Text me before you know."

"How do I text you before I know?"

"Text me when you're scared," I say. "That's before."

He stands in the door with one hand on my father's brass pull a second longer than he needs to.

"Okay," he says, and goes out across the yard to his car, and I don't watch him go, I go back to the vise, but I hear the gate roll back and close, and I'd know the sound of his car out of every car in Michigan.

The radiator chair is pushed back at an angle where he got up out of it. I leave it.

The day is a Friday in a plant.

Irene and I spend an hour at the glazing table with the Holland quote and the Grand Rapids Glass quote side by side, and she runs one finger down the Holland one and stops on page three.

"Freight," she says. "They hid it in the fuel surcharge. Eleven hundred a truck."

I call Holland and tell them about it, and they take it off, because Holland would rather eat a freight charge than lose to Grand Rapids. Darnell comes in at eight with his eyes half shut and a photo of Nevaeh asleep on his chest that he shows to everybody on the knee line, one at a time, as if he's passing a collection plate.

"Four hours," he tells me when it's my turn. "Four hours straight, in a row. I don't even know what to do with my hands."

A little after ten Adam texts me. *Called Kurt. He said "let me think about it" in a voice that means yes. Asked for the weld schedule in the second sentence. I hung up.*

Then, a minute later, the phone goes again in my hand: *Scared. Gordon has refreshed the bank portal so many times it asked him if he's a robot.*

I write back: *Tell him to stop refreshing. It's a bank. It doesn't know he's there.*

Lolo comes in early for second shift at noon to set up the training bay at the far end of the knee line, where twenty-three people from La Vergne, Tennessee, are going to start on Monday. Twenty-two of them have found places to live, between the two duplexes Benny's cousin owns off Clyde Park, now that he's discovered he's a landlord with a heart, and the motel on Twenty-Eighth Street where Gordon got a rate. The twenty-third is Kiana, who's bringing Jaylen, and who texted Lolo on Wednesday to ask if Michigan has tornadoes, and Lolo texted back *only the one in the yard, hon*, which I think she's very proud of.

"You're really going to train them," I say, standing in the training bay with Lolo while she sets out coupons on the bench in a row like place settings.

"Time and a half," she says.

"You voted no."

"I voted no, and I lost. That's what voting is, hon. You lose, you show up Monday." She squares a coupon to the edge of the bench with one finger. "Eighteen months. That's what I told Kiana. I'm giving that girl eighteen months to make me unnecessary, and then I'm going to Florida whether you're rich or not."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair," Lolo says. "It's just how it is." Then she squints at me over her glasses. "Where'd I hear that?"

"Don't."

"You say it in your sleep now. Benny heard you say it to the vending machine."

On her way out of the bay she stops at the whiteboard in the break room, uncaps the red marker, and writes under the vote, *LOLO: 18 MOS. THEN FLORIDA*, and underlines *FLORIDA* twice so nobody can say they weren't told.

Mid-afternoon my phone buzzes on the bench, a number with a 312 area code.

*Haverhill offered. I start March 1. I have rented a truck for the 27th, which I priced, and I will unpack all fourteen boxes in Chicago. I have a door that closes there. It does not have a corner. I find I do not miss the corner.*

And under it:

*Also. The "two years" in Furnishings Weekly was Halvard's own comms team. Gordon forwarded me the thread on Monday; I had been copied on it in January and never opened it. It was a new plan, written the day after Kurt stood in your yard. I thought you should know who the person familiar was. — S.*

I stand there at the bench and read it twice.

*Thank you*, I start to type, and then I delete it, because she asked me not to, once, in writing.

*I'm glad you know who it was*, I write instead. *I'm glad you have a door.*

The answer comes back a minute later.

*Tell him the shirt is wrong. Tavi sent me a photo. The crease is on the wrong side of the sleeve.*

Near five my phone rings, and it isn't Adam. It's Gordon.

"Pip," he says, and he's in the stairwell, because I can hear the pigeons. "Pip. Adam said to call you first. He said you should hear it from me before he hears it from me, which I didn't understand, but he was very firm, so I'm calling." He takes a breath. "Six point four one at close. The escrow from two weeks of cuts, Gil's terms, and a week of Nashville we didn't spend. Neil just called. We're over the floor by four hundred and ten thousand dollars." He laughs, a little wildly. "One week of Nashville, Pip. Almost to the dollar."

I sit down on the stool at my bench.

"Gordon," I say.

"I know," he says. "I know. I'm going to go sit in my car for a while."

He hangs up. I sit there on the stool with the phone in my hand. Across the floor the knee line is running under the yellow curtains, four arcs going, the long crackle of the beads. The press brake goes *chunk*. Somebody's radio is playing Bob Seger, because it's always Bob Seger at five o'clock on a Friday in West Michigan; it's a law.

My phone buzzes again, and it's Adam: *Gordon called you?*

*He called me*, I write.

*Okay.* Then, a second later: *I'm going to sit in the car with Gordon for a while.*

I go out to the break room. Darnell's there, getting his coat on, with his lunch cooler and the photo of Nevaeh as his phone background. Benny's there with a cruller from a box somebody left on the counter, and a couple of the kids from glazing, and Tavi at the microwave cart with his laptop open on the dashboard that still shows the dots.

Every one of them stops what they're doing.

"We're over," I say. "The bank. Four hundred and ten thousand over."

Nobody says anything for a second. Then Darnell takes his coat back off, goes to the whiteboard, uncaps the green marker, and under the vote, under Lolo's eighteen months, under the count, he writes in his blocky capitals: *FEB 12 — OVER*. He caps the marker and stands back and studies it with his hands on his hips.

"That's going to stay up there," he says. "Nobody erase that."

"Nobody's erasing it," says Benny, with his mouth full.

Tavi doesn't say anything. He looks at his laptop, at the map of orange dots, and then he closes it, gently, and for once he doesn't tell anybody the number.

Second shift comes on at three and goes off at eleven-thirty, and somewhere in between I go back out to the room in the yard, because it's mine and I like it there. I sit at my desk with the lamp on and do nothing for a while, and the building stays up. Out the glass corner, the yard light makes its white circle on the snow. The rolling door is up and the knee line is lit, and through it Lolo's at the far end in the training bay, alone, setting out one more coupon on the bench, and then standing there over the row of them with her glasses off.

The phone buzzes once more, and it's Adam. *Out of the car, Gordon's okay. I'm going home, are you?*

*Soon*, I write. *By eleven.*

*I'll leave the light on in the kitchen.*

I set the phone face up on the desk and sit back in my chair, set low, my feet flat on the floor.

His chair is still where he left it this morning, pushed back from the radiator at an angle.

I don't straighten it. He'll want it at five to seven.
