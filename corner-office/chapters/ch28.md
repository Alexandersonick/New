## Chapter 28 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

At five fifty-two on Saturday morning there are a hundred and forty-one people standing on the floor of the Clyde Park plant, and none of them are working, and the only machine running is the coffee urn.

Darnell rolled the whiteboard out of the break room at five-thirty. It's on its wheeled stand in the open space between the knee line and the glazing table, where we stage finished walls before they go to the roof cassette line, the one patch of floor big enough to hold everybody. Both shifts. First shift came in early and second shift never went home, or went home and came back. They're standing in a ragged half-circle in their coats and hoods and safety glasses, holding paper cups, some of them in their church clothes underneath their Carhartts because it's Saturday and half of them have a baptism or a funeral or a kid's hockey game after this. It smells like coffee and cold air and the sawdust that never quite leaves the roof line, and Marge's crullers, eleven boxes of them on a pallet by the time clock, because Lolo called Marge at four and Marge opened early.

I'm standing next to the whiteboard. I've got the blue marker in my hand. I didn't sleep.

Tavi's at the edge of the crowd in his Carhartt with his phone out. He sees me looking. He looks down at the phone. Then he puts it in his pocket, both hands, like he's putting away something hot.

Gordon's here. I didn't ask him. He's standing by the coffee urn in his cardigan holding his laptop against his chest, the way he holds it, sweating already though it's forty-eight degrees in here. He sees me see him and lifts one hand off the laptop, half a wave, half a man about to be called on in class.

And at the back, by the time clock, under the old schoolhouse clock that runs four minutes slow, there's my husband.

He's in a white shirt. It's wrinkled. It's so wrinkled it looks like he slept in it, which he did, on the floor of my room under my grandmother's afghan. No tie. No jacket. His hair's wet from the shower in the locker room, which he's never used in nine years, and which smells like Lava soap and nickel. He's not standing with anybody. He's standing by himself with his hands at his sides, the way he never stands. Adam stands in rooms with his hands doing things. Holding a coffee, holding a clicker, holding a man's elbow.

He looks at me across a hundred and forty heads. He doesn't smile. He nods, once.

The schoolhouse clock says 5:56, which means it's six.

"Okay," I say.

It goes quiet. Not all at once. In waves, from the front to the back, people shushing people, Benny saying *hey, hey* to the kids from glazing, until it's so quiet I can hear the coffee urn ticking as it heats.

"I'm going to tell you the worst of it," I say. "Then I'm going to put something to you, and you're going to decide it. Not me."

Nobody moves.

"The company's almost out of money," I say. "Not the plant. The plant's fine. You're fine. You made seven hundred knees last quarter and every one of them's perfect, Irene checked." Somebody laughs, short, nervous. Irene doesn't. "The company. Nashville's been losing money every week for a year. In October we almost missed a payroll. You didn't know that. Adam borrowed nine million dollars with his own shares to make it, so you'd get paid on a Friday and never find out. You didn't know that either."

A murmur goes through the floor. I don't look at the back. I keep going.

"There was a company in Minneapolis that wanted to buy us. They'd have paid everybody's bills and fixed everything, and in two or three years they'd have closed this building and put a post in the corner. You saw that in the paper. I went on Channel 6 and said no." I look at the floor for a second, the concrete, the old paint lines from when this was a cabinet plant, worn almost to nothing. "And I didn't tell anybody first. Not Adam. Not Simone. Not you. Forty of you were standing right there in that door and I didn't tell you anything. I told you *I'm handling it*. I've been telling you that for a month. Benny, I told you *nobody's selling this plant* in January, and I didn't know if that was true. I decided what you could carry. I'm sorry. That's not how my dad did it, and it's not how this place works. That's the bad part."

The floor's very quiet. Benny's looking at me with his cigarette behind his ear and his arms crossed. He nods once, slowly, like a man accepting a part that came back from a supplier with a defect and a note.

"Here's what's true now," I say, and I turn to the board. "We've got seven thousand nine hundred people who put money down on a room with no post in the corner. That money's in a bank in Cleveland, and every time we cut steel for one of them, ten percent comes loose. Gil Chamberlain in Monroe gave us sixty days on steel. The glass houses gave us forty-five. The state's paying for us to train a second shift on the knee. Simone did that. Before she left." I tap the numbers. "We've got a hole. One point seven million dollars. And we've got six days. Next Friday a bank in Chicago looks at our cash, and if we're under six million, they take Adam's shares and the whole thing starts over without us."

I put the marker on the line that says *SATURDAYS × 12 WKS*.

"So here's what I'm asking. Twelve weeks of six-day weeks. Training a second shift on the knee while you're still welding it. Faster cuts, faster escrow, out of the hole. And for it, five percent of this company, out of my own shares, into a trust that belongs to everybody standing on this floor. Not a bonus. A piece. Of the room."

Nobody says anything. Then, from the left, by the glazing table, a hand goes up.

It's Trey, the nineteen-year-old from glazing, in a Detroit Lions hoodie under his coat.

"What's five percent of a unicorn?" he says.

"About as much as you'd get from a unicorn," says Benny, before I can answer, and people laugh, and it breaks something loose.

They ask questions. A lot of them. Irene asks about glass lead times, and I answer, and she nods. Darnell asks if the paint booth can run two shifts without a new filter, and I say no, and he says he figured, and writes something on his hand. A woman from the roof line asks if Saturdays would be paid, and I say yes, straight time, and she says *straight time* back to me in a voice that tells me exactly what she thinks of straight time on a Saturday. Somebody asks what happens if it doesn't work. I tell them the truth. I tell them I don't know. It's the first time I've said that in front of this crew in nine years.

Then I say: "Anybody else?"

Lolo stands up.

She's been sitting on an upturned five-gallon bucket at the front, the way I used to sit by my father's welder, in her bowling jacket, SPARE PARTS across the back in iron-on letters that have given up. She stands slowly, one hand on her knee. She takes her glasses off and lets them fall on their chain.

"Pip," she says. "I want the pool."

The floor goes completely still.

"I want the Halvard money. Eighteen months of base, for staying. I'm sixty-three. I've been on a line since I was nineteen. My hands go numb at night. My sister's got a lanai in Port Charlotte with a spare room and a ceiling fan, and that money's the lanai." She looks around at everybody, slowly, the whole half-circle. "I love this girl. I've known her since braces. Her father trained me. I think her plan's a good plan and I think Halvard would've been a sin, and I'm voting no anyway, because I'm tired and I want to go to Florida and I'm allowed to want that. I wanted her to hear it from me. In front of everybody. Not in the parking lot." She puts her glasses back on. "That's it. That's all I got."

She sits back down on her bucket.

Nobody says anything. I look at Lolo. Lolo looks at the board. I want to say something to her and there isn't anything to say that isn't *thank you*, and she'd hate it, so I don't.

"Anybody else?" I say again.

At the back, by the time clock, a hand goes up.

It goes up slowly. Not like a man at a pitch meeting, stabbing the air. Like a kid in school who isn't sure he's allowed. A hundred and forty people turn around to look.

I look at him over all their heads.

"Can I?" Adam says.

"Go ahead," I say.

He doesn't come up to the front. He stays where he is, at the back, under the schoolhouse clock, in his wrinkled shirt, so that everybody has to turn around to look at him, and he doesn't move to make that easier. He puts his hands in his pockets, then takes them out again.

"I disagree with her," he says.

There's a sound on the floor. Not a word. A shifting.

"Not about the plan. The plan's good. The plan's the best thing anybody's done in this company since she drew the knee on a napkin. I disagree about the Saturdays." He looks at me, not at them. "There's another way to fill the box. She left it off the board. She left it off for me." He takes a breath. "Close Nashville."

Somebody near the front says *oh*.

"It's my plant," Adam says. "I opened it two years ago without asking her. I stood on a stage and told you all about it with a slide. It's been losing four hundred and twenty thousand dollars a week for a year, building a room nobody wants since the video. Gordon ran it last night." He tips his head at Gordon, who goes red to the ears. "If we stop the Nashville line Monday, the box is full. You don't need Saturdays to make the test. Saturdays still make it faster, and if you want to work them, you should, at time and a half, like it says on the wall. But you shouldn't have to. Not to pay for my mistake."

"It's sixty jobs," I say.

I hear myself say it. Out loud, across the floor, in front of everybody. I didn't plan it. It comes out of me like a weld spitting.

He looks at me. "I know it's sixty jobs."

"There's a girl down there who learned to read prints at a Wendy's."

"Kiana. I know." He doesn't look away. "Odell called her last night. Twenty-three of them said they'd move up here to learn the knee. If you'll train them. Kiana was the first one."

"You can't just decide that—"

"I'm not deciding it. I'm asking them." He gestures at the floor, at all of them. "And you. You protected Nashville because it's mine. That's the kindest thing you've done for me in two years and you shouldn't have done it. You don't get to carry my mistake so I don't have to. Not anymore. That's the heavy end, and it's mine."

"Who says?"

"Your father." He almost smiles. "It's on the door."

We're arguing. In front of a hundred and forty people, at six in the morning, across the whole floor of the plant, my husband and I are arguing, and nobody's stepping in, and nobody's leaving, and I realize I haven't argued with him in two years. We stopped. We went into separate rooms and filed things. And this is the first real fight we've had since the all-hands, and it's out loud, and everybody's watching, and he's not pitching and I'm not handling it, and it feels like the first time I put my hand flat on a weld that held.

"If we close Nashville," I say slowly, "the five percent still goes to the trust."

"It should go anyway," he says. "They should've had it from the start."

"And Saturdays are voluntary. Time and a half."

"Time and a half."

"And whoever comes up from Tennessee gets trained on the knee and gets a place on second shift."

"If you'll train them."

"I'm not training them," I say. "Lolo's training them."

From her bucket, without turning around, Lolo says, "Not for free I'm not."

Everybody laughs. The whole floor. It goes through them like a wind through a field, and some of the tension goes with it, and Adam stands at the back with his hands at his sides, laughing too, the real one, short and surprised.

I turn back to the board.

The box is empty. The blue marker's in my hand. I can feel every eye in the building on the back of my neck.

He gave it to me. That's what he did. He stood up at the back of the room and disagreed with me in front of everybody and then he stopped talking. He didn't close it. He could've. He's the CEO. He could've called Odell yesterday and pulled the plug and sent a memo. He didn't. He told me before, on the porch, in the cold, and then he came here and said it out loud, and then he left the box empty for me.

I uncap the marker. I write in the box. Four letters, in capitals, in my handwriting.

*NASH*.

Then I turn around.

"I'm taking it," I say. "His amendment. That's my decision. If you hate it, hate me for it, not him." I put the cap on the marker. "So here's the plan, with the amendment. Nashville's line stops Monday. Saturdays are voluntary, time and a half. Second shift trains on the knee, and anybody from Tennessee who wants to come is welcome. Five percent of the company from my shares goes into a trust for everybody on this floor. We build every one of those rooms here. With no post. That's what we're voting on."

I look out at them. A hundred and forty-one faces. Irene. Darnell, who named his daughter after heaven backwards. Trey in his Lions hoodie. Benny. Lolo on her bucket.

"All in favor," I say.

The hands go up.

Not all at once. They go up in ones and twos, like the first knees coming off the line in the morning, slow, checked. Irene first, flat palm, no hesitation, the way she glazes. Darnell. Tavi, way at the back, both hands, and then he looks around and puts one down. The woman from the roof line who said *straight time*. Trey. Then more, then a lot, rising up out of the coats and hoods across the whole floor, quiet, no cheering, just hands.

Darnell counts. He walks the half-circle with his lips moving and his finger pointing, the way he counts pallets, and nobody puts a hand down while he does it, and the coffee urn ticks, and the schoolhouse clock moves a minute.

"Opposed," I say.

Lolo's hand goes up from her bucket, straight up, firm. She doesn't look at me.

Benny's goes up too. He shrugs at me across the floor. "I don't like change, boss," he says. "Nothing personal."

"I know, Benny."

More hands. Not many. Some. A few of the old-timers from Lindqvist, who've seen plants close and plants saved and don't trust either one. Darnell counts them too.

He goes to the whiteboard. He takes the marker out of my hand without asking, which is the most Darnell thing he's ever done, and under the box with *NASH* in it, he writes the tally in his blocky numbers.

*YES: 97*
*NO: 41*
*ABSTAIN: 3*

He caps the marker. He hands it back to me.

A hundred and forty-one people stand on the concrete in the cold and read the numbers. Irene reads them twice, lips moving, the way she reads a glass order. Somebody near the back blows on their coffee. Lolo, on her bucket, looks at the forty-one and then at me, and nods once, like a woman signing for a delivery she ordered.

Then Benny uncrosses his arms, walks over to the press brake, and turns it on. The hydraulics hum up. He looks back over his shoulder at everybody.

"Well," he says. "We got knees to make."
