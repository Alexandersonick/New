## Chapter 28 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Just before six on Saturday morning there are a hundred and forty-one people standing on the floor of the Clyde Park plant, and none of them are working, and the only machine running is the coffee urn.

Darnell rolled the whiteboard out of the break room at five-thirty. It's on its wheeled stand in the open space between the knee line and the glazing table, where we stage finished walls before they go to the roof cassette line, the one patch of floor big enough to hold everybody, and there's a new eraser on the tray, still in its plastic. "Brought it," Darnell says when he catches me looking, and taps it with one finger.

Both shifts are here. First shift came in early and second shift never went home, or went home and came back. They're standing in a ragged half-circle in their coats and hoods and safety glasses, holding paper cups, some of them in their church clothes underneath their Carhartts because it's Saturday and half of them have a baptism or a funeral or a kid's hockey game after this. It smells like coffee and cold air and the sawdust that never quite leaves the roof line, and Marge's crullers, eleven boxes of them on a pallet by the time clock, because Lolo called Marge at four and Marge opened early.

I'm standing next to the whiteboard with the blue marker in my hand, and I didn't sleep.

Tavi's at the edge of the crowd in his Carhartt with his phone out. He sees me looking and looks down at the phone, and then he puts it in his pocket, both hands, like he's putting away something hot.

Gordon's here, though I didn't ask him. He's standing by the coffee urn in his cardigan holding his laptop against his chest, the way he holds it, sweating already though it's forty-eight degrees in here. He catches my eye and lifts one hand off the laptop, half a wave, half a kid about to be called on in class.

And at the back, by the time clock, under the old schoolhouse clock that runs four minutes slow, there's my husband.

He's in a white shirt, and it's wrinkled, so wrinkled it looks like he slept in it, which he did, on the floor of my room under my grandmother's afghan. There's no tie and no jacket, and his hair's wet from the shower in the locker room, which he's never used in nine years, and which smells like Lava soap and nickel. He's standing by himself with his hands at his sides, the way he never stands. Adam stands in rooms with his hands doing things: holding a coffee, holding a clicker, holding a man's elbow.

He finds me across a hundred and forty heads, and he doesn't smile. He nods, once.

The schoolhouse clock says 5:56, which means it's six.

"Okay," I say, and it goes quiet in waves, from the front to the back, people shushing people, Benny saying *hey, hey* to the kids from glazing, until it's so quiet I can hear the coffee urn ticking as it heats.

"I'm going to tell you the worst of it," I say. "Then I'm going to put something to you, and you're going to decide it, not me."

Nobody moves.

"The company's almost out of money," I say. "The company, not the plant; the plant's fine, and you're fine. You made seven hundred knees last quarter and every one of them's perfect, Irene checked." Somebody laughs, a short nervous laugh. Irene doesn't. "Nashville's been losing money every week for a year. In October we almost missed a payroll, and you didn't know that. Adam borrowed nine million dollars against his own shares to make it, so you'd get paid on a Friday and never find out, and you didn't know that either."

A murmur goes through the floor. I keep my eyes off the back and keep going.

"There was a company in Minneapolis that wanted to buy us, and in two or three years they'd have closed this building and put a post in the corner. I went on Channel 6 and said no." I look at the floor for a second, the concrete, the old paint lines from when this was a cabinet plant, worn almost to nothing. "And I didn't tell anybody first, not Adam, not Simone, not you. Forty of you were standing right there in that door, and all I told you was *I'm handling it*, and I've been saying that for a month. Benny, I told you *nobody's selling this plant* in January, and I didn't know if that was true. I decided what you could carry, and I'm sorry. My dad didn't run a shop that way, and this place doesn't work that way. That's the worst of it."

The floor's very quiet. Benny's got his arms crossed over his Carhartt, and he nods once, slowly, the way he signs for a part that came back from a supplier with a defect and a note.

"Here's what's true now," I say, and I turn to the board. "We've got seven thousand nine hundred people who put money down on a room with no post in the corner. That money's in a bank in Cleveland, and every time we cut steel for one of them, ten percent comes loose. Gil Chamberlain in Monroe gave us sixty days on steel. The glass houses gave us forty-five, and the state's paying for us to train a second shift on the knee. Simone did that, before she left." I tap the numbers. "We've got a hole of one point seven million dollars, and we've got six days. Next Friday a bank in Chicago looks at our cash, and if we're under six million, they take Adam's shares and the whole thing starts over without us."

I put the marker on the line that says *SATURDAYS × 12 WKS*.

"So here's what I'm putting to you: twelve weeks of six-day weeks, training a second shift on the knee while you're still welding it. Faster cuts, faster escrow, out of the hole. And for it, five percent of this company, out of my own shares, into a trust that belongs to everybody standing on this floor, a piece of the room."

For a second nobody says anything, and then, from the left, by the glazing table, a hand goes up.

It's Hector, the nineteen-year-old from glazing, in a Detroit Lions hoodie under his coat.

"What's five percent of a unicorn?" he says.

"About as much as you'd get from a unicorn," says Benny, before I can answer, and people laugh, and it breaks something loose.

They ask questions, a lot of them. Darnell asks if the paint booth can run two shifts without a new filter, and I say no, and he says he figured, and writes something on his hand.

Irene doesn't raise her hand. She just talks, the way she talks across the glazing table. "Holland's at five weeks on the big lites. You run twelve weeks of Saturdays, we're out of glass in week four."

"Grand Rapids Glass will do three weeks if we pay for the truck."

"Then pay for the truck," Irene says, and nods, and that's her question done.

A woman from the roof line in a Griffins toque puts her hand halfway up. "Are the Saturdays paid?" she says.

"They're paid at straight time," I say.

"Straight time," she says back to me, in a voice that tells me exactly what she thinks of straight time on a Saturday, and the people around her laugh the way you laugh in a dentist's chair. "My boy's got hockey at seven on Saturdays. Who drives him, you?"

"If you can't do a Saturday, I need to know it today," I say, "and not in week six, when I've got a frame on the jig and nobody to weld it."

She thinks about that, and nods, and puts her hand back in her pocket, and the man next to her, who I'm fairly sure is the hockey player's father, studies the ceiling like there's a leak in it.

From the back somebody calls, "And if it doesn't work?"

"Then I don't know," I say. In nine years I haven't said that to this crew, and it comes out easier than I thought it would.

I look along the half-circle and say, "Anybody else?"

Lolo stands up.

She's been sitting on an upturned five-gallon bucket at the front, the way I used to sit by my father's welder, in her bowling jacket, SPARE PARTS across the back in iron-on letters that have given up. She stands slowly, one hand on her knee, takes her glasses off and lets them fall on their chain.

"Pip," she says, "I want the pool."

The floor goes completely still.

"I want the Halvard money, eighteen months of base for staying. I'm sixty-three, and I've been on a line since I was nineteen, and my hands go numb at night. My sister's got a lanai in Port Charlotte with a spare room and a ceiling fan, and that money's the lanai." She looks around at everybody, slowly, the whole half-circle, and then points at me with one hand. "I love this girl. I've known her since braces, and her father trained me. I think her plan's a good plan and I think Halvard would've been a sin, and I'm voting no anyway, because I'm tired and I want to go to Florida and I'm allowed to want that. I wanted her to hear it from me, in front of everybody, and not in the parking lot." She puts her glasses back on. "That's it, that's all I got."

She sits back down on her bucket.

Nobody says anything. Lolo looks at the board instead of at me, and I want to say something to her, but there isn't anything to say that isn't *thank you*, and she'd hate it, so I keep my mouth shut.

"Is there anybody else who wants to talk?"

At the back, by the time clock, a hand goes up.

It goes up slowly, the way a kid's hand goes up in school when he isn't sure he's allowed, and a hundred and forty people turn around to look.

"Can I?" Adam says.

"Go ahead," I say, over all their heads.

He doesn't come up to the front. He stays where he is, at the back, under the schoolhouse clock, in his wrinkled shirt, so that everybody has to turn around to look at him, and he doesn't move to make that easier. He puts his hands in his pockets, then takes them out again.

"I disagree with her," he says.

Something goes through the floor, boots shifting on the concrete, a thermos lid squeaking.

"Not about the plan; the plan's the best thing anybody's done in this company since she drew the knee on a napkin. I disagree about the Saturdays." He's talking to me, not to them. "There's another way to fill the box. She left it off the board, and she left it off for me." He takes a breath. "Close Nashville."

Somebody near the front says *oh*.

"It's my plant," Adam says. "I announced it without asking her. I stood on a stage and told you all about it with a slide, and it's been losing four hundred and twenty thousand dollars a week for a year, building a room nobody's wanted since the video. Gordon ran the numbers."

"It's true," Gordon says from the coffee urn, too loud, and then, quieter, to the people turning to look at him, "I ran it three times."

"Course you did, Gordon," Lolo says from her bucket, and Gordon goes red all the way down into his cardigan.

"If we stop the Nashville line Monday, the box is full, and you don't need Saturdays to make the test," Adam says. "Saturdays still make it faster, and if you want to work them, you should, at time and a half, like it says on the wall. But you shouldn't have to work them to pay for my mistake."

"It's sixty jobs," I say.

It comes out of me before I've planned it, loud enough for the back row, like a weld spitting.

"I know it's sixty jobs," he says, and he doesn't look away.

"There's a girl down there who learned to read prints at a Wendy's."

"Her name's Kiana, and Odell called her last night. Twenty-three of them said they'd move up here to learn the knee, if you'll train them, and Kiana was the first one."

"You can't just decide that—"

"I'm not deciding it, I'm asking them." He opens a hand at the floor, at all of them. "And you. You protected Nashville because it's mine, and you shouldn't have. You don't get to carry my mistake so I don't have to, not anymore. That's the heavy end, and it's mine."

"And who says it's yours?"

"Your father," he says, and he almost smiles. "It's on the door."

In front of a hundred and forty people, at six in the morning, across the whole floor of the plant, my husband and I are arguing, and nobody's stepping in, and nobody's leaving. We haven't had a fight since the all-hands. We went into separate rooms and filed things. This one's out loud, with everybody listening, and he isn't pitching and I'm not handling it, and my hand is steady on the marker.

"If we close Nashville," I say slowly, "the five percent still goes to the trust."

"It should go anyway," he says. "They should've had it from the start."

"And Saturdays are voluntary, at time and a half."

"Time and a half," he says.

"And whoever comes up from Tennessee gets trained on the knee and gets a place on second shift."

"If you'll train them," he says.

"I'm not training them," I say, "Lolo is."

From her bucket, without turning around, Lolo says, "Not for free I'm not."

The whole floor laughs. It goes through them like a wind through a field, and some of the tension goes with it, and Adam stands at the back with his hands at his sides, laughing too, the real one, short and surprised at himself.

I turn back to the board.

The box is empty and the blue marker's in my hand, and every eye in the building is on the back of my neck.

He's the CEO. He could've called Odell yesterday and pulled the plug and sent a memo. He told me on the porch, in the cold, and then he came here and said it out loud, and then he stopped talking and left the box where it is.

I uncap the marker and write in the box, four letters, in capitals, in my handwriting.

*NASH*.

Then I turn around to face them.

"I'm taking it," I say. "His amendment." I put the cap on the marker. "That's my decision, and if you hate it, hate me for it, not him. So here's the plan, with the amendment. Nashville's line stops Monday. Saturdays are voluntary, time and a half. Second shift trains on the knee, and anybody from Tennessee who wants to come is welcome. Five percent of the company from my shares goes into a trust for everybody on this floor. We build every one of those rooms here, with no post. That's what we're voting on."

A hundred and forty-one faces look back at me: Irene, Darnell, who named his daughter after heaven backwards, Hector in his Lions hoodie, Benny, Lolo on her bucket.

"All in favor," I say.

The hands go up in ones and twos, like the first knees coming off the line in the morning, slow, every one of them checked. Irene's is first, flat palm, no hesitation, the way she glazes. Then Darnell's, and then Tavi's, way at the back, both hands, until he looks around and puts one down. The woman from the roof line who said *straight time* puts hers up, and Hector, and then more, then a lot, rising up out of the coats and hoods across the whole floor, quiet, no cheering, just hands.

Darnell counts, walking the half-circle with his lips moving and his finger pointing, the way he counts pallets, and nobody puts a hand down while he does it, and the coffee urn ticks, and the schoolhouse clock moves a minute.

"Opposed," I say.

Lolo's hand goes up from her bucket, straight up, firm. She doesn't look at me.

Benny's goes up too, and he shrugs at me across the floor. "I don't like change, boss, it's nothing personal," he says.

"I know it isn't, Benny."

More hands go up, not many: a few of the old-timers from Lindqvist, who've seen plants close and plants saved and don't trust either one. Darnell counts them too.

He goes to the whiteboard and takes the marker out of my hand without asking, which is the most Darnell thing he's ever done, and under the box with *NASH* in it, he writes the tally in his blocky numbers.

*YES: 97*
*NO: 41*
*ABSTAIN: 3*

He caps the marker and hands it back to me.

A hundred and forty-one people stand on the concrete in the cold and read the numbers. Irene reads them twice, lips moving, the way she reads a glass order. Somebody near the back blows on their coffee. Lolo, on her bucket, looks at the forty-one and then at me, and nods once, like a woman signing for a delivery she ordered.

Then Benny uncrosses his arms, walks over to the press brake, and turns it on. The hydraulics hum up, and he looks back over his shoulder at everybody.

"Well," he says. "We got knees to make."
