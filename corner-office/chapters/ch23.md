## Chapter 23 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

On Monday, day one of ten, I try to fix it by myself, and by four in the afternoon I've learned what every bank in West Michigan sounds like when it's being kind.

West River Bank, where we've had our operating account since 2015, sounds like a man named Curtis who went to Catholic Central with my brother Marek and who says *I'm going to be honest with you, Pip* four times in eleven minutes before telling me that a two-point-three-million-dollar bridge to a company with an active lender dispute and no acquirer isn't something his committee will touch in ten days, or ten weeks, or, honestly, Pip, at all.

Lakeshore Community Credit Union sounds like a woman named Fran who saw the video.

"Is it true you're a member?" she says. "Personally? Somebody here said you were a member."

"Since 1998, Fran. I opened it with my First Communion money."

"Oh, honey," Fran says, and I can hear exactly how the rest of the call is going to go, and it goes that way. Fran's sorry, and then Fran's so sorry, and then she asks if I'd sign a photo for her nephew.

A dentist in Ada who bought the first Corner we ever sold to a stranger, in 2015, and who's sent me a Christmas card every year since with a photo of himself painting watercolors in it, sounds like a man stepping very carefully backward out of a room. He's so proud of me. He's going to send a check for five hundred dollars, personally, no paperwork, because he believes in me.

"Dr. Vander Molen, please don't," I say. "Keep it. Buy paint."

"I have plenty of paint, Philippa. I have more paint than sense." He sends it anyway. It arrives on Thursday, and I keep it on my desk under the vise.

By four o'clock I've called nine people. I've written their names down on the back of an invoice in a column, and next to each one I've written a number, and the numbers are zero, zero, zero, zero, *maybe in March*, zero, zero, *$500 (Dr. Vander Molen, watercolors)*, zero.

I've got Simone's model open on my laptop in a folder called PIP. I've got Irene's numbers on the whiteboard in the break room and Gil Chamberlain's steel coming on Thursday. I've got two point three million dollars of hole and nine days to fill it and nobody to fill it with.

Around ten after four it comes to me that I've sat at my desk in the yard all day without once looking at his chair.

It's been moved an inch, maybe two. It's angled the wrong way, toward the glass instead of the desk, like somebody sat in it and looked out. And my chair, which I keep set low so my feet are flat on the floor, the way my father taught me, has been raised and put back down. I can tell, because the lever's at a different notch. Somebody's put it back to almost exactly where I had it, almost, the way you'd put back something you borrowed in a house where you're not sure you're welcome.

The moving blanket on the shelf by the door is folded with the corners square. Darnell doesn't fold the corners square. Darnell folds it in a sort of lump and calls it folded.

I sit there with my hands in my lap, looking at the blanket.

I don't need to call Benny and ask, because he told me. Friday night, just before midnight, two texts in a row. I read them standing in the dark kitchen with the laptop still playing, and I've read them maybe forty times since.

*I'm going to sleep in the room in the yard. I'll be out before first shift. I'm telling you so you don't hear it from Benny.*

And under it, three minutes later:

*I'm sorry for what I said at the end. Not because it wasn't true. Because I said it to hurt you. That was the wrong reason to tell you a true thing.*

He said he'd be gone before Benny. I don't call Benny to check, and I don't walk the yard looking for tire tracks. I just look at the blanket for a while, and then I put my chair back down one more notch, to where it was.

I don't text him back. I wouldn't know what to put in it. I'm the one who told him to go, and he went where I sent him, and folded the blanket.

At six o'clock I drive to my parents' house.

They live on the West Side on Garfield Avenue in the same house I grew up in, a narrow two-story with green asbestos siding and a statue of the Virgin in a half-buried bathtub in the front yard, painted blue, with a little string of Christmas lights around her that my father leaves up till Easter because he says she likes them. The garage where we built Unit 001 is still out back, with a new roof. My father keeps his welder in it. He still welds on Saturdays, small things: boot scrapers, a fence gate for the neighbor, a bird feeder shaped like the basilica.

My mother's at Bingo. My father's at the kitchen table in his undershirt, doing the crossword in pen.

"She's at Bingo," he says, when I come in. "There's gołąbki in the oven. Don't touch the plum ones, they're for the priest."

"I'm not hungry, Tato."

He looks up at me over his reading glasses and keeps looking. Then he puts down the pen and gets up, slowly, one hand on the table, and gets a plate out of the cupboard and puts two gołąbki on it from the oven with a fork and sets it in front of me, and sits back down, and picks up his pen.

"Eat," he says.

So I eat, and they're very good. They're always very good. My mother's been making them the same way since 1971, and she'll tell you it's the rice, but it's the cabbage, she blanches it twice.

My father does the crossword while I eat. The clock over the stove ticks, the one shaped like a teapot that's been running eight minutes fast since the Clinton administration.

"I did something stupid," I say.

"I saw. On Channel 6." He writes a word in. "You looked good. Very strong. Like your mother at a parish council."

"I didn't tell him first, Tato."

"Who didn't you tell?"

"Adam."

My father lays the pen down crosswise on the crossword, which is what he does instead of clearing his throat.

"I didn't tell anybody first," I say. "I didn't tell Adam, I didn't tell Simone, I didn't tell the crew. I had my phone in my hand for twenty minutes and I put it in my pocket. And now the deal's dead and the bank's calling the loan in nine days and Simone quit and I told him to leave." I put the fork down. "I did exactly what he did to me. With Nashville. On the stage. I stood up and decided in public, and the people who were in it with me found out from the TV."

My father squares the pen against the edge of the newspaper and looks at it instead of me.

"Your rule," he says. "On the door."

"Nobody decides alone."

"You know what I meant by that? In the garage?"

"You meant the thermostat, Tato. You told me."

"I told you the joke." He nods at the wall where the thermostat is, under the Last Supper. "Thirty-one years, your mother and me and that thing. You know who won?"

"Mama, because Mama always wins."

"Nobody won." He smiles, a little. "That's not what it was about. You thought it meant deciding together, like a committee with a vote. Your mother and me, we never agreed about anything. Not the thermostat, not the car, not your brother Joe joining the fire department, not you quitting Ferris State to build sheds in my garage with a boy from Kalamazoo." He taps the table with one finger. "But every time she was going to turn that thermostat, she said, *Stanley, I'm turning it to sixty-two*. Every single time, and then she turned it. And I'd get up and turn it back to sixty-eight, and I'd say, *Halina, I'm turning it back.*"

"That's the whole thing? You just told each other?"

"That's the whole rule. You don't have to agree. You just have to say it out loud to the person before you do it, so they're not alone with it when they find out." He picks the pen back up. "*Nobody decides alone* doesn't mean you decide together, Pipsqueak. It means nobody finds out alone."

I sit there at the kitchen table on Garfield Avenue with my mother's gołąbki going cold on the plate and the teapot clock ticking eight minutes fast, and I think about the thermos on my desk at Clyde Park, still hot, and *is she in yet*. I think about the text on level three of the ramp with a phone number and a drawer and a password in it, and about the knock.

He's been doing it for two weeks, badly, with *um*, saying every bit of it out loud to me first.

And I went on television.

"Eat your gołąbki," my father says, and fills in another word.

At eight-thirty I'm standing in the eleventh-floor hallway of a building on Ionia Avenue that used to keep West Michigan supplied in ham, knocking on Simone Achterberg's door.

She opens it in the MSU ENGINEERING MOM sweatshirt with a roll of packing tape in her hand. Behind her, the boxes have multiplied. There are fourteen now, three new ones open in the middle of the floor and half full, and a roll of bubble wrap, and her lighthouse print in its frame leaning against the couch with a moving blanket over it.

"Pip," she says, and doesn't move out of the doorway.

"I'm sorry," I say.

I say it in the doorway, before she can ask me in, before I can plan it. I say it the way Adam said it to Gil Chamberlain in Monroe, with the worst of it in the first sentence.

"I'm sorry I didn't tell you. You were in it with me. You lied for me on a call with Kurt for forty minutes and I didn't text you. I held my phone for twenty minutes and I put it in my pocket. It was the exact thing. You were right. I did the thing he did to me with the slide, and I did it to you, and I'm sorry."

Simone stands in the doorway with the packing tape and turns the roll once around her finger, then again.

"Come in," she says finally. "Mind the bubble wrap."

I come in and mind the bubble wrap. She doesn't offer me wine this time. She puts the tape down on top of a box marked KITCHEN, MISC and leans against the steel counter with her arms crossed.

"I accept your apology," she says. "I do not accept many apologies. Most people apologize so that you will make them feel better. You did not do that. You just said it."

"I've been practicing on bankers all day. They're a tough room."

"I can tell. Your husband called me yesterday." She watches my face. "At ten in the morning. On a Sunday. He told me he was going to stop calling me at night, and he told me before he stopped." Her mouth gives up a quarter inch on one side, which on Simone is a grin. "Then he asked me if he could call Dave Haverhill and tell him I was exemplary. He asked, Pip. He used the word *can*."

"Did you let him?"

"Yes. I have an interview in Chicago on Thursday." She looks at the boxes. "I am not coming back. Let me be precise about why. I am not angry anymore. But I was the one he called in the dark, and as long as I am in that building, that is still a little bit true. You two should have the building."

I pick the tape up off the KITCHEN box, because my hands want something to do, and start closing the half-full box nearest me.

"You may tape," Simone says. "You may not label. You print like a welder."

"I print like an engineer."

"You print like an engineer who welds." She lets me finish anyway, and she watches every strip go down.

I tape the box shut and stand there between the others with nothing left in my hands. *Thank you* feels wrong. She asked me not to say it, the last time, and I don't think she's changed her mind.

"Is there anything—" I start.

"Yes." She unfolds her arms and goes to a box marked OFFICE, open on the floor, and takes out a folder. It's green, which on Simone is practically a party hat. "There is a state fund for skilled-trades training at manufacturers with fewer than five hundred employees. It reimburses wages paid to trainees during certified training hours, and you qualify for the knee. I applied for you on Friday afternoon, after I resigned, between the bank and the bubble wrap. I put your name on it. You have to sign it by Wednesday."

She hands me the folder. I open it. It's the application, forty pages, filled out in her small level printing, every line. At the top of the first page, where it says *Program Contact*, she's written *Philippa Sobczak, Co-Founder*.

At the bottom, in the box that says *Estimated Reimbursement*, she's written six hundred and twelve thousand dollars.

"Simone, this is six hundred thousand dollars."

"Six hundred and twelve. That takes you to one point seven," Simone says. "You will not get one point seven from a bank in nine days. I know every bank in this state. You will have to get it from people."

"Which people?"

She looks at me over her reading glasses the way she looked at the gauge on the test rig the first day, like I'm reading two percent high.

"I think you know which people," she says. "I think you have been avoiding a box at the bottom of my column with a line through it. And I think there is a building full of people on Clyde Park Avenue who stood in a doorway in the snow and clapped for you, and who you still have not asked for anything."

I hold the green folder against my chest. Fourteen boxes and a lighthouse under a moving blanket, and a woman in a sweatshirt who filled out forty pages for me in her small level printing after she'd already quit.

"How do I ask any of them? How do you ask people for something after you've spent two years deciding for them?"
