## Chapter 2 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Twenty hours earlier, I'm standing in the elevator of the Lindqvist building with a box of donuts from Marge's, because it's the first Monday of the year and I'm the kind of co-founder who brings donuts on the first Monday of the year to a floor she's been on four times since September.

The elevator is the original freight car, cage doors and a brass lever nobody's allowed to touch anymore. We put a button panel in it in 2018. It still goes up like it's thinking about something else. Two women from marketing get on at two, see me, see the box, and say "Oh my God, Pip" in the voice you use for a cousin who flew in for a funeral. I let them take a cruller each. They tell me they love the new color on the Eight. I didn't change the color on the Eight. Somebody changed the color on the Eight.

"It's very calming," says the taller one, whose name is Bridget.

"It's beige," I say.

"Oatmeal," says Bridget.

"Oatmeal's a breakfast."

"Who made it oatmeal?" I ask.

"It came down from the integration team," Bridget says, and then her friend elbows her, and Bridget gets very interested in the floor numbers.

They laugh like I'm doing a bit. I'm half doing a bit. On three they get off. On four the doors open and I step out onto the floor with the donuts held up in front of me like a peace offering, and I'm already saying "Happy New Year, you degenerates" when I look across the floor at the northwest corner.

There's a woman in my room.

The floor of the Lindqvist building is long and old, a hundred feet of maple boards with the bolt holes still in them from the furniture presses, brick walls, steel columns painted a green that was popular in 1922. We put ninety people up here. Sales on the east side by the windows, marketing in the middle, design studio along the south wall under the skylights. And in the far northwest corner, where the two big windows meet over the river, the first room I ever built, sitting on its four bolt plates like a cat that found the one sunny spot in the house.

Cedar siding gone silver. Two glass walls meeting at the corner with no post. A door on the east side, birch, with a brass pull my father turned on his lathe.

The door is propped open with a banker's box. There's a ficus on my desk.

Nobody is watching me. Ninety people are very busy not watching me. Monitors are being looked at with tremendous concentration. A man from sales is on the phone saying "Absolutely, absolutely" to what I would bet is a dial tone. Somebody in design has their headphones on and their eyes so wide open they look like a cartoon of a person hearing a noise in an attic.

I walk across the floor. I walk at a normal speed with the donuts.

The woman in my room is in her forties, tall, with gray-blond hair cut very straight at her jawline and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and she's lifting a framed picture of a lighthouse out of a box like it's a birthday cake. She's wearing a navy sweater that probably has a fabric content she could tell you. I know her. Simone Achterberg. Vice President of Operations as of last May. We've been in six meetings together, maybe seven. She took notes in every one of them by hand, in a black notebook, in handwriting so small and level I once thought it was printed. In the third meeting she asked me a question about weld times on the knee frame that nobody in that company had asked me in two years, and I answered it for twenty minutes, and she wrote down all twenty minutes.

She sees me in the doorway and puts the lighthouse down on my desk, face up.

"Pip," she says. "Good morning."

"You're in my room."

"I asked for it," she says. "He said yes before I finished the sentence."

She says it the way you'd say the weather in Grand Rapids in January: not friendly, not unfriendly, a thing that is true and has consequences for your commute. I'll give her that. I've had people take things from me with a smile on their face and I'd rather have it level.

I look at the ficus. I look at her pens, which are in my drawer. I look at the photo on the windowsill of a girl in a green sweatshirt with her arm around a dog.

"That your daughter?"

Simone glances at the photo, then back at me, a small adjustment behind the eyes, like she's checking a measurement.

"Hannah," she says. "She's a sophomore at State."

"What's she taking?"

"Mechanical engineering. She wants to build bridges, or she did in October."

"Good school for bridges." I hold the box out. "Donut?"

She looks at the box for a second longer than a person normally looks at donuts. Then she takes a plain cake one, the least donut of all the donuts, and holds it without eating it.

"Thank you," she says.

"You're welcome."

"I would have asked you," Simone says. "If I had known it was yours. Personally yours."

"Who did you think it belonged to?"

"The company. It's on the asset register." She sets the donut on a napkin and squares the napkin to the edge of my desk. "Line forty-one. Furniture and fixtures, founders' office, one unit."

"It's on a lease."

"Then the register is wrong."

"The register's been wrong since 2018."

"Then I will fix it," she says, and she means it, and I believe her, which is the worst part of the morning so far.

That's when Adam comes around the end of the design studio with two coffees in a cardboard tray and his coat over his arm. He's moving fast, the way he always moves, as if every room he walks into is already a little late for him. He's wearing the blue suit he wears for flights. His hair is still wet at the back from the gym. When he sees me he doesn't stop walking, but something in his face slows down.

There are eleven years of Adam Kerrigan's faces in my head, catalogued and cross-referenced. This is the one he makes when he walks into a room and the room isn't the room he planned for. It lasts half a second. Then he puts the other face on, the one that sold three hundred million dollars of a company to people who'd never stood in a backyard in their lives.

"Pip," he says, and hands one of the coffees to Simone, over my shoulder, into my room.

Simone takes it. She has a donut in one hand and a coffee in the other now, and she stands there in my room holding both, and none of us says anything for a while.

"Adam," I say. "Why is Simone in my room?"

He looks at the room. He looks at me. He takes a sip of his coffee.

"You barely use it," he says.

Behind me, the man in sales says "Absolutely" one more time, softer, and hangs up on the dial tone.

You barely use it.

He doesn't say it mean. That's the thing I'll think about at three in the morning for the next week. He says it like a fact on a slide. Like the occupancy rate of a conference room. He says it in the same voice he'd use to tell you that the Eight is now oatmeal.

"That's interesting," I say.

Adam has been married to me for eleven years and he knows what "that's interesting" means. Everybody in my family knows. My mother says it to the parish council. My brother Marek said it to a referee in a church league game once and the referee left the gym. It means you've already lost and you're the last to know. I watch Adam hear it. His thumb goes to the plastic lid of his coffee and presses the little tab down, presses it down again.

"Simone's running integration," he says. "She needs a door that closes. You're at the plant five days out of five. You come up here for the board and the holiday party. The room sits empty, Pip. People walk past it all day and it's dark. It looks like a museum."

"Integration of what?"

He glances at Simone. It's quick. It's nothing. It's the kind of glance you give the one person in the room who already knows the answer, to check that you're both going to give the same one.

I've seen that glance before. I used to be the person who got it.

"It's on the agenda for the Monday meeting," he says. "Which is right now. Which you'd know if you came to the Monday meeting."

"I'm here."

"You're here with donuts."

"They're from Marge's."

"I know where they're from, I can see the box." He breathes out through his nose. "Listen. We'll get you something better. A real office up here, south side, under the skylights. Whatever you want. You can design it."

"I designed this one."

"I know you did."

"I built this one."

"Pip. I know."

He says it gently, and there's a version of me that hears the gentleness and wants to put the donuts down. There's another version that's been sitting at a workbench at the plant for two years watching the parking lot every morning at seven. I don't know which one is going to talk until she does.

"Is that the plan for everything?" I ask. "You find out what I barely use, and then you give it to somebody who'll use it more?"

That lands somewhere. I see it land. He sets the tray down on the corner of a desk that belongs to somebody in marketing, who rolls her chair two feet away from it as though it might go off.

"That's not fair," he says, quiet.

"Did you ask me?"

"I'm asking you now."

"You're telling me now, in front of sales. That's a different verb."

"Pip, it's a room."

"It's a room," I agree. "It's ten by twelve with a door on the east side and a corner with no post in it. I know exactly what it is. I'm asking if you asked me."

He doesn't answer that. He looks at Simone instead, and that's an answer.

"That's interesting," I say again, and now a couple of people actually look up.

Simone sets her coffee on my windowsill next to her daughter. She doesn't set the donut down. She holds it like she's going to be asked to account for it later.

"I can take the south side," she says to Adam. "It isn't important to me where I sit."

"It's done," Adam says. "Facilities moved the badge access Friday."

Friday. I was at the plant Friday until nine at night with Lolo and a jig that wouldn't hold square. He didn't call. He doesn't call Fridays. He texts. *Late one, don't wait.* I didn't wait. I haven't waited in a long time. I made eggs and ate them over the sink and went to bed, and somewhere in the building where I used to have a door, a man from facilities was reprogramming a lock.

I look at the door. My father turned that pull on a lathe in our garage in 2014 out of a piece of brass rod from a bathroom fixture somebody threw out on Bridge Street. On the inside of the door, in carpenter's pencil on the raw birch, there's writing. Three lines, two different hands. You can't see all of it from here. The banker's box is leaning against the bottom of the door, holding it open, and it covers the top line.

I can see the bottom one. *3. Coffee at seven.* That line is in my handwriting. Underneath it, an arrow, and *(not 7:15)* in his.

I married him in that room. June of 2015, in my father's garage, before it had a desk in it or a company around it. A judge from the Kent County court who bowled with my uncle, my mother in her church hat, my father in a clean shirt holding the door open with his foot because the closer wasn't hung yet. Ten minutes. Afterward we had pierogi on a sheet of plywood on two sawhorses, and Adam made a speech to four people as if it were four hundred, and my father laughed until he had to sit down on a bucket.

I don't say any of that. You can't say a thing like that on a Monday in front of ninety people and a ficus.

"Okay," I say.

Adam's shoulders come down a quarter inch. He thinks I mean *okay*. I let him think it. He picks up the coffee tray again, the meeting already pulling on him from across the floor, the glass conference room where I can see the Monday people gathering, Gordon Pyle sweating into a cardigan, somebody setting out a projector clicker like it's a gavel.

"Come to the meeting," he says. "Please. There's something we need to walk through, and I'd rather you heard it from me."

"From you and Simone."

"From me."

"I'll be in in a minute," I say. "I'm going to give these out."

I hold up the donuts.

He looks at the box, then at me, and for half a second there's a face I don't have catalogued, something that might be about to ask a question. Then his phone buzzes in his pocket and he takes it out and the face is gone.

"Chicago," he says to Simone, not to me. "The Halvard dinner's at seven. I'm on the noon flight."

"I'll send you the deck tonight," she says.

"You're the best."

He says it the way you say it to a waiter. He says it the way he used to say it to me.

I go back across the floor. I give out donuts. I give out every donut in the box, cruller, glazed, bismarck, the long johns, the paczki Marge only makes in January because she can't help herself. I stop at every desk. I ask the woman in design whose headphones are on how her dad's hip is doing, and she takes the headphones off and tells me, for some time. I ask Bridget about the oatmeal. I tell the man in sales that next time he should call somebody real.

When the box is empty I fold it flat and put it in the recycling by the elevator.

Then I don't go to the meeting. I go to the stairwell, the old iron one in the northwest corner that nobody uses because the elevator's right there and the stairwell smells like pigeons and radiator paint. I go down one flight and stop on the landing at three where there's a window, tall and narrow and filthy, looking straight down onto Monroe Avenue.

From here I can see the whole width of the street. Two lanes, a bus stop, a fire hydrant. The streetlight posts are the old kind, cast iron, set back from the curb.

Plenty of room, I think.

Then I go down the rest of the stairs to my truck.
