## Chapter 13 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

The Nashville plant is the cleanest building I've ever paid for.

It sits on forty acres off Interstate 24 in La Vergne, Tennessee, between a mattress distribution center and a company that makes the plastic clamshells strawberries come in. White steel walls, a polished concrete floor you could do surgery on, skylights the length of a football field, so the light comes down on the line soft and even, like a museum. When we cut the ribbon fourteen months ago I stood up on a scissor lift in a hard hat with a pair of giant scissors and told three hundred people from Rutherford County that this was what the future of work looked like. The local paper ran a photo of me on the lift with my arms out. My mother had it framed, and it hangs in her condo in Sarasota, over the TV.

On Monday morning the line is running at forty percent.

"We've got three Lites on the floor," says Odell Haynes, walking me down the center aisle with his hands behind his back. "Two more in finishing. That's the week. That was last week too."

Odell's my plant manager. Halfway down the aisle he bends, without breaking stride, to pick a zip tie off the floor, and tells a kid at station four that his mother called the front office and it isn't an emergency, she just wants her casserole dish back. He hired every one of the sixty people working here and knows their kids' names and which ones are trying to quit smoking. He drives in from Murfreesboro every morning in a Twodesk polo tucked into creased khakis, which is more than I do.

"What happened to the order book?" I ask.

Odell stops walking and turns around, and gives me a kind of mild pity.

"You happened to it, boss," he says. "Or your wife did. That video."

"I saw the video, Odell."

"Everybody saw the video. My mama saw the video. My mama called me and said, *Odell, does your plant make the ones with no post?* And I said, no, Mama, we make the ones with the post, and she said, *well, what for?*" He shrugs, slowly. "Since the video, Lite orders are down eighty-one percent. People want the room that can leave. They don't want ours."

Down the line there are sixty people, give or take, in gray T-shirts with the company logo, at stations with good lighting and ergonomic mats and torque wrenches that talk to an iPad. I designed this line with a consultant from Detroit who'd done three car plants. It's beautiful, and it can build twenty-two Lites a day.

It can't weld the knee. We never set it up to. The knee takes eleven hours and four of Pip's people, and the whole point of Nashville was not to need Pip's people. That was my pitch to the board, in the glass conference room: volume, a second plant that doesn't depend on four welders in Wyoming, Michigan, who are the best in the country and can't be cloned, a posted corner, a lower price, a bigger market. The future.

Pip didn't come to that meeting. I didn't ask her to. I told everyone at the all-hands the next morning, from the stage, with a slide.

"Show me Kiana," I say.

Kiana's on wall framing, station six: twenty-four, from Smyrna, a single mother with a five-year-old named Jaylen who wants to be a tornado when he grows up. When I hired Odell she was working the drive-through at a Wendy's off Old Nashville Highway. Now she reads blueprints, which she learned on Tuesday nights in the training room at the back from a guy Odell brought in from the community college.

She sees me coming and lifts her nail gun in a little salute. "Morning, Mr. Kerrigan."

"Kiana, how's Jaylen?"

"Still wants to be a tornado." She grins. "Now he wants to be the tornado that picks up a little room and carries it away. He watched that video forty times."

"So did my whole building."

"Is it true she just stole it? Out the window?"

"She didn't steal it. It was hers."

Kiana nods slowly, like that's the most interesting thing anybody's said to her all week. "Good for her," she says. Then she remembers who she's talking to. "I mean—"

"No," I tell her. "Good for her."

She wipes her hand on her jeans and takes her phone out of her back pocket and shows me a framing drawing, zoomed in, a wall section with her pencil marks all over it. "I read that one myself last Tuesday," she says. "Whole wall. Odell didn't help."

"That's a Lite wall."

"I know what it is." She puts the phone away. "Someday I'm reading the other one."

I walk the rest of the line with Odell. I shake every hand and ask about every kid. I know about half the names without help, and Odell murmurs the other half to me as we walk, the way a good chief of staff does at a funeral. Everybody's polite and everybody's glad to see me, and everybody keeps glancing at the order board on the wall by the break room, a big screen with the week's builds, and the week's builds are three.

"I tried turning that board off last week," Odell says, low, as we pass it. "Kiana's crew turned it back on. They said they'd rather know."

In his office, a glass box over the floor with a view of the whole line, he closes the door and sits down and doesn't offer me coffee.

"I'm going to say something," he says. "You're not going to like it."

"Go ahead, Odell."

"You need to decide about this place."

"I've decided. It stays."

"It's costing you four hundred grand a week and building three rooms." He says it gently. "I've been in plants that ran like this. A truck plant in eighty-nine. A parts plant in oh-eight. You know what happens? Nothing. Nothing happens for months. And then everything happens on a Friday, and the people find out from the parking lot."

"That's not going to happen here."

"Then tell me what's going to happen here."

I sit there in his good chair, in his good glass box over my beautiful empty line, and I don't know. That's three times now. I'm going to have to start keeping count.

"You want to know what they ask me at the time clock?" Odell says. "They ask me if the lady with the crane is coming for us next."

"What do you tell them?"

"I tell them I don't know." He spreads his hands flat on the desk. "I'm a plant manager. I'm allowed to say it. You're the one who isn't."

I don't argue with him. Arguing with Odell is like arguing with the scoreboard in the fourth quarter, when you're down twenty.

My phone buzzes. It's Gordon. I put it on speaker because Odell's earned it.

"Nine weeks," Gordon says, without hello. "It was eleven, now it's nine. The Lite inventory's not moving and Nashville payroll hit Friday and the steel deposit for Q1 cleared. Nine weeks, Adam."

"I heard you the first time, Gordon."

"Northgate called again. They want the minutes from tomorrow's board, all of them, the day they're signed. They used the phrase *material adverse*." Gordon breathes. He sounds like he's in a stairwell. "I'm putting the cash on the agenda for the nineteenth. I have to, and Ruth agrees with me."

"Make it one line," I say. "*Cash position, discussion.* I'll walk them through it myself."

"One line is how we got here, Adam." He's almost whispering. "I'm sorry, it's my job. I can't put Halvard on the agenda without putting the reason for Halvard on the agenda. My father was an accountant for the Christian Reformed Church for thirty years, I won't—" He stops himself. "It's going on the agenda."

He hangs up. Odell waits with his hands folded on the desk.

"Who's Northgate?" he asks.

I tell him. I couldn't tell you why. Maybe because he's sixty people's plant manager in a glass box in Tennessee and they're going to find out from a parking lot otherwise, and I've just heard him say that's the worst way. I tell him about October, and the payroll, and nine million dollars borrowed in my own name with my shares on the line, and the covenant in March. I don't pitch it. It arrives in the wrong order, with the important part in the middle, the way true things do.

Odell listens to the whole thing. When I'm done he sits back and lets his chair creak, and looks out at the line.

"Does your wife know?" he says.

"No."

"Why not?"

*I don't know* is the honest answer, and it isn't the answer. The answer is that she told me Nashville was wrong. Not to my face; she never got the chance. She told Gordon, and Gordon told me, and I stood on a stage the next morning anyway. If I tell her about Northgate, I'm telling her she was right, and that every dollar of it went into the hole I dug while she was saying so. I'd rather owe Chicago nine million dollars than owe her that sentence.

"Because I'm an idiot, Odell," I say.

"Uh-huh." He swivels back to me. "Well. My wife's been married to an idiot for thirty-one years. I'll tell you what I learned. She doesn't mind the idiot so much. She minds finding out from the parking lot."

"What would you tell her?"

"Me? I'd tell her before the board does." He gets up and opens the door for me. "And then I'd tell my people, because they're next. What do I say at the time clock tomorrow, boss?"

"Tell them I was here."

"They know you were here. They shook your hand." He holds the door. "I'll tell them you're thinking. That one's free."

I fly home on the late flight out of BNA, a regional jet with two seats on one side and one on the other, and I take the one. The guy across the aisle is watching the video on his phone, the room coming out of the building, the streetlight going through the corner. He watches it twice and then shows it to the woman next to him, and she watches it, and says *oh, that's lovely*, and he says *she just took it, I guess, the wife*, and the woman says *good for her*.

He catches me looking. "You seen this?"

"Once or twice."

"My wife wants one. The no-post one. I told her that's thirty-eight grand for a shed."

"It isn't a shed," I say, before I can stop myself.

He gives me a long look over the phone. "Buddy," he says, "you sound just like her."

Everybody keeps saying *good for her*.

Over Kentucky I take out a legal pad, because I can't do this on a screen, and I write down what's still true. It doesn't fill a page. There are nine weeks of cash, and a Northgate covenant in March that will come sooner now. There's Halvard at one point one billion, which needs Pip, and Pip's no. There's Pip's plan, four thousand one hundred rooms a year, which needs six to eight million up front that I can't touch, because everything I own is thirty-four percent of a company and Northgate is holding it. At the bottom I write what Nashville costs a week, four hundred and twenty thousand, and I stare at that line until the flight attendant asks me twice if I want pretzels.

Under it I write *Tell her*.

I underline it twice. Then I sit there over Kentucky with the pen in my hand and do the thing I always do, which is plan the conversation. I plan where we'll be and what I'll say first. I plan the slide in my head, God help me, I can see the slide, it has a title.

Then I put the pen down and look out the window at the dark. In my head I cross out the slide, and the kitchen at midnight, and the yard with a bowling hat on my knee.

Seven o'clock tomorrow, in the room, before the board, with a coffee for each of us. I'll get there at five to, like I used to. I'll sit in the radiator chair, and when she comes in I'll tell her all of it before she takes her coat off, in the wrong order, the true way.

Past midnight, back in the room across the hall from her, I'm still awake when my phone lights up on the nightstand with a text.

*You haven't called in three nights. Are you all right?* — Simone.

I hold the phone over my face in the dark, the screen dimming and coming back when I touch it.

*Fine*, I type. *Sleeping.*

It's the first lie I've told Simone since May. I tell it in the dark with my eyes open, and set an alarm for five-thirty.
