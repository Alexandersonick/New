## Chapter 13 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

The Nashville plant is the cleanest building I've ever paid for.

It sits on forty acres off Interstate 24 in La Vergne, Tennessee, between a mattress distribution center and a company that makes the plastic clamshells strawberries come in. White steel walls. Polished concrete floor you could do surgery on. Skylights the length of a football field, so the light comes down on the line soft and even, like a museum. When we cut the ribbon fourteen months ago I stood up on a scissor lift in a hard hat with a pair of giant scissors and told three hundred people from Rutherford County that this was what the future of work looked like. The local paper ran a photo of me on the lift with my arms out. My mother had it framed. It's in her condo in Sarasota, over the TV.

On Monday morning at nine-forty, the line is running at forty percent.

"We've got three Lites on the floor," says Odell Haynes, walking me down the center aisle with his hands behind his back. "Two more in finishing. That's the week. That's last week too."

Odell's my plant manager. Fifty-five, Murfreesboro, twenty-six years in auto, a man so steady he makes the floor feel level. He hired every one of the sixty people working here. He knows their kids' names and their dogs' names and which ones are trying to quit smoking. He wears a Twodesk polo tucked into creased khakis, every day, like a man who takes the company more seriously than the company does.

"What happened to the order book?" I ask.

Odell stops walking. He turns and looks at me with a kind of mild pity.

"You happened to it, boss," he says. "Or your wife did. That video."

"I saw the video."

"Everybody saw the video. My mama saw the video. My mama called me and said, *Odell, does your plant make the ones with no post?* And I said, no, Mama, we make the ones with the post, and she said, *well, what for?*" He shrugs, slowly. "Since the video, Lite orders are down eighty-one percent. People want the room that can leave. They don't want ours."

I look down the line. Sixty people, give or take, in gray T-shirts with the company logo, at stations with good lighting and ergonomic mats and torque wrenches that talk to an iPad. I designed this line with a consultant from Detroit who'd done three car plants. It's beautiful. It can build twenty-two Lites a day.

It can't weld the knee. We never set it up to. The knee takes eleven hours and four of Pip's people, and the whole point of Nashville was not to need Pip's people. That was the pitch. That was my pitch, to the board, in the glass conference room, two years ago. Volume. A second plant that doesn't depend on four welders in Wyoming, Michigan, who are the best in the country and can't be cloned. A posted corner, a lower price, a bigger market. The future.

Pip didn't come to that meeting. I didn't ask her to. I told everyone at the all-hands the next morning, from the stage, with a slide.

"Show me Kiana," I say.

Odell takes me to Kiana. She's on wall framing, station six, twenty-four years old, from Smyrna, a single mother with a five-year-old named Jaylen who wants to be a tornado when he grows up. When I hired Odell, Kiana was working the drive-through at a Wendy's off Old Nashville Highway. Now she reads blueprints. She learned it here, in the training room at the back, on Tuesday nights with a guy Odell brought in from the community college. When she saw me at the ribbon cutting she came up and shook my hand and said, *Mr. Kerrigan, I can read a print now*, and showed me a framing drawing on her phone like it was a picture of her kid.

She sees me coming and lifts her nail gun in a little salute.

"Mr. Kerrigan."

"Kiana. How's Jaylen?"

"Still wants to be a tornado." She grins. "Now he wants to be the tornado that picks up a little room and carries it away. He watched that video forty times."

"Everybody did."

"Is it true she just stole it? Out the window?"

"She didn't steal it. It was hers."

Kiana nods slowly, like that's the most interesting thing anybody's said to her all week. "Good for her," she says. Then she remembers who she's talking to. "I mean—"

"No," I say. "Good for her."

I walk the rest of the line with Odell. I shake every hand. I ask about every kid. I know about half the names without help and Odell murmurs the other half to me as we walk, the way a good chief of staff does at a funeral. Everybody's polite. Everybody's glad to see me. Everybody keeps glancing at the order board on the wall by the break room, a big screen with the week's builds, and the week's builds are three.

In Odell's office, a glass box over the floor with a view of the whole line, he closes the door and sits down and doesn't offer me coffee.

"I'm going to say something," he says. "You're not going to like it."

"Okay."

"You need to decide about this place."

"I've decided. It stays."

"It's costing you four hundred grand a week and building three rooms." He says it gently, the way you'd tell a man his dog is old. "I've been in plants that ran like this. A truck plant in eighty-nine. A parts plant in oh-eight. You know what happens? Nothing. Nothing happens for a long time. And then everything happens on a Friday, and the people find out from the parking lot."

"That's not going to happen here."

"Then tell me what's going to happen here."

I sit there in his good chair, in his good glass box over my beautiful empty line, and I don't know. That's three times now. I'm going to have to start keeping count.

My phone buzzes. It's Gordon. I put it on speaker because Odell's earned it.

"Nine weeks," Gordon says, without hello. "It was eleven. It's nine. The Lite inventory's not moving and Nashville payroll hit Friday and the steel deposit for Q1 cleared. Nine weeks, Adam."

"I heard you."

"Northgate called again. They want the board minutes from Tuesday. All of them. They used the phrase *material adverse*." Gordon breathes. He sounds like he's in a stairwell. "I'm putting the cash on the agenda for the nineteenth. I have to. Ruth agrees. I'm sorry."

"Gordon—"

"I'm sorry, Adam. It's my job. I can't put Halvard on the agenda without putting the reason for Halvard on the agenda. I can't. My father was an accountant for the Christian Reformed Church for thirty years, I'm not going to—" He stops himself. "It's going on."

He hangs up. I look at the phone. Odell looks at me.

"Who's Northgate?" he asks.

I tell him. I don't know why. Maybe because he's sixty people's plant manager in a glass box in Tennessee and he's going to find out from a parking lot otherwise, and I've just heard him say that's the worst way. I tell him about October, and the payroll, and nine million dollars borrowed in my own name with my shares on the line, and the covenant in March. I tell him like a person telling a story, not like a pitch. It comes out of me in the wrong order, with the important part in the middle, the way true things do.

Odell listens to the whole thing. When I'm done he takes his reading glasses off and folds them and sets them on the desk.

"Does your wife know?" he says.

"No."

"Why not?"

That's four. *I don't know* is the honest answer and it's not the answer. The answer is that I've been carrying it so long by myself that I've started to think carrying it is the job. That if I set it down in front of her she'll pick it up, because she's Pip, she picks up everything, she'd pick up the heavy end of a building if you let her. And then it'll be hers too, and that'll be my fault.

"Because I'm an idiot, Odell," I say.

"Uh-huh." He puts his glasses back on. "Well. My wife's been married to an idiot for thirty-one years. I'll tell you what I learned. She doesn't mind the idiot so much. She minds finding out from the parking lot."

I fly home on the four-fifty out of BNA, a regional jet with two seats on one side and one on the other. I take the one. The guy across the aisle is watching the video on his phone. I can see it from here, the room coming out of the building, the streetlight going through the corner. He watches it twice and then shows it to the woman next to him, and she watches it, and says *oh, that's lovely*, and he says *she just took it, I guess, the wife*, and the woman says *good for her*.

Everybody keeps saying *good for her*.

Over Kentucky I take out a legal pad, because I can't do this on a screen. I write down what I know. I'm good at this part. This is the part where you find out what's still true.

*Nine weeks.*
*Northgate covenant: March. Probably sooner now.*
*Halvard: 1.1B. Needs Pip.*
*Pip: no.*
*Pip's plan: build here. 4,100/yr. Needs 6–8 up front.*
*I don't have 6–8. I have nothing. I have 34% that belongs to Northgate if this goes wrong.*
*Nashville: 420/wk.*

I look at that last line for a long time.

Under it I write *Tell her*.

I underline it. I underline it again. Then I sit there over Kentucky with the pen in my hand and do the thing I always do, which is plan the conversation. I plan where we'll be. I plan what I'll say first. I plan the slide in my head, God help me, I can see the slide, it has a title.

Then I put the pen down and look out the window at the dark.

Not a slide. Not the kitchen at midnight. Not the yard at eleven with a bowling hat on my knee.

Seven o'clock. In the room. Tomorrow, before the board. Two coffees, hers and mine. I'll get there at five to, like I used to. I'll sit in the chair by the radiator and when she comes in I'll tell her all of it before she takes her coat off, in the wrong order, the true way.

At one in the morning, back in the room across the hall from her, I'm still awake. My phone lights up on the nightstand. Not a call. A text.

*You haven't called in three nights. Are you all right?* — Simone.

I look at it a long time.

*Fine*, I type. *Sleeping.*

It's the first lie I've told Simone since May, and I tell it in the dark with my eyes wide open, and I set an alarm for five-thirty.
