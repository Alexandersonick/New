## Chapter 5 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

He knocks on the glass. On my glass, on my room, in my yard, my husband stands in the snow in his travel suit and knocks on the corner with one knuckle like he's at a drive-through.

I let him knock twice before I get up and open the door.

The cold comes in with him, and the smell of airplane and the cedar soap he's used since 2013, and he stands just inside the door on the mat my mother braided out of old bread bags and looks around: at my desk, which is mine again, at the empty space along the wall where Simone's boxes would be if I hadn't stacked them on four, at the vise and the weld sample and the file, and last of all at me.

"Hi," he says, and I say it back, and then he says, "You moved your office."

"I took my office. It's different."

"Pip." He does a thing with his hand, opening it, as if he's going to set something down on the desk between us and then realizes there's nothing in it. "There's a man from Minneapolis in my car."

"I know, he waved at me through the windshield."

"He's the CEO of Halvard."

"I know who Kurt Lindahl is, Adam. He was on the cover of *Furnishings Weekly* in a turtleneck."

"He's here to see the room."

"Well, here it is," I say, and hold my arms out to the walls.

He almost laughs. It comes up in him and gets pushed back down, which is new. Adam used to laugh at everything I said, even when it wasn't funny, especially when it wasn't funny, like a man throwing money at a street musician to make sure she stays on the corner.

"Why is the CEO of Halvard here to see my room?" I say.

"It was in the Monday deck."

"Which one?"

"All of them, since October. Pip, I sent you every single one. I *sent* them to you."

"I don't open them."

"I know you don't open them!" It comes out louder than he means. He glances over his shoulder at the car and brings it back down. "I know you don't, the software tells me, every week. Philippa Sobczak, not viewed. I see your name every Monday in a little gray box."

That stops me for a second, the idea of him at his desk on a Monday finding my name in a little gray box. I don't know what to do with that, so I take my phone out of my back pocket and open the folder called LATER and scroll down past four hundred emails to the one from Simone yesterday morning, and I open *North_Integration_v14.pdf*, standing in my own room with my husband dripping on my mother's rug.

Slide one is *Project North*, over a nice photo of the Lindqvist building at sunset that somebody paid for.

Slide two, *Transaction overview*: *Acquisition of 100% of Twodesk, Inc. by Halvard Group*, and under it a number, *$1.1B*, one point one billion dollars in a font somebody chose because it looked confident.

Slide three is a table of approvals. *Board: obtained (Dec); shareholders: 75% special approval; founders: Kerrigan (34%), committed, Sobczak (33%), pending.*

*Pending.* I'm on a slide, in a table, with the word *pending* next to my name in a gray box.

Slide eleven, because I keep scrolling, because I can't stop: *Manufacturing integration*. *Phase 1 (months 0–6): retain Clyde Park, Nashville. Phase 2 (months 6–18): consolidate production into Halvard facilities (Holland, MI; Monterrey, MX).* And a little footnote, in eight-point type, the kind of type people use when they'd like you to be in a hurry: *Corner product line to transition to Lite architecture (posted corner) to align with Halvard cost structure.*

The phone is cold in my hand, and I set it face down on the desk next to the vise.

"You're selling my company," I say.

"*Our* company."

"To a furniture conglomerate that's going to close my plant and put a post in my corner."

"That's phase two, that's eighteen months out, those are planning assumptions, they're not—"

"There's a slide, Adam."

"There's a slide for everything. There's a slide for the holiday party."

"Is there a slide for when you were going to tell me?"

That's the one, the question I'll be asking the ceiling for the next while, and he doesn't answer it. He stands there with snow melting off his shoulders onto the bread-bag rug, and out past the corner Kurt Lindahl is getting out of the car and putting on a pair of leather gloves and looking at the yard like he's going to buy it.

"I needed seventy-five," Adam says finally, quietly. "I've got sixty-seven, with the board and Ruth. I needed you."

"Then you've got sixty-seven," I say. "I'm a no."

He closes his eyes and opens them again.

"You can't just be a no," he says. "You haven't even read it. You opened it thirty seconds ago."

"I can just be a no. That's what the seventy-five is for. It's so somebody can just be a no." I fold my arms. "I wrote that clause too. You stood behind me while I typed it. You said nobody would ever read it."

"Pip, listen to me."

I hold up the phone. "Who else has read version fourteen?"

"The board, Ruth, Gordon." He counts them off on his fingers and stops for exactly as long as it takes to decide not to lie. "Simone built it."

"Of course she did."

"She built it because I asked her to build it. Don't put that on her, Pip."

"I'm not putting anything on anybody. I'm reading a slide."

"Simone's running integration," I say. "Integration of us."

"That's what integration is, Pip. You don't understand what's—"

"Explain it, then."

And there it is again, a door opening in his face and him closing it. He decides not to tell me something, right there in front of me. He's done that a hundred times since Nashville and I've never caught what's behind it. I'm good at reading where a thing is going to crack, in a weld or in a person, and I've never been able to read the inside of that particular door.

"It's a good deal," he says instead, and his voice changes, it gets that warm, round, Rotary-luncheon thing it gets. "It's a great deal, Pip. It's a life-changing number for both of us, for everybody with options, for Gordon, for the crew."

"Don't pitch me."

"I'm not pitching you."

"You're pitching me. Your voice went up into your nose. You do the nose voice for Rotary."

"I do not have a nose voice." He stops, because he does, and we both heard it. "Fine. Then here's the other thing, and you know it already. You can't sell either. The lock-up runs twenty-four months from the D. You can't walk out with your third, you can't sell it to anybody without the board, and the board wants the deal. So you can be a no all you want, but you can't leave, and I can't sell without you." He spreads his hands. "Congratulations. We're married."

It's a good line, the best one he's said to me in two years. I hate that it is, and I keep my hands flat on the desk.

"Get your man out of the snow," I say. "He's going to freeze to the yard."

Kurt Lindahl ducks under the header when he comes in, though he doesn't need to. Tall men do that in my rooms. I build them seven-foot-two inside, but tall men walk into a small building and assume it's going to hit them on the head, and when it doesn't, they get generous about it.

He looks the rug over before he gets around to my face. "Is this handmade?"

"My mother made it out of bread bags."

"We own a textiles brand in Wisconsin, recycled fibers. She should talk to our people."

"She'd want to know what you pay."

"Everybody wants to know what we pay," Kurt says, pleased, as if I've passed a test I didn't sign up for. He peels off a glove to shake my hand. His hand is warm, which means he's been keeping it in his pocket in the car especially. "Kurt. It's such an honor, Pip. I've wanted to meet you since October. Your corner is the single most elegant detail in residential product I've seen in ten years. I mean that."

"Thank you," I say, and he's already pointing past me. "May I?"

He means the glass. He walks over to the corner without waiting for me to answer and puts his bare hand flat on the joint where the two panes meet, exactly where I put mine at four in the morning on the trailer. Irene wiped mine off first thing this morning with the Windex, the way Lolo said she would. He looks up at the roof where there's no post holding anything, and down at the floor, and shakes his head slowly, smiling, like a man watching a very good magician.

"How?" he says.

"There's a moment frame in the walls," I say. "Welded steel, buried in the stud cavity, both sides, meeting at a knee under the floor. It carries the roof around the corner. The crew calls it the knee."

"The knee," he says, and his whole face lights up. "And it's welded by hand?"

"By four people, here, under those yellow curtains." I nod toward the plant. "They're the best in Michigan, maybe the country. It takes about eleven hours a unit."

"Eleven hours." His face does something small and quick, a calculator under the charm. "And in a high-volume environment—"

"You'd put a post in it."

He laughs, delighted, caught. "I'd look at the cost architecture holistically."

"You'd put a post in it," I say again, and next to me Adam makes a sound through his nose that I haven't heard since about 2022, which is the sound he makes when he's trying not to laugh in a meeting. When I turn my head, he's studying the ceiling.

Kurt takes his hand off the glass and leaves a print. I'll get it later.

"I love this," he says, to the room. "I love the story, two founders and a garage, and now this, moving the room out of HQ overnight and back to the plant floor. It's a hell of a statement. It's very authentic. Did you know it's on the *Grand Rapids Ledger* website?"

"I posted it," I say.

Adam's head comes around.

"At six fifty-two," I tell him, "from my account. The kid's footage, the good one."

"You *posted*—"

"It's my room and it's my video," I say. "It's a beautiful video, Adam. You should read the comments. Somebody wants to know if we deliver to Ohio."

I didn't know I was going to say that until I said it. That's not true either. I knew a minute before I posted it, sitting on the trailer steps with Tavi's file open on my phone and the sun not up yet and my room coming out of the building on a three-inch screen. I knew that if I didn't post it, somebody would cover the bolt plates with a rug and tell a man from Minneapolis it was being refurbished.

Kurt looks from me to Adam and back again, and then he smiles wider, which is how you know a man like Kurt has decided something.

"Well," he says, "it's certainly brand-positive."

"Careful," I tell him. "Around him, I mean." I tip my head at my husband. "He'd sell you your own chair."

"I've heard," Kurt says. "I've been looking forward to it." He pulls his glove back on, finger by finger. "Walk me down your knee line sometime? Not today. Sometime."

"Sometime," I say, the way my mother says it to the Jehovah's Witnesses.

They leave for the eleven o'clock tour of a building with no room in it. Adam lets Kurt go ahead to the car and stops in the door, one hand on the frame, the snow blowing in past him onto the rug.

"The city's going to fine you for the permit," he says.

"Walt says the permit's good."

"The permit's for rooftop units."

"Then they'll fine me, and I'll pay it."

"Gordon's going to have a stroke."

"Gordon has a stroke every quarter. He's very healthy."

"Ruth's going to want to know who paid for the crane."

"I paid for the crane. Walt takes pierogi."

He almost smiles, and catches it, and puts it away. His eyes go over the desk, the vise, my phone, and then to the inside of the door, where the writing is, the three lines in carpenter's pencil in two handwritings, and his face goes still in a way I don't have a name for.

"Come to the board on the nineteenth, Pip," he says. "Ruth's putting you on the agenda."

"For what?"

"Censure," he says, and he says it like he's sorry, and then he's gone across the yard through the snow to the warm car, and the man from Minneapolis waves to me through the windshield like we're all on the same team.

I close the door and sit down at my desk and pick up the file.

Through the glass corner, Kurt Lindahl's handprint is still on the window, five fingers and a palm, right at the joint.

"He loves it," I tell the knee frame in the vise. "We're in so much trouble."
