## Chapter 16 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Ruth catches me in the hall outside the boardroom before I can get to the stairs.

"A word," she says, and steers me by the elbow into the little kitchenette on six where the old Lindqvists kept their sherry and we keep a Keurig nobody cleans. She shuts the door with her heel. She's shorter than me by a foot and she runs four miles a day and she backs me up against the counter like a woman in a nature documentary.

"What was that?" she says.

"What was what?"

"You voted against the censure."

"It failed."

"I know it failed, Adam, I was there, I counted. Two-two. You were the two." She doesn't raise her voice. Ruth never raises her voice. She lowers it, which is worse, like a car downshifting before it passes you. "Kurt Lindahl watched the CEO of the company he's buying vote with the founder who's blocking the deal. On camera. He wrote something down. Did you see him write something down?"

"I saw him."

"He writes things down when he's recalculating."

"Let him recalculate."

"It's my fund, Adam." For a second something shows under the public-radio voice, something tired and older than fifty-eight. "Lakeshore has a hundred and forty million dollars in this company. I have pension money in this company. Teachers. Firefighters in Toledo. I have to go back to Ann Arbor and tell my partners that the CEO, who has borrowed nine million dollars against his own stock in a covenant that's going to trip in March, just voted to protect his wife from a symbolic word in the minutes. I need you to tell me which side you're on."

I look at her. I look past her at the Keurig, which has a sticky note on it in Gordon's handwriting that says *PLEASE DESCALE ME*.

"I'm on hers," I say.

Ruth stares at me for a long moment. Then she nods, slowly, like a woman who's been told the price of something and is deciding whether to pay it.

"Then you'd better help her find seven point four million dollars," she says, "in thirty days," and opens the door and goes.

She isn't in the stairwell. She isn't on four. She isn't at her truck in the lot behind the building where she always parks, because her truck isn't in the lot. Simone, coming down the hall toward me from the glass room with her notebook pressed to her chest, opens her mouth to say something and I put a hand up and she closes it, and I go down the stairs two at a time, the old iron stairs that smell like pigeons and radiator paint, all the way to the ramp.

I find her on level three of the Monroe Street ramp, standing next to my car.

Not hers. Mine. My black Audi with the Grand Valley sticker in the back window she gave me as a joke when we got married, because I didn't go to Grand Valley, I went to Western, and she said I needed to be loyal to something local. She's leaning against the driver's door with her arms crossed and her coat open and her face as still as the side of a building, and the ramp is cold and gray and smells like exhaust, and a woman getting out of a Subaru two spaces down takes one look at us and gets back into the Subaru.

"You moved your truck," I say.

"It's on four. I walked down."

"Why?"

"Because you'd find me at my truck." She doesn't uncross her arms. "I wanted to be where you'd have to walk up to me."

So I walk up to her. I stop about four feet away, which is the distance I'd stop from a horse I didn't know.

"You pledged your shares," she says.

"Yes."

"All of them."

"All of them."

"In October."

"October ninth. It was a Thursday."

"I know what day of the week it was, Adam. I was there. I was in the house." Her voice is level. It's so level I can hear what it's costing her to keep it there. "I made eggs. I ate them over the sink. You came in at one and went upstairs. You'd just put your whole life on a piece of paper in Chicago and you went *upstairs*."

"I didn't want you scared."

"You didn't want me in the room."

"That's not—"

"Then what?" She uncrosses her arms. She puts her hands flat against the car door behind her, like she's holding herself up, or holding the car down. "Tell me what. Because I've been sitting at that walnut table for an hour doing the arithmetic, and I keep getting the same answer. You decided. You decided we needed nine million dollars and you decided where to get it and you decided what to put up and you decided not to tell me. That's four decisions. You made all four alone."

"Not alone," I say, and I hear it as I say it, and I'd give a great deal to have it back.

She goes very still.

"Who knew?" she says.

"Gordon. He had to. He's the CFO."

"Who else?"

I could lie. I've been lying since October in small ways that I told myself were scheduling. I could say *nobody*. She'd believe it for about a week.

"Simone," I say.

Pip doesn't do anything. She doesn't move, she doesn't make a sound. Her face doesn't change. That's what's terrible. I've seen Pip angry, I've seen her throw a framing square across a shop and stick it in a wall, I've seen her cry exactly twice. I've never seen her do nothing like this, nothing at all, as if somebody had turned her off at the wall.

"She had the numbers," I say. "She does operations, Pip, she was there in August when Nashville started bleeding, she built the model, she—"

"Did she call you, or did you call her?"

"What?"

"In October. On the ninth. A Thursday. You signed the papers at the kitchen counter, I'm guessing, because that's where you sign things, at my end, because it's got the better light. And then you called someone." She tilts her head. "Did you call her? At one in the morning?"

I don't answer.

"That's interesting," Pip says, very quietly.

The woman in the Subaru starts her car and backs out very slowly, looking straight ahead, and drives down the ramp to level two with the care of a woman leaving a crime scene.

"I came at seven," I say.

It comes out of me wrong. It comes out like an excuse. I didn't mean it as one. I meant it as the only true thing I've got in my pocket.

"I know," Pip says. "Benny told me. The thermos was on my desk."

"I was going to tell you. All of it. Northgate, the covenant, all of it, before the board, I was going to sit in the chair and tell you before you took your coat off—"

"I was late," she says.

We look at each other across four feet of cold concrete.

"I was late," she says again. "I was bowling. I slept through my alarm. I drank your coffee at seven-thirty and it was still hot. You made it in my pot."

"I made it in your pot."

"It's terrible coffee, Adam."

"I know. I've always hated it."

"You never said."

"You never asked."

That one lands on both of us. I watch it land on her. I feel it land on me, somewhere around the sternum, like walking into a beam in the dark.

"Why didn't you knock?" she says. "In October. On the ninth. Why didn't you come up the stairs and knock on my door?"

There are a lot of answers. I've got them lined up the way I line up answers for investors, in order of persuasiveness. *You were asleep. It was late. You had the plant. I didn't want to load it on you. It was my mistake to fix.* I could give her any of them and they'd all be a little bit true.

"Because you'd have picked it up," I say. "The heavy end. You always pick up the heavy end, Pip. You'd have taken it off me and carried it, and I'd have let you, because I always let you. And then Nashville would have been yours too. Everything I broke would have been yours to fix."

She looks at me for a long time.

"That's my end," she says finally. "The heavy end. That's my end, Adam. I'm a welder."

She pushes herself off my car. She walks past me, close, and stops, and for a second we're standing shoulder to shoulder facing opposite directions on level three of the Monroe Street ramp, like two people in a duel who forgot to turn around.

"You voted no," she says, not looking at me. "On the censure."

"I did."

"Ruth's going to make you pay for that."

"She already sent the invoice."

Something happens in her face. In profile, at the corner of her mouth, it's there and gone, like a pilot light catching.

"Thirty days," she says.

"I know."

"I'm going to find seven point four million dollars in thirty days."

"I know you are."

"And you're not going to decide anything about this company without me. Not one thing. Not a supplier, not a hire, not a coffee order. You're going to tell me before. Not after. Before."

"Okay."

"Don't say okay like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a man agreeing to terms." She turns her head and looks at me, and her eyes are wet and her face is perfectly calm, and the combination is the most frightening thing I've seen in my life. "I'm not setting terms. I'm telling you what it's like to be married to me. It's like that. You tell me before."

Then she walks up the ramp toward level four and her truck, and her boots go up the concrete, one step after another, and she doesn't look back, and I stand there by my car until I can't hear them anymore.

My phone buzzes in my pocket. I take it out.

*Are you all right? I saw you leave. I am in the glass room if you want to talk. — S.*

I look at it for a long time. I look up at the ceiling of the ramp, at the pipes and the fluorescent tubes and a pigeon sitting on a sprinkler head, looking down at me with one orange eye like it's waiting to see what I'll do.

I don't answer Simone. I put the phone in my pocket.

Then I take it out again, and open my texts, and scroll down past Gordon and Ruth and Kurt Lindahl and the Northgate guy and my mother, all the way to PIP, the photo of her in safety glasses on the roof of the Lindqvist building the night we hung the sign, laughing at something off-camera that was me.

I type *I'm sorry*. I delete it. It's a thing I'd say after.

I type: *Seven tomorrow? I'll bring both coffees.*

I look at it. I don't send it. I stand there on level three with my thumb over the button, and the pigeon watches me, and I think, *she said before*. Before, not after. And coffee at seven isn't before anything. It's a thing we used to have. You can't ask somebody back into a room you let go cold by texting them about the coffee.

I delete it.

Then I type the only thing I can think of that's true and in the right order.

*Northgate's number is 312-555-0148. The loan officer is Neil Pruitt. The papers are in the second drawer of my desk at home, left side, under the Nashville lease. The password to my laptop is your mother's maiden name and the year we got married. I won't do anything about any of it without you.*

I send that one.

It sits there on the screen, delivered. Not read. I watch it until the screen goes dark.

Above me the pigeon lifts off the sprinkler head, beats twice in the low concrete air, and goes out through the open side of the ramp into the snow.
