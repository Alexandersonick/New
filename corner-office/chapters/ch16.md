## Chapter 16 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Ruth catches me in the hall outside the boardroom before I can get to the stairs.

"A word," she says, and steers me by the elbow into the little kitchenette on six where the old Lindqvists kept their sherry and we keep a Keurig nobody cleans. She shuts the door with her heel. She's shorter than me by a foot and runs four miles a day, and she backs me up against the counter like a border collie with one sheep.

"What was that?" she says.

"What was what?" I say, though I know.

"You voted against the censure."

"It failed, if that helps."

"I know it failed, Adam, I was there, I counted two-two, and you were the two." She doesn't raise her voice; Ruth never raises her voice. She lowers it, which is worse, like a car downshifting before it passes you. "Kurt Lindahl watched the CEO of the company he's buying vote with the founder who's blocking the deal, on camera, and he wrote something down. Did you see him write something down?"

"I saw him write it down."

"He writes things down when he's recalculating."

"Then let him recalculate, it's good for his heart."

"It's my fund you're joking about, Adam." For a second something shows under the public-radio voice, something tired and older than fifty-eight. "Lakeshore has a hundred and forty million dollars in this company. I have pension money in this company, teachers, firefighters in Toledo. I have to go back to Ann Arbor and tell my partners that the CEO, who has borrowed nine million dollars against his own stock in a covenant that's going to trip in March, just voted to protect his wife from a symbolic word in the minutes."

She takes her phone out of her blazer pocket and turns the screen toward me. It's a text from Kurt, sent while the rest of us were still pushing our chairs back from the walnut: *Is he going to be a problem?*

"He wants an answer by tonight," Ruth says. "So do I. Which side are you on?"

I look past her at the Keurig, which has a sticky note on it in Gordon's handwriting that says *PLEASE DESCALE ME*.

"I'm on hers," I say.

Ruth stares at me, and then she nods slowly, like a woman who's been told the price of something and is deciding whether to pay it.

"And what do I tell Kurt?"

"Tell him I'm a problem."

"I'm going to tell him you're tired." She types it with one thumb, fast, and puts the phone back in her blazer. "And you'd better help her find seven point four million dollars in thirty days, because I can't keep calling you tired."

She opens the door. On her way out she jabs the Keurig's brew button with one knuckle, and the machine groans like something in a cellar and produces nothing at all.

Pip isn't in the stairwell, and she isn't on four, and she isn't at her truck in the lot behind the building where she always parks, because her truck isn't in the lot. Simone, coming down the hall toward me from the glass room with her notebook pressed to her chest, opens her mouth to say something, and I put a hand up and she closes it, and I go down the stairs two at a time, the old iron stairs that smell like pigeons and radiator paint, all the way to the ramp.

I find her on level three of the Monroe Avenue ramp, standing next to my car.

It's my black Audi she's leaning on, the one with the Grand Valley sticker in the back window that she gave me as a joke when we got married, because I didn't go to Grand Valley, I went to Western, and she said I needed to be loyal to something local. She's got her arms crossed and her coat open and her face closed for business, and the ramp is cold and gray and smells like exhaust and somebody's old french fries, and a woman getting out of a Subaru two spaces down takes one look at us and gets back into the Subaru.

"You moved your truck," I say.

"It's up on four. I parked it there and walked down."

"How'd you know which car was mine?"

"I put a sticker on it eleven years ago so I'd always know." She doesn't uncross her arms. "I wanted to be where you'd have to walk up to me, instead of the other way around."

So I walk up to her and stop about four feet away, which is the distance I'd stop from a horse I didn't know.

"You pledged your shares," she says.

"Yes."

"All of them, in October?"

"October eighth, it was a Thursday," I say, like I'm reading it off a receipt.

"I know what day of the week it was, Adam, I was in the house." Her voice is so level I can hear what it's costing her to keep it there. "I made eggs and ate them over the sink, and you came in at one, and I heard you in the kitchen for an hour, and then you went upstairs. You'd just put your whole life on a piece of paper in Chicago and you went *upstairs*."

"I didn't want you scared."

"You didn't want me in the room."

"That's not—"

"Then what?" She uncrosses her arms and puts her hands flat against the car door behind her, like she's holding herself up, or holding the car down. "Tell me what, because I've been sitting at that walnut table for an hour doing the arithmetic and I keep getting the same answer. You decided we needed nine million dollars, and you decided where to get it, and you decided what to put up, and you decided not to tell me. That's four decisions, and you made all four alone."

"Not alone," I say, and I hear it as I say it, and I'd give a great deal to have it back.

She goes very still against the car and says, "Who knew?"

"Gordon, because he had to, he's the CFO."

"Who else?"

I could lie; I've been lying since October in small ways I told myself were scheduling. I could say *nobody*, and she'd believe it for about a week.

"Simone," I say.

Pip doesn't move or make a sound, and nothing in her face changes, and that's what's terrible. I've seen Pip angry. I've seen her throw a framing square across a shop and stick it in a wall, and I've seen her cry exactly twice, but I've never seen her do nothing like this, nothing at all, as if somebody had turned her off at the wall.

"She had the numbers," I say. "She does operations, Pip, she was there in August when Nashville started bleeding, she built the model, she—"

"Did she call you, or did you call her?"

"What?" I say, which buys me nothing.

"In October, on the eighth, a Thursday. You signed the papers at the kitchen counter, I'm guessing, because that's where you sign things, at my end, because it's got the better light, and then you called someone." She tilts her head at me. "Did you call her at one in the morning?"

I don't answer, and somewhere down on level two a car alarm starts and stops.

"That's interesting," Pip says, very quietly.

The woman in the Subaru starts her car and backs out very slowly, looking straight ahead, and drives down the ramp to level two with the care of a woman leaving a crime scene.

"I came at seven," I say.

It comes out of me wrong, like an excuse, and I didn't mean it as one. I meant it as the only true thing I've got in my pocket.

"I know, Benny told me," Pip says. "The thermos was on my desk."

"I was going to tell you all of it, Northgate, the covenant, everything, before the board. I was going to sit in the chair and tell you while you were still taking your coat off—"

"I was late," she says.

We look at each other across four feet of cold concrete.

"I was late," she says again. "I was bowling, and I slept through my alarm, and I drank your coffee at half past seven and it was still hot. You made it in my pot."

"I made it in your pot."

"It's terrible coffee, Adam."

"I know, I've always hated it."

"You never said."

"You never asked."

That one lands on both of us. I watch it land on her, and I feel it land on me somewhere around the sternum, like walking into a beam in the dark.

"Why didn't you knock?" she says. "In October, on the eighth, why didn't you come up the stairs and knock on my door?"

There are a lot of answers. I've got them lined up the way I line up answers for investors, in order of persuasiveness: *you were asleep, it was late, you had the plant, I didn't want to load it on you, it was my mistake to fix.* I could give her any of them and every one would close. Strike that. Every one would be a little bit true.

"Because you were right," I say. "About Nashville. You told Gordon it would eat us, and it ate us, and every dollar I borrowed went into feeding it. If I'd knocked, I'd have had to stand in your doorway and say *you were right and I was wrong and it's costing us the company*. I couldn't. I'd rather borrow nine million dollars against everything I own than say that sentence to you."

A car comes down the ramp behind me, slow, and its headlights slide across her boots and away before she answers.

"You'd rather owe a bank in Chicago," she says finally, "than owe me an *I told you so*."

"Yes."

"I wouldn't have said it."

"I know," I say. "That's what made it worse."

She pushes herself off my car and walks past me, close, and stops, and for a second we're standing shoulder to shoulder facing opposite directions, like two people in a duel who forgot to turn around.

"You voted no on the censure," she says, not looking at me.

"I did."

"Ruth's going to make you pay for that."

"She already sent the invoice."

"What did it say?"

"It asked which side I'm on." I wait, and she waits, both of us looking at different walls of the ramp. "I told her yours. Standing next to a Keurig that needs descaling, so it wasn't much of a ceremony."

In profile, the dimple in her left cheek shows, the one she gets from her father, there and gone, like a pilot light catching.

"Thirty days," she says, to the concrete in front of her.

"I know."

"I'm going to find seven point four million dollars."

"I know you are," I say.

"I don't know how yet." She turns her head, and her eyes are wet and her face is perfectly calm, and the combination is the most frightening thing I've seen in my life. "I haven't said that to you since Nashville. *I don't know.* You should write it down somewhere."

Then she walks up the ramp toward level four and her truck, and her boots go up the concrete, one step after another, and she doesn't look back, and I stand there by my car until I can't hear them anymore.

My phone buzzes in my pocket, and I take it out.

*Are you all right? I saw you leave. I am in the glass room if you want to talk. — S.*

I read it twice and look up at the ceiling of the ramp, at the pipes and the fluorescent tubes and a pigeon sitting on a sprinkler head, looking down at me with one orange eye like it's waiting to see what I'll do.

The phone goes back in my pocket with Simone unanswered.

Then I take it out again, and open my texts, and scroll down past Gordon and Ruth and Kurt Lindahl and the Northgate guy and my mother, all the way to PIP, the photo of her in safety glasses on the roof of the Lindqvist building the night we hung the sign, laughing at something off-camera that was me.

I type *I'm sorry* and delete it, because it's a thing you say after. I type *I voted for you* and delete that too, because it reads like a yard sign.

I type *Seven tomorrow, both coffees on me* and look at it for a while without sending it, because you can't ask somebody back into a room you let go cold by texting them about the coffee. I delete it.

I think about Odell in his glass box over the empty line in La Vergne. *She doesn't mind the idiot so much. She minds finding out from the parking lot.* I'm standing in a parking ramp, which is almost funny.

So I'll stop being the parking lot. Whatever I know, she hears it from me first, before I act on it, every time, whatever it costs. Nobody asked me to; it's just the only repair I can think of that doesn't need her to do anything.

Then I type the only thing I can think of that's true and in the right order.

*Northgate's number is 312-555-0148. The loan officer is Neil Pruitt. The papers are in the second drawer of my desk at home, left side, under the Nashville lease. The password to my laptop is your mother's maiden name and the year we got married. I won't do anything about any of it without you.*

I send that one as it is, which makes it the only thing I've sent all year that nobody polished.

It sits there on the screen, delivered and not read, and I watch it until the screen goes dark.

The pigeon lifts off the sprinkler head above me, beats twice, and goes out the open side of the ramp into the snow.
