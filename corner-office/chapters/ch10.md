## Chapter 10 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Kurt Lindahl orders the bone-in ribeye, medium rare, and then asks the waiter, a kid named Tyler with a neck tattoo of a compass, whether the beef is from Michigan.

"I think it's from a cow, sir," says Tyler.

Kurt laughs so hard he has to put his napkin to his mouth. I laugh too, a beat behind him, the laugh you do at the table with the man who's writing the check. Tyler doesn't laugh, because Tyler has said a true thing and doesn't understand why it's funny, and he goes away to find out about the cow.

We're at Moretti's on Ottawa, in the back room with the red leather booths and the photos of Gerald Ford shaking hands with people who are all dead now. I booked the back room because it's quiet. Kurt likes quiet when he's about to say a number.

"So," he says, and folds his own on the tablecloth. "The hands."

"The hands," I say, and I already don't like how it sounds in here.

"I've been thinking about her all week." He spreads butter on a roll with great care, all the way to the edges. "I watched the video eleven times. My wife watched it with me. My wife said, *That woman isn't for sale, Kurt.* My wife is very perceptive."

"Your wife sounds right to me."

"Everybody's for sale, Adam. You and I both know that. Not for money, necessarily. For the right thing." He sets the knife down. "So what's the right thing?"

That's the question I've been asking myself since she said *I'm a no* in a room in a yard with snow melting off my coat. I'm a closer: I find out what people want and I give it to them in a shape they can say yes to. I've done it with pension funds, with a Saudi family office, with a woman in Holland who owned the only lot zoned for our first showroom and wanted us to name the parking lot after her late poodle. We did. It's called Biscuit Lot, and there's a plaque.

I don't know what Pip wants. I haven't known since Nashville. I used to know what she wanted before she did.

"Clyde Park, Kurt," I tell him. "She wants the plant."

"Done," Kurt says, without even blinking. "Three years, we'll put it in the agreement. No consolidation of Clyde Park for thirty-six months post-close."

"Thirty-six months is phase two plus eighteen."

"It's a long time in manufacturing."

"It's not long to Benny Szymanski."

"I don't know who that is."

"He drives the forklift at Clyde Park. Fifty-eight, with a daughter at Grand Valley."

Kurt smiles at me like I've made a lovely point at a dinner party. "We'll make it thirty-six months and a retention pool, a generous one. And for Pip," he says, and leans in over the bread, "Chief Design Officer, Halvard Residential. All eleven brands. Her own studio, anywhere she wants. Grand Rapids, Minneapolis, Milan. Whatever she wants it to look like. She can design it."

*She can design it.* I said that to her. A week ago, on four, with a coffee tray in my hands. *We'll get you something better. You can design it.* The sentence comes back across the table to me in another man's mouth, and now I know how it sounds, which is like handing someone a coloring book after you've taken their house.

"She'll say no," I say.

"Make it sound like yes."

"I don't know how."

He tilts his head at me. I've said it twice now in a week. *I don't know.* It's like a cough I can't get rid of.

"Then let me help you with that." He takes his phone out of his jacket and turns it around on the tablecloth so it faces me, and slides it past the bread basket. "Our studio put this together this afternoon for the spring catalog, very rough."

It's a render of the Corner, my wife's Corner, set in a backyard that doesn't exist, all blue sky and tasteful mulch, with a Halvard wordmark in the bottom right and under it, smaller, *by Twodesk*. The glass walls are right and the roof line is right, and at the joint where the two walls meet, a post, slim, brushed bronze, the color of a nice faucet.

"There's a post," I say.

"There's a very elegant post. Our engineers say you can't see it from the house."

"You can see it from inside the room."

"Adam," he says, and takes the phone back and puts it face up beside his plate, where it lights up twice while we talk and he doesn't look at it. "I'll build her original for every deposit on the books. Every one of those people gets the room from the video, and Ruth knows that. After that it's a catalog, and catalogs have posts. Nobody in Phoenix is going to sit in the yard and cry about a post."

"Somebody in Duluth will cry about it."

"Then I'll sell Duluth the Long." He picks his knife back up. "I don't need her signature tonight. I need yours. My board meets on the twenty-first. I can tell them I have a CEO in Grand Rapids who can deliver his co-founder, or I can tell them I have a very nice video. Which one do I tell them?"

The waiter's coming with the plates. Kurt doesn't look at the waiter. He sits there with his knife in his hand, pleasant, waiting, the way a room waits after you've asked it for money.

"Tell them the first one," I say.

I say it before the waiter gets to the table. It comes out that fast, and I pick up my water glass so I have something to do with my hand.

"Good," Kurt says, warmly. "You sold me a garage in Grand Rapids for one point one billion dollars. You'll figure it out, and afterward you'll be a very rich man, Adam, in the bank and not just on paper."

I have eleven thousand dollars in the bank. I smile at him over the water glass like a man with four hundred million.

The ribeye comes on a wooden board. It's from a farm in Ionia County, Tyler reports. The cow's name is unavailable.

I get home at five to midnight. The house is dark except for the kitchen, where the light over the stove is on, and when I come in through the back she's sitting at the counter at her end in my old Grand Valley sweatshirt and her wool socks, with the moka pot on the stove going *blup* and a mug in front of her and a pad of graph paper covered in drawings.

She doesn't look up from the paper.

"You're up late," I say, from the door.

"I'm up, and so are you."

"It's midnight, and you're making coffee."

"I'm making coffee at midnight, write it down."

I put my keys down. I take my coat off and hang it on the hook by the door, the one she put up in 2019 by drilling straight into the brick, and I stand there at my end.

The drawings are of the knee. I know it from across the room. I've watched her draw the knee a thousand times: on napkins, on whiteboards, on the back of a Costco receipt in the truck at a red light. She draws it like other people doodle a cat. This one is different though. There are numbers all down the side in her blocky little handwriting, and arrows, and a box around something that says *CP 2ND SHIFT?* and another one that says *6 DAYS?*

I nod at the pad. "What's that you're drawing?"

"Arithmetic."

"And what's the arithmetic for?"

She turns the page over.

Okay, that's fair, that's a fair move. I've been turning pages over on her since the spring before last and calling it strategy.

"How was dinner?" she asks.

"The ribeye was from Ionia."

"Good for the ribeye."

"Kurt wants to meet you again."

"He met me, and he put his hand on my window."

"He wants to meet you properly, over dinner. He has an offer, Pip, and it's—" It's happening. My voice is climbing up into the place she calls the nose voice, the Rotary voice, the voice I used on the poodle lady, and I can't get it back down. "It's a real offer. He'll put Clyde Park in the agreement. Thirty-six months, no consolidation, in writing. There's a retention pool for the crew. And for you, Chief Design Officer, Halvard Residential, all eleven brands, your own studio, wherever you want, you can design—"

"Adam."

"Design it from the ground up, Milan if you want, you always said you wanted to see—"

"You're pitching me."

I stop, and the moka pot gurgles. She gets up and turns off the burner and pours the coffee into her mug, slowly, the black sludge of it, and sits back down and doesn't drink it.

"Strike that," I say. "Sorry, strike all of it."

"You keep saying that," she says.

"Saying what?"

"*Strike that*, under your breath. You've been doing it for a year. You say a thing and then you mutter *strike that* like you're in a courtroom." She wraps both hands around the mug. "Who are you talking to?"

Nobody's ever asked me that. I didn't know anybody could hear it.

"I'm talking to me," I say.

"What do you say back?"

"Usually I say *try again*."

She lifts her eyes from the mug. She's tired, and there are lines by her eyes that weren't there when we met at twenty-five in the back of a bar on Bridge Street where she was explaining to a man who designed office chairs that his chair was wrong. She has a smudge of graphite on her cheekbone. She has always had a smudge of graphite somewhere. I used to find it on her neck, in bed, and lick my thumb and get it off, and she'd say *leave it, it's structural*.

"Try again, then," she says.

I stand there with my hands on the counter.

The true thing is sitting in my chest, the shape of a stone you swallowed as a kid that your mother swore would come out on its own. It has to do with coffee at seven, and how it ended, and the version of that I've been telling myself ever since. I can't get it up my throat. Not in a kitchen at midnight with my coat still damp on the hook.

"I miss you," I say instead.

It's true. It's just not the true thing.

She takes that in without doing anything with her face. Pip's face, when she doesn't want you to read it, is like the side of a building.

"I'm in the next room," she says.

"I know which room you're in."

"I've been in the next room for two years, Adam."

"I *know*."

"Then you don't miss me. You just haven't come in."

She picks up the graph paper, keeping it turned over, and her mug, and she goes past me at the end of the counter, close, so close I can smell the cedar from the room on her sweater, my sweater, and the graphite and the coffee. She stops for a second at the door to the hall. I think she's going to say something else.

"Kurt can't have the corner," she says. "Tell him that, it's not for sale. It's not a corner if you put a post in it, it's a wall that gave up."

I think of a brushed-bronze post the color of a nice faucet, on a phone face up beside a plate. I don't tell her about it.

Then she goes up the good stairs, and the ninth one creaks.

I stand in the kitchen while the stove light hums and the radiator knocks, and the moka pot cools on the stove with an inch still in it.

At one o'clock I'm sitting on the edge of the bed in the room across the hall from her, in the dark, with my phone in my hand. Simone will be up, Simone is always up. I could call and we'd go over the Kurt numbers and I'd tell her about the cow, and she'd say *that is not funny* in the way that means it's a little funny, and I'd feel better.

I keep the phone in my hand. Through the wall, two layers of plaster and a closet full of winter coats, there's no sound at all.

I put the phone face down on the nightstand and lie back on the bed in my clothes.
