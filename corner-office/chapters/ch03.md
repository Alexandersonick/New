## Chapter 3 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The Clyde Park plant sounds like a kitchen the night before a wedding, if the kitchen were the size of a hockey rink and everybody in it were holding a welding torch.

I hear it from the parking lot. I always hear it from the parking lot, the long rising whine of the panel saw, the press brake going *chunk* like a church door, the hiss and crackle from the knee line where four welders stand in a row under the yellow curtains making the hidden frames that let my corner stand up without a post. I park the truck by the dumpsters where I always park it and sit there with the engine running and listen to it for a while. Some people have a beach. I have this.

Inside, Irene Kwiatkowski looks up from the glazing table and lifts her chin at me, which from Irene is a parade. Benny Szymanski is running the forklift with a cigarette behind his ear that he will not light, because I'll fire him, and he'll pretend it's because of the rule. He swings the forks around and idles up next to me.

"Boss. Thought you were downtown today."

"I was downtown today."

"Short day downtown." He looks at my face and then at the forks. "You want me to move something heavy for you? I'm feeling strong."

"Move the Traverse City unit to finishing, Benny."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant," I say, and he backs away beeping, which is Benny's version of a hug. Somebody's radio is playing Fleetwood Mac, the song every radio in Michigan plays at least once an hour in case you've forgotten you're sad.

I walk the floor. I walk it every morning. Today it's afternoon and I walk it anyway, past the wall framing jigs and the roof cassette line and the finishing bay where three Corners sit in a row under work lights, nearly done, their glass still wearing the blue protective film. One of them is going to a retired dentist in Ada who wants to paint in it. One is going to a couple in Traverse City who are getting divorced and need a place for him to live in the backyard while they figure out who gets the house. I know because I read every order. Nobody else reads every order. Adam says reading every order doesn't scale.

At my bench in the back corner, by the big rolling door to the yard, there's a weld sample I was in the middle of cutting on Friday, a section of the knee frame clamped in a vise with its cross-section sawn clean so you can see the bead. I sit down and pick up my file and look at it.

I can't work. The file goes back on the bench.

I get up and go out through the rolling door into the yard and there's a stack of pallets against the fence, and I kick one of them. Then I kick it again. I keep kicking it until it's the pallet's fault. That's a thing I do. I've done it since I was fourteen and my brothers told me girls can't weld, which I'd have argued about more if I hadn't been fourteen and holding a stick welder upside down. Joe found me kicking the garage door that night and stood there in his socks and didn't say anything. Then he went inside and came back out with my father's welding hood and put it on my head backwards so I couldn't see, and said, "There. Now you look like a welder."

The pallet has a crack in it now and I feel a little better, though not much.

My phone buzzes in my back pocket. It's Adam.

*Missed you in the meeting. We need to talk properly — tonight? I'm in Chicago til Tues PM. Call me after 10.*

*We need to talk properly*. He's the one who started talking improperly, as a policy, around the time the company passed two hundred employees and his calendar went from a calendar to a weather system. I put the phone in my back pocket.

It buzzes again, an email this time. From Simone Achterberg. Subject line: *Project North — integration workstreams (CONFIDENTIAL — leadership only)*. There's an attachment called *North_Integration_v14.pdf*, and the preview line says *Pip, as discussed with Adam, sharing the full deck so you're*—and then it cuts off.

As discussed with Adam. Version fourteen.

I hold my thumb over it. I could open it. I could read the whole thing standing right here in the yard by my cracked pallet. I've been getting Monday decks from this company for two years and I don't open them. I tell myself it's because they're written by people who say "learnings" and "socialize." The real reason is that every time I open one, something's in it I wasn't asked about. It's easier to know a thing is happening than to see the slide where it happens.

I swipe it into a folder called LATER. LATER has four hundred and some emails in it. LATER is my professional legacy.

Then I go back inside and up the iron steps to the little mezzanine office over the paint booth that I never use either, and I open the file cabinet in the corner. Second drawer down, a hanging file labeled *UNIT 001* in my own handwriting from when my handwriting was neater.

Bill of sale, sort of. A receipt from the steel yard on Market Avenue for the original frame stock, made out to *P. Sobczak*. A receipt from Grand Rapids Glass, also *P. Sobczak*, for two pieces of insulated glass with a polished edge, and a note on it in the counterman's handwriting: *good luck w/ the corner*. A photo of my father in his welding jacket with the room behind him on sawhorses, taken with a disposable camera, his face squinting into the sun like he's about to tell the photographer to move.

And the lease, two pages, stapled crooked. *Lessor: Philippa M. Sobczak. Lessee: Twodesk, Inc.* One dollar a year. I wrote it myself in 2018, on the kitchen table, the week before we craned the room into the Lindqvist building, because our lawyer at the time said we couldn't put my personal property on the company's insurance without one. Adam stood behind me reading it over my shoulder while I typed.

"'Terminable at will by Lessor,'" he read. "That's a terrible lease."

"It's a great lease."

"For you."

"I'm the lessor."

"Nobody's ever going to read it," he said, and kissed the top of my head, and went to take a call.

I read it twice now, sitting on a folding chair over the paint booth with the smell of primer coming up through the floor. Terminable at will. Lessee to return the premises in the condition received. *Premises* means the room. I put that word in there because I'd read it in somebody else's lease and liked how it sounded, like we were renting out a manor.

At ten to three, Lolo comes up the steps with her lunch cooler in one hand and her reading glasses on a beaded chain around her neck, and she stops at the top step and looks at me sitting there with the file in my lap, and she knows. I don't know how she knows. Lolo would've made a terrible poker player and a great priest.

"Benny says you kicked a pallet," she says.

"Benny should be driving the forklift."

"He was driving the forklift. He drove it over to tell me." She sets the cooler down on the desk. "What'd it do?"

"What did who do?"

"The pallet, hon."

"It was there."

"Uh-huh." She sits down across from me on the other folding chair, which complains. "Gina from the office called Darnell's wife, and Darnell's wife called Darnell, and Darnell told the whole knee line, so let's skip it. There's a woman in your room."

"There's a woman in my room."

"With a plant."

"A ficus."

"Is it a nice ficus?"

"It's a fine ficus, Lolo. It's not the ficus's fault."

She nods slowly, putting her glasses on, taking them off. She's been doing that with her glasses since 1994 according to my father, who used to do an impression of it at Christmas.

"And Adam?"

"He said I barely use it."

Lolo makes a noise. It's not a word. It's the noise you'd make if someone told you they'd put ketchup on pierogi in front of your mother.

"Do you?" she says.

"Do I what?"

"Use it."

I look down at the lease. "No."

"Why not?"

There's a short answer, which is *because I'm here*, and a long answer, which starts two years ago on a Tuesday morning at seven o'clock and which I've never said out loud to anybody, including the person it's about. I give her the short answer. Lolo has known me since I was a kid on a bucket by the welders. She hears the long one behind it like a radio in the next room.

"Okay," she says. She takes a sandwich out of the cooler, unwraps it, looks at it, wraps it back up. "So what do you want to do, hon? You want it out the window or out the door? 'Cause the door's a no. That room doesn't fit in the elevator, and I'm not carrying it down the stairs at my age."

I look up at her.

She looks back, glasses off, completely serious.

"The window," I say. "The bay unbolts. I built it to unbolt."

"I know you did, I was there, I held the bolts." She jerks her chin at the phone on the desk. "So call Walt."

"It's Monday."

"Walt doesn't care what day it is. Walt cares if it's raining." She leans over, picks up my phone, and holds it out to me. "Call him before you think about it so long you turn into a person who doesn't do things. You're getting close. I can see it from here."

I take the phone.

Walt Kasprzak picks up on the second ring. He sounds like he's eating. Walt always sounds like he's eating, because Walt is always eating.

"Sobczak," he says. "Happy New Year. What fell over this time?"

"Nothing fell over. I need a pick."

"Where at?"

"The Lindqvist building, fourth floor, the northwest bay."

There's a silence on the line. I can hear him stop chewing.

"Your room," he says.

"My room."

"The one we put in there in eighteen. On your—" He stops. He was at the install. He was at the party after, on the roof, the night we hung the sign. He watched Adam give a speech to two hundred people as if it were two thousand. "Out?" he says.

"All the way out."

"When?"

"Tonight."

More silence. A long one. Then I hear his chair creak, which means Walt has put his sandwich down and leaned back, which means he's going to do it and he wants me to know how much he's going to charge me.

"Permit's still good," he says slowly. "The standing one for the building. I renewed it in October 'cause they wanted the rooftop units swapped in spring. Says Monroe Avenue, says night work, says Kasprzak Rigging. Doesn't say what's coming out the window. City'll fine you if anybody squawks."

"Let them fine me."

"Wind's supposed to drop after midnight. Snow, but light." He breathes out. "It'll cost you, Pip."

"Everything costs me."

"Not like this. Overtime, the hundred-ton, a lowboy, a flagger, me. And I'm going to want pierogi. Your mother's. Not the store kind."

"Done."

"Who's got the inside? The bay bolts?"

I look at Lolo. Lolo is unwrapping her sandwich again. She doesn't look up.

"Darnell," she says to the sandwich. "He'll want to. He's been bored since the holidays."

"Darnell," I tell Walt.

"And you're sure."

He doesn't make it a question. He's known me since I was in braces. He's watching me in his head the way Lolo is watching me across the desk, from the corner of her eye, chewing, pretending she's somewhere else.

I think about the ficus. I think about the lighthouse face up on my desk, and the banker's box against the door covering the first line, and Adam handing Simone her coffee over my shoulder. I think about *you barely use it* said in the voice for a conference room. I think about version fourteen.

*Coffee at seven.* We haven't had coffee at seven in two years. I couldn't tell you the day it stopped. That's not true. I could tell you the day, and the hour, and what I was wearing. I'm just not going to.

"I'm sure, Walt."

"What time?"

Outside the little office window, down on the floor, second shift is coming in, Lolo's people, stamping snow off their boots, punching the clock one after another with the sound of a small gun going off very politely. The radio has moved on from Fleetwood Mac to Bob Seger. Somebody laughs over by the knee line, a big laugh, and somebody else tells them to shut up.

"Three in the morning," I say. "I'll bring the bolts."
