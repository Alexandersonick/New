## Chapter 3 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The Clyde Park plant sounds like a kitchen the night before a wedding, if the kitchen were the size of a hockey rink and everybody in it were holding a welding torch.

It reaches me in the parking lot, the long rising whine of the panel saw, the press brake going *chunk*, the hiss and crackle from the knee line where four welders stand in a row under the yellow curtains making the hidden frames that let my corner stand up without a post. I park the truck by the dumpsters where I always park it and sit there with the engine running and listen for a while. Some people have a beach. I have this.

Inside, Irene Kwiatkowski looks up from the glazing table and lifts her chin at me.

"Film stays on the Ada unit till Thursday," she says. "Don't let anybody peel it."

"Who'd peel it?"

"You would. You always want to see the glass." She goes back to her bead of sealant without waiting for me to argue.

Benny Szymanski is running the forklift with a cigarette behind his ear that he will not light, because I'll fire him, and he'll pretend it's because of the rule. He swings the forks around and idles up next to me.

"Boss. Thought you were downtown today."

"I was downtown today."

"Short day downtown." He looks at my face and then at the forks. "You want me to move something heavy for you? I'm feeling strong."

"Move the Traverse City unit to finishing, Benny."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant."

"The Traverse City guy called again." He scratches under the band of his hard hat. "They still don't know who gets the house, so he's moving into the room. He wants to know can we put a lock on the inside of the door and also a lock on the outside."

"Why both?"

"So they can lock each other out of it at the same time, I guess." Benny shrugs. "I told him that sounds like a marriage, and he should call a lawyer."

"Put a deadbolt on the inside and tell him the outside one's extra."

"How much extra?"

"A lot," I say, and he swings the forks toward finishing, grinning like I've given him a raise.

Somebody's radio on the glazing table is playing Fleetwood Mac, the song every radio in Michigan plays at least once an hour in case you've forgotten you're sad.

I walk my morning loop of the floor, though today it's afternoon, past the wall framing jigs and the roof cassette line and the finishing bay where three Corners sit in a row under work lights, nearly done, their glass still wearing the blue protective film. One of them is going to a retired dentist in Ada who wants to paint in it, mostly birds. I know because I read every order. Nobody else reads every order. Adam says reading every order doesn't scale.

At my bench in the back corner, by the big rolling door to the yard, there's a weld sample I was in the middle of cutting on Friday, a section of the knee frame clamped in a vise with its cross-section sawn clean so you can see the bead. I sit down and pick up my file. I can't work, and the file goes back on the bench.

I get up and go out through the rolling door into the yard, where there's a stack of pallets against the fence, and I kick one of them, and then I kick it again. I keep kicking it until it's the pallet's fault. I've done that since I was fourteen and my brothers told me girls can't weld, which I'd have argued about more if I hadn't been fourteen and holding a stick welder upside down. My oldest brother, Joe, found me kicking the garage door that night and stood there in his socks. Then he went inside and came back out with my father's welding hood and put it on my head backwards so I couldn't see, and said, "There. Now you look like a welder."

The pallet has a crack in it now, and my toes hurt inside my boot, which helps.

Benny has followed me out. He's sitting on the forklift in the rolling door with the engine idling, looking at the pallet like it's a dog that got hit.

"That one owe you money?" he says.

"It was in the way."

"It was against the fence, boss. That's the opposite of in the way."

"Did you move the Traverse City unit?"

"Moved it." He puts the forklift in reverse. "I'm going to go tell Lolo, so it's not a secret."

"Benny, don't you dare."

"It's for your own good," he says, and backs away beeping.

My phone buzzes in my back pocket, and it's Adam.

*Missed you in the meeting. We need to talk properly. Tonight? Back Tues AM w/ Kurt. Call me after 10.*

*We need to talk properly*. He's the one who started talking improperly, as a policy, around the time the company passed two hundred employees and his calendar went from a calendar to a weather system. I shove the phone back in my pocket.

It buzzes again, an email this time, from Simone Achterberg. Subject line: *Project North: integration workstreams (CONFIDENTIAL, leadership only)*. There's an attachment called *North_Integration_v14.pdf*, and the preview line says *Pip, as discussed with Adam, sharing the full deck so you're*—and then it cuts off.

As discussed with Adam. Version fourteen.

I hold my thumb over it. I could open it and read the whole thing standing right here in the yard by my cracked pallet. I get a Monday deck from this company every week and I don't open them. I tell myself it's because they're written by people who say "learnings" and "socialize." The real reason is that every time I open one, something's in it I wasn't asked about. It's easier to know a thing is happening than to see the slide where it happens.

I swipe it into a folder called LATER. LATER has four hundred and some emails in it. LATER is my professional legacy.

Then I go back inside. Irene doesn't look up from the glazing table as I pass.

"Benny's gone to tell Lolo," she says.

"I know," I say, and keep walking.

"You want me to go stop him?"

"Could you?"

"No," Irene says, and runs her bead of sealant clean to the corner without a wobble.

I go up the iron steps to the little mezzanine office over the paint booth that I never use either, and open the file cabinet in the corner. In the second drawer down there's a hanging file labeled *UNIT 001* in my own handwriting, from when my handwriting was neater.

A receipt from the steel yard on Market Avenue for the original frame stock, made out to *P. Sobczak*. A receipt from Grand Rapids Glass, also *P. Sobczak*, for two pieces of insulated glass with a polished edge, with a note on it in the counterman's handwriting: *good luck w/ the corner*. A photo of my father in his welding jacket with the room behind him on sawhorses, his face squinting into the sun like he's about to tell the photographer to move.

And the lease, two pages, stapled crooked, Philippa Sobczak to Twodesk, Inc., one dollar a year. I wrote it myself in 2018, on the kitchen table, the week before we craned the room into the Lindqvist building, because our lawyer at the time said we couldn't put my personal property on the company's insurance without one. Adam stood behind me reading it over my shoulder while I typed.

"'Terminable at will by Lessor,'" he read. "That's a terrible lease."

"It's a great lease," I said.

"For you, it's a great lease."

"I'm the lessor, Adam."

"Nobody's ever going to read it," he said, and kissed the top of my head, and went to take a call.

I read it twice now, sitting on a folding chair over the paint booth with the smell of primer coming up through the floor. Terminable at will. Lessee to return the premises in the condition received. *Premises* means the room. I put that word in there because I'd read it in somebody else's lease and liked how it sounded, like we were renting out a manor.

A little before three, Lolo comes up the steps with her lunch cooler in one hand and her thermos in the other. She stops at the top step and takes in the file in my lap, and she knows. I don't know how she knows. Lolo would've made a terrible poker player and a great priest.

"Benny says you kicked a pallet," she says.

"Benny should be driving the forklift."

"He was driving the forklift. He drove it over to tell me." She sets the cooler down on the desk. "What'd it do?"

"What did who do?"

"The pallet, hon."

"It was there," I say.

"Uh-huh," she says, and sits down across from me on the other folding chair, which complains. "Gina from the office called Darnell's wife, and Darnell's wife called Darnell, and Darnell told the whole knee line, so let's skip it. There's a woman in your room."

"There's a woman in my room."

"With a plant, I heard."

"A ficus."

"Is it a nice ficus?"

"It's a fine ficus, Lolo. It's not the ficus's fault."

She nods slowly and puts her reading glasses on, as if the ficus might be somewhere in the fine print.

"And Adam?"

"He said I barely use it."

Lolo makes the noise you'd make if somebody put ketchup on pierogi in front of your mother.

"Do you?" she says. "Use it?"

I look down at the lease instead of at her. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm here," I say.

Lolo has known me since I was a kid on an upturned bucket by the welders. She knows a short answer when she hears one, and she lets it go.

"I ever tell you I ran a line in that building?" she says. "When it was still Lindqvist Furniture?"

"About four hundred times."

"Your dad trained me on the press brake on two, right by the freight car, in ninety-four. Only man in the place who never explained anything to me twice." She takes a sandwich out of the cooler, unwraps it, looks at it, wraps it back up. "Thirty years I put into that building one way and another, and at your Christmas party a kid at the front desk asked me was I there about the copier."

"What'd you tell him?"

"I told him yes, and I fixed the copier. It was a paper jam, it's always a paper jam. I'm not proud of it." She sets the sandwich on the desk. "So what do you want to do, hon? You want it out the window or out the door? 'Cause the door's a no. That room doesn't fit in the elevator, and I'm not carrying it down the stairs at my age."

When I look up, she's looking back at me, completely serious.

"The window," I say. "The bay unbolts, I built it to unbolt."

"I know you did, I was there, I held the bolts." She jerks her chin at the phone on the desk. "So call Walt."

"It's Monday."

"Walt doesn't care what day it is. Walt cares if it's raining." She leans over, picks up my phone, and holds it out to me. "Call him before you think about it so long you turn into a person who doesn't do things. You're getting close. I can see it from here."

I take the phone, and Walt Kasprzak picks up on the second ring, sounding like he's eating. Walt always sounds like he's eating, because Walt is always eating.

"Sobczak," he says. "What fell over this time?"

"Nothing fell over. I need a pick."

"Where at?"

"The Lindqvist building, fourth floor, the northwest bay."

On the other end of the line, Walt stops chewing.

"Your room," he says.

"My room."

"The one we put in there in eighteen. On your—" He stops. He was at the install, and at the party after, on the roof, the night we hung the sign and Adam talked for twenty minutes without a note. "Out?" he says.

"All the way out."

"When?"

"Tonight, Walt."

He goes quiet long enough that I check the screen to see if I've lost him. Then his chair creaks, which means Walt has put his sandwich down and leaned back, which means he's going to do it and he wants me to know how much he's going to charge me.

"Permit's still good," he says slowly. "The standing one for the building. I renewed it in October 'cause they wanted the rooftop units swapped in spring. Says Monroe Avenue, says night work, says Kasprzak Rigging. Doesn't say what's coming out the window. City'll fine you if anybody squawks."

"Let them fine me."

"Wind's supposed to drop after midnight, snow but light." He breathes out through his teeth. "It'll cost you, Pip."

"Everything costs me."

"Not like this. Overtime, the hundred-ton, a lowboy, a flagger, me. And I'm going to want pierogi. Your mother's, not the store kind."

"Done."

"Who's got the inside, on the bay bolts?"

I look over at Lolo, who is unwrapping her sandwich again and doesn't look up.

"Darnell," she says to the sandwich. "He'll want to. He's been bored since the holidays." She already has her phone out in her other hand and is typing with one thumb, slow, like every letter is a separate decision. It buzzes back before she's set it down. "He says is he allowed to use your good chisel."

"No."

She types. "I told him yes."

"Lolo."

"Let the man have a chisel, hon. He's had a long December."

"Darnell," I tell Walt.

"Adam know about this?" Walt says.

"Adam's in Chicago."

"That's not what I asked you."

"It's what I'm answering, Walt."

"And you're sure."

He doesn't make it a question. He's known me since I was in braces. He's watching me in his head, same as Lolo is watching me across the desk, from the corner of her eye, chewing, pretending she's somewhere else.

I think about the ficus, and the lighthouse face up on my desk, and the banker's box against the door covering the first line, and Adam handing Simone her coffee over my shoulder. *You barely use it*, said in the voice for a conference room. Version fourteen.

*Coffee at seven.* We haven't had coffee at seven in two years. After Nashville I stopped going up at seven, and he stopped asking where I was.

"I'm sure, Walt."

"What time?"

Outside the little office window, down on the floor, second shift is coming in, Lolo's people, stamping snow off their boots, punching the clock one after another with the sound of a small gun going off very politely. The radio has moved on from Fleetwood Mac to Bob Seger. Somebody laughs over by the knee line, a big laugh, and somebody else tells them to shut up.

"Three in the morning," I say. "I'll bring the bolts."
