## Chapter 21 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Halvard walks at 8:14 on Friday morning, by email, in four sentences.

Ruth forwards it to me at 8:16 with no message, just the email, the way you'd forward a photo of a car accident. I read it in my room in the yard with my coat still on.

*In light of yesterday's public statements by a Twodesk founder, Halvard Group has concluded that the conditions for a successful transaction are not present at this time. We are therefore withdrawing from discussions, effective immediately. We wish the Twodesk team every success. We remain admirers of the product.*

*We remain admirers of the product.* Kurt wrote that line himself. I can hear him say it, in a turtleneck, with his hand flat on my glass.

I sit there. I wait to feel good. This is what I wanted. This is what I said on television in front of eleven million people and forty of my own in the snow: *There's no deal.* And now there isn't one. Nobody's putting a post in my corner. Nobody's moving the knee line to Monterrey.

I don't feel good. I feel like somebody took the floor out from under a scaffold I was standing on and told me congratulations, you don't need the scaffold.

At 9:40 Northgate calls Adam.

I know because Adam texts me at 9:52, in the old way, the before way: *Northgate. Pruitt. Credit committee met at 9. Halvard walking + "public founder dispute" + "material uncertainty". They're invoking §11 early. Cash test is now 10 days from today, not March. If we're under 6 on Feb 8, they call.*

Then, at 9:53: *I'm sorry. I don't know what else to tell you before.*

Ten days. Simone's tape said twenty-three. Simone's tape said maybe twenty-one. It didn't say ten. Nothing on that tape ever said ten.

I do the arithmetic sitting at my desk with my coat on. I do it three times on the back of an invoice. Gil's net sixty starts the day his first trailer of tube hits the dock, which is Tuesday. Steel gets cut Wednesday. First escrow releases Thursday, a million and a half. Second shift can't start for six weeks because I don't have anybody who can weld the knee. And the hole is two point three, and it doesn't care about any of that, it's just two point three, sitting there, the way a hole sits there.

Ten days.

At 10:30 Simone walks into my room without knocking.

She's in her navy coat with her black notebook under her arm. She doesn't wipe her boots on my mother's rug. She stands on it with snow melting off them onto the bread bags and looks at me, and her face is the same as always, level, a manifest, and I know something's wrong because her hands are shaking. Simone's hands don't shake. Simone's hands write in printing so small and straight I thought it was a font.

"I was on a call with Kurt at seven-thirty this morning," she says. "Before he sent the email. He asked me to stay on after Ruth dropped. He asked me, *Simone, did you know she was going to do that?* I said no. He asked me, *Did you give her our supplier analysis? The cost model? Did you help her build a plan to blow up my deal while you were sitting on my integration calls?*" She stops. "I said I had not shared any Halvard material. Which is true. I have not."

"I know you haven't."

"He did not believe me. He said, *Then why does it look exactly like what you'd build?*" Her voice doesn't change. Her hands do. "He said I would never work in this industry in the Midwest again. He has three friends at the two companies I would have gone to next. I know them. They are the kind of men who play golf in Scottsdale."

"Simone, I didn't—"

"You didn't tell me," she says.

It's quiet. It's so quiet in the room I can hear the press brake from across the yard. *Chunk*.

"I was on a call with Kurt Lindahl for forty minutes on Wednesday," Simone says, "lying by omission for you, because you asked me. Because you *asked*. I have not been asked for anything in eight months, and you came to my apartment at nine-forty at night and asked me, and I said yes before I could think about whether I should." She takes a breath. "And then on Thursday you went on television and decided the whole thing in ninety seconds, and you did not text me. You did not call me. I watched it on my phone in the glass room with Gordon. Gordon cried. I found out from Carla Ruiz."

"There wasn't time—"

"There were twenty minutes. Darnell told me. He said you were in the room with your phone for twenty minutes." She looks at the desk where my phone is. "You held it in your hand, Pip."

I don't say anything. I don't have anything.

"You did the thing," Simone says quietly. "The exact thing. The thing he did to you, with the slide, at the all-hands. You stood up in front of everybody and decided alone, and the people who were in it with you found out from the parking lot."

She takes a white envelope out of her notebook and puts it on my desk. On top of the invoice with my arithmetic on the back.

"My resignation. Effective today. I have already sent it to Ruth and Diane." She straightens. "I have also sent Gordon the model. All of it. The supplier terms, the escrow schedule, the training plan, the tape. It is in a folder called PIP. You will need it. I do not want anything to happen to the plant because I was angry."

"Simone."

"Yes."

"Don't go."

She looks at me for a long moment. Something moves behind her face, the measurement again, taken one last time with the best instrument she's got.

"You are asking me," she says. "I notice that. I want you to know I notice it." She turns to the door. "But I have a daughter at State and eleven boxes I have not unpacked, and this morning I learned that I should probably not unpack them. I would like to go and think about where to take them."

She opens the door. The cold comes in.

"I liked working with you," she says, not turning around. "Very much. That's not a thing I say."

Then she's gone across the yard to the gray Volvo with the ski rack and no skis, and she drives away slowly, between the lines, and doesn't look back.

He's at the house when I get home at nine.

He's standing at his end of the counter in his shirtsleeves with his tie off and his hands flat on the quartz. The good one, the one that looks like a river. There's no food. There's no coffee. The kitchen's bright and cold and there's a glass of whiskey by his hand that he hasn't touched, and the Channel 6 clip is playing on his laptop, open beside it with the sound off. My face in the camera light. The glass corner. The snow. My finger pointing back at the rolling door.

He watches me come in. He doesn't say hi.

"Ruth's calling an emergency board for Monday," he says. "Del's flying back from Phoenix. Diane's been on the phone with Northgate's counsel since noon. They're not budging on section eleven. It's ten days, Pip."

"I know it's ten days."

"Simone quit."

"I know. She told me first."

"She told *you* first." Something goes across his face. "Of course she did."

I put my bag down. I don't take my coat off.

"Go ahead," I say.

"Go ahead what?"

"Say it. Whatever you've been standing here not saying since five-oh-two yesterday. Say it."

He looks at the laptop. At my face in the camera light, my mouth moving, no sound.

"You didn't call me," he says.

"No."

"You stood in the ramp. On level three. You told me *before, not after*. You said I don't decide one thing about this company without you, not a coffee order. You said that's what it's like to be married to you." His voice is very quiet. "And then you went on television and killed a billion-dollar deal and set off a covenant on my shares and you didn't text me. I was in the glass room with Gordon. Gordon *cried*, Pip. I found out from Tom."

"Who's Tom?"

"The anchor! Tom! *Back to you, Tom!*" He laughs, and it's not any laugh I've got catalogued. It's an ugly sound. "I found out about my own company from Tom Bielski on Channel 6."

"Now you know how it feels," I say.

It comes out of me cold and fast and I can't stop it. It comes out of the bottom of two years.

"Now you know what it's like to find out from a stage. Now you know what it's like to be the person in the gray box. Pending. That's what I was, Adam. On your slide. *P. Sobczak, pending.* You did that to me at the all-hands with Nashville, and you did it with the room, and you did it with Northgate, and you did it every night at one in the morning when you picked up the phone and called *her*—"

"Don't."

"—and told her the things you were afraid of instead of me—"

"Don't, Pip—"

"—so don't you stand at that counter and tell me about *before*."

"Then why did you do it?"

He's not shouting. He's leaning on the counter with both hands like he'll fall down if he doesn't.

"You knew," he says. "You knew how it felt. You've known for two years. You stood in that ramp and told me exactly how it felt, and then you held your phone for twenty minutes, Darnell says twenty minutes, and you put it in your pocket and did it anyway. So why?"

Because forty people were standing in a doorway in the snow.

Because I was scared.

Because I was handling it.

I don't say any of that. I stand there in my coat in my kitchen with my keys still in my hand, and he looks at me across twelve feet of quartz with the river in it, and his face does something I've never seen it do. It doesn't close. It opens, all the way, like a door off its hinges, and what's behind it is so tired.

"Now you know," he says, very quietly. "What it's like. To be scared, and alone with it. And to think if you just decide fast enough, nobody else has to carry it."

That's it. That's the one. It goes in under my ribs like the corner of a sheet of glass.

Because he's right. Because I did. Because I've been doing it for two years, in a different shape. Sitting at my bench at six-thirty in the morning, filing things, deciding he'd stopped coming, deciding not to open the decks, deciding to sit in a room in a yard. Deciding alone and calling it his fault.

I can't stand in this kitchen with him. I can't stand in a room with somebody who can see me that clearly.

"I need you to go," I say.

He closes his eyes.

"Where?" he says.

I don't know. I don't have an answer. I've got nothing in my hands but my keys and nothing in my head but ten days and Simone's white envelope and *now you know*. I look at him across the counter, and what comes out of my mouth is the last thing I want to say, the worst thing, his own words, in his own voice.

"You barely use it," I say.

He opens his eyes and looks at me. He knows what I mean. The room. Ten by twelve, on cribbing, in a yard on Clyde Park Avenue, with the light on.

He takes his coat off the hook by the door, the hook I drilled into the brick in 2019. He doesn't pack a bag. He takes his keys and his phone and he doesn't touch the whiskey, and he stops in the back door with his hand on the frame, the way he stopped in the door of my room in the yard on the first morning with the snow blowing in.

He doesn't say anything. He just looks at me for a while.

Then he goes, and the door shuts, and I stand in the kitchen and listen to his car back down the drive.

On the counter, the laptop plays my face again, silent. My finger points behind me at nothing.
