## Chapter 21 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

Halvard walks at 8:14 on Friday morning, by email, in four sentences.

Ruth forwards it to me two minutes later with no message, just the email, the way you'd forward a photo of a car accident. I read it in my room in the yard with my coat still on.

*In light of yesterday's public statements by a Twodesk founder, Halvard Group has concluded that the conditions for a successful transaction are not present at this time. We are therefore withdrawing from discussions, effective immediately. We wish the Twodesk team every success. We remain admirers of the product.*

*We remain admirers of the product.* Kurt wrote that line himself. I can hear him saying it in a turtleneck, with his hand flat on my glass.

I sit there and wait to feel good. This is what I wanted. It's what I said on television to eleven million people and to forty of my own standing in the snow: *There's no deal.* Now there isn't one. Nobody's putting a post in my corner, and nobody's moving the knee line to Monterrey.

I don't feel good. I get up and check that the space heater's plugged in, because it's the one thing in the room I know how to fix, and it is plugged in, and I sit back down.

Northgate calls Adam a little before ten. I know because Adam texts me ten minutes later, the way he's texted me everything since the ramp: *Northgate. Pruitt. Credit committee met at 9. Halvard walking + "public founder dispute" + "material uncertainty". They're invoking §11 early. Cash test is now ten business days out, not March. Friday the 12th, close of business. If we're under 6, they call.*

A minute after that: *I'm sorry. That's all of it. You have everything I have.*

Ten business days. Simone's tape said twenty-three, maybe twenty-one on a bad week. Nothing on that tape ever said ten.

I do the arithmetic at my desk with my coat on, three times, on the back of an invoice. Gil's net sixty starts the day his first trailer of tube hits the dock, which is Thursday, and we cut that same day, and the first escrow releases when the steel's cut, a million and a half. Second shift can't start for six weeks because I don't have anybody who can weld the knee. And the hole is two point three, and it doesn't care about any of that. It just sits there on the back of the invoice being two point three.

Simone walks into my room at half past ten without knocking.

She's in her navy coat with the black notebook under her arm. She doesn't wipe her boots on my mother's rug. She stands on it with snow melting off them onto the bread bags, and her face is the same as always, level as a manifest, and I know something's wrong because her hands are shaking. Simone's hands don't shake. Simone's hands write in printing so small and straight I once thought it was a font.

"I was on a call with Kurt at seven-thirty this morning," she says. "Before he sent the email. He asked me to stay on after Ruth dropped. He asked me, *Simone, did you know she was going to do that?* I said no. He asked me, *Did you give her our supplier analysis? The cost model? Did you help her build a plan to blow up my deal while you were sitting on my integration calls?*" She stops and closes the notebook. "I said I had not shared any Halvard material. Which is true. I have not."

"I know you haven't, Simone."

"He did not believe me. He said, *Then why does it look exactly like what you would build?*" Her voice stays level while her hands go on shaking. "He said I would never work in this industry in the Midwest again. He has three friends at the two companies I would have gone to next. I know them. They are the kind of men who play golf in Scottsdale."

"Simone, I didn't—"

"You didn't tell me," she says.

It's so quiet in the room I can hear the press brake across the yard, one long *chunk* through the glass.

"I was on a call with Kurt Lindahl for forty minutes on Wednesday," Simone says, "lying by omission for you, because you asked me. Because you *asked*. I have not been asked for anything in eight months, and you came to my apartment at nine-forty at night and asked me, and I said yes before I could think about whether I should." She takes a breath. "And then on Thursday you went on television and decided the whole thing in ninety seconds, and you did not text me. You did not call me. I watched it on my phone in the glass room with Gordon. I found out from Carla Ruiz."

"There wasn't time—"

"There were twenty minutes. Darnell told me. He said you sat in the room with your phone for twenty minutes." She looks at the desk, where my phone is lying on the invoice. "You held it in your hand, Pip."

The phone's right there between us, and I don't pick it up now either.

"You did the thing," Simone says quietly. "The exact thing. The thing he did to you with the slide at the all-hands. You stood up in public and decided alone, and the people who were in it with you found out from the parking lot."

She takes a white envelope out of her notebook and puts it on my desk, on top of the invoice with my arithmetic on the back.

"My resignation, effective today. I have already sent it to Ruth and Diane." She straightens her coat. "I have also sent Gordon the model. All of it, the supplier terms and the escrow schedule and the training plan and the tape. It is in a folder called PIP. You will need it. I do not want anything to happen to the plant because I was angry with you."

"Simone, please don't go," I say.

She stands with one hand on the notebook, and her mouth goes flat and careful, the way it goes when a number she's checked twice comes out the same both times.

"You are asking me," she says. "I notice that. I will remember that you did." She turns to the door. "But I have a daughter at State and eleven boxes I have not unpacked, and this morning I learned that I should probably not unpack them. I would like to go and think about where to take them."

She opens the door, and the cold comes in around her.

"I liked working with you," she says, without turning around. "Very much. I do not say that to people."

Then she's gone across the yard to the gray Volvo with the ski rack and no skis, and she drives away slowly, between the lines, and doesn't look back.

A minute later Darnell comes over from the plant with two coffees from the lunch truck in a cardboard tray. He stops in my doorway and watches the Volvo turn onto Clyde Park.

"One of these was hers," he says. "Black, no lid. She likes to see it."

"She's not coming back, Darnell."

He looks at the second cup like it's a part that came off a truck with no paperwork. "You mean today, or like at all?"

"I mean at all. She resigned this morning."

"Huh." He sets the tray down on my desk, right on the envelope, and I slide the envelope out from under it. "You want to know something stupid? Irene printed you off the TV. She taped you up on the break room door, and Benny drew a cape on you with a Sharpie. It's a pretty good cape."

"Take it down, Darnell."

"I'm not taking it down. Lolo would kill me." He takes his own cup and leaves hers. "Guys keep coming up to me asking if we won. I keep telling them yeah."

He waits in the door for me to tell him yeah. I pick up Simone's coffee instead, because it's hot and it's there, and drink it black with no lid, and he nods like that counts as an answer and goes back across the yard to the rolling door.

He's at the house when I get home at nine.

He's standing at the counter in his shirtsleeves with his tie off and his hands flat on the quartz, the good one, the one that looks like a river. There's no food and no coffee. The kitchen's bright and cold, and there's a glass of whiskey by his hand that he hasn't touched, and beside it the Channel 6 clip is playing on his laptop with the sound off. My face in the camera light, the glass corner and the snow, and my finger pointing back at the rolling door.

He doesn't say hi when I come in.

"Ruth's calling an emergency board for Monday," he says. "Del's flying back from Phoenix. Diane's been on the phone with Northgate's counsel since noon, and they're not budging on section eleven. It's ten days, Pip."

"I know it's ten days."

"Simone quit."

"I know. She told me first, this morning, in the yard."

"She told *you* first." Something goes across his face and stays there. "Of course she did."

I put my bag down on a stool and keep my coat on.

"Go ahead," I say. "Say whatever you've been standing here not saying since yesterday at five."

He doesn't pretend he doesn't know what I mean. He turns the laptop a few degrees toward me, so we can both watch me.

"You didn't call me," he says.

"No," I say. "I didn't call you."

"For two weeks I've told you everything before I did it. Every call, every email, every man from Chicago. I said *um* in front of Simone. I forgot a man's name. I did it badly and I did it every time." His voice is very quiet. "And then you went on television and killed a billion-dollar deal and set off a covenant on my shares, and you didn't text me. I was in the glass room with Gordon. Gordon *cried*, Pip. I found out from Tom."

"I don't know who Tom is."

"The anchor, Pip, the guy you handed it back to, *back to you, Tom*." He laughs, and it's no laugh I've got catalogued. It's an ugly sound. "I found out about my own company from Tom Bielski on Channel 6."

"Now you know how it feels," I say.

It comes out of me cold and fast and I can't stop it. It comes up from the bottom of everything since Nashville.

"Now you know what it's like to find out from a stage, to be the person in the gray box on the slide. That's what I was, Adam, *P. Sobczak, pending*, in front of three hundred people. You did that to me at the all-hands with Nashville, and you did it with the room, and you did it with Northgate, and you did it every night at one in the morning when you picked up the phone and called *her* and told her the things you were afraid of instead of me, so don't you stand at that counter and tell me about *before*."

He says *don't* twice while I'm saying it, quietly, and I go right over him both times.

"Then why did you do it?"

He's not shouting. He's leaning on the counter with both hands like he'll fall down if he lets go.

"You knew," he says. "You knew how it felt. You've known since Nashville. You stood in that ramp and told me exactly how it felt, and then you held your phone for twenty minutes, Darnell says twenty minutes, and you put it in your pocket and did it anyway. So why did you do it, Pip?"

Because forty people were standing in a doorway in the snow with their hands in their pockets. Because I was scared, and because I was handling it.

I don't say any of it. I stand in my own kitchen in my coat with my keys still in my hand, and he watches me across twelve feet of quartz with the river in it, and his face does something I've never seen it do. It opens all the way, and what's behind it is so tired.

"Now you know," he says, very quietly. "What it's like. To be scared, and alone with it. And to think if you just decide fast enough, nobody else has to carry it."

That's the one. It goes in under my ribs like the corner of a sheet of glass.

Because he's right. I've been doing it since Nashville in a different shape: sitting at my bench at six-thirty in the morning filing things, deciding he'd stopped coming, deciding not to open the decks, deciding to sit in a room in a yard. Deciding alone and calling it his fault.

I can't stand in a kitchen with somebody who can see me that clearly.

"I need you to go," I say.

He closes his eyes. "Where am I supposed to go, Pip?"

I don't have an answer. I've got nothing in my hands but my keys and nothing in my head but ten days and Simone's white envelope and *now you know*. What comes out of my mouth is the last thing I want to say, the worst thing, his own words in his own voice.

"You barely use it," I say.

He opens his eyes. He knows what I mean. The room, ten by twelve, up on cribbing in a yard on Clyde Park Avenue with the light on.

He takes his coat off the hook by the door, the hook I drilled into the brick in 2019. He doesn't pack a bag. He takes his keys and his phone and leaves the whiskey where it is, and he stops in the back door with his hand on the frame, the way he stopped in the door of my room in the yard on the first morning with the snow blowing in behind him.

He stands there with his hand on the frame and keeps his mouth shut, and so do I.

Then he goes, and the door shuts, and I stand in the kitchen and listen to his car back down the drive.

On the counter, the laptop plays my face again, silent. My finger points behind me at nothing.
