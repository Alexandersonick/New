## Chapter 15 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

There's a thermos on my desk.

It's seven twenty-six on Tuesday morning, and I've just come across the yard from the truck with my coat open and my hair still wet because I slept through two alarms after bowling, and I open the door of my room and stop on my mother's rug. It's a tall green steel thermos with a dent in the bottom, the one that lives in the cupboard over our refrigerator at home behind the cake pans nobody uses. Next to it there's a paper cup from the espresso machine, the little brass eagle stamped on the sleeve, with a plastic lid. The chair by the radiator is pushed back from the desk at an angle, like somebody got up out of it fast.

I put my hand on the paper cup, and it's cold. The rim of the lid is bitten flat in one spot, which is what he does to lids on planes when the landing is bad.

When I unscrew the thermos, steam comes up out of it, and the smell. It's moka-pot coffee, my coffee, the tire fire. Somebody made it at home in my dented pot on my side of the counter and brought it here in a thermos from behind the cake pans and sat in that chair and waited.

I stand there holding the lid. Across the yard the plant's rolling door is up and first shift is going, and I can hear the press brake from here. *Chunk*.

Benny's on the forklift by the steel rack. I go out and wave him down. "Was Adam here?"

Benny idles the forklift and gives me a look of mild interest and lets the engine tick.

"Little before seven," he says. "Parked by the fence and walked in with a thermos and one of them fancy cups. Went in your room and sat."

"Did he say anything to you?"

"Said good morning. Said *is she in yet*. I said not yet, she was bowling." Benny shifts his cigarette from one ear to the other. "He asked how you bowled. I said one strike and one gutter, back to back, and he said that sounded about right. Then he stood inside your door with his coat on and read that writing on the back of it, the whole thing, like it was the specials."

"Did he order anything?"

"He brought his own, Pip." Benny says it slowly, for my benefit. "Then he sat there till twenty-five after. His phone rang, and he talked on it for a minute, and he left in a hurry. Didn't drink his coffee."

"Who called him?"

"I'm a forklift driver, Pip, not the CIA." He puts it in gear. "Looked like bad news, though. He did that thing with his face."

He backs away, beeping, and swings the forks around toward the steel rack.

I go back into the room and sit down at my desk. I pour the moka-pot coffee into the thermos lid and drink it, and it's terrible, it's perfect, it tastes exactly like every morning from 2018 to the morning after Nashville, and my eyes sting, and I tell myself it's because I didn't sleep.

*Is she in yet.*

Seven o'clock. He came at seven o'clock.

There's nothing from him on my phone, no text, no missed call. There's just Tavi, in capitals: *SIX THOUSAND*. And then, a second later: *a morning show in new york ran the video again, the site is on fire*.

I type *you came* to Adam, and delete it. I type *I was late*, and delete that too. I screw the lid back on the thermos, tight, so it'll stay hot for nobody. Then I take the *UNIT 001* file out of my desk drawer, where it's been since the morning I brought it down from the mezzanine, and drive downtown with the file on the passenger seat and the thermos in the cup holder, where it rolls against the gearshift at every light.

The boardroom is on six at the Lindqvist, the old executive floor of the furniture company, where men in vests used to sit around a table made from a single slab of Michigan black walnut and decide what everybody in Grand Rapids would sit on for the next fifty years. We kept the table, and the portraits on the paneled walls, old Lindqvists in oil, one after another, getting balder down the line. The windows look west over the river and the bridges and the glass towers that went up downtown in the last ten years, where everybody's hoping to sell something to somebody.

I'm the last one in. I'm always the last one in.

They're all at the walnut table. Ruth at the head, in a black blazer instead of the vest, which is how you know she means it. Next to her Nadia Farouk, her partner from Lakeshore, who never says anything in meetings and writes everything down on an iPad with a stylus like a court reporter. Del Ortiz across from Nadia, seventy, our independent director, who ran the Chevy stamping plant in Wyoming for twenty years before GM closed it and still wears his small gold UAW pin on his lapel to every board meeting, so you know where he's from. Gordon at the far end with his laptop open and his cardigan on and his face the color of a radiator. Diane Holm, our general counsel, and Simone at a side table, Diane with a legal pad and Simone with her black notebook and her pens in their row.

And there's Adam, halfway down on the window side, in the blue travel suit, with his back to the river and a cup of espresso in front of him that I can tell from here he hasn't touched.

He lifts his head when I come in. Our eyes meet, and he doesn't look away.

There's an empty chair across the table from him, my chair, and I sit in it, and for a second it's just the two of us across a slab of black walnut with the whole board around us, and I think about the thermos rolling in my cup holder, and the words *I was late* are right there in my mouth, and I don't say them, because Ruth is saying "Good morning, everyone, let's begin," in her public radio voice, and the meeting has started, and once the meeting has started you can't say anything that matters, that's what meetings are for.

Item one is the minutes, and they pass.

Item two is the quarter. Gordon goes through it in a monotone, his eyes on the laptop, the way people read names at a memorial: revenue up, margin down, Nashville down, Clyde Park up. Customer deposits, he says, have increased by a significant amount in the last two weeks, and he turns to the side table, and Simone says:

"Six thousand and forty-one, as of nine this morning. All of them for the original Corner." She says it without looking at me. "None for the Lite."

Del Ortiz lets out a long low whistle through his teeth.

Item three is Halvard.

Ruth touches her tablet, and the big screen at the end of the room lights up, and there he is, Kurt Lindahl, on a video call from what looks like a glass box in Minneapolis, in a turtleneck, smiling, with the Halvard logo on the wall behind him in brushed steel.

"Good morning, Grand Rapids," he says. "Thank you for having me for this item. I'll be brief, and I'll say up front that we're thrilled, we're very energized. I know the founders are still aligning, and I want to say I respect that process enormously." He smiles at me through the screen. "Pip, it's wonderful to see you."

"Kurt," I say, and leave it there.

"Our diligence team has a handful of open items. Most of them are for Diane, they're boring, I'll spare you. But there's one I wanted to ask in the room, because our IP counsel asked me to and because, honestly, I'm curious." He glances down at something. "It's for the patent schedule. The frame on the corner, the moment frame, what your people call the knee. Who's the inventor of record?"

Ruth turns to Adam, and so does everybody else. That's what happens in this company when somebody asks who: they turn to Adam. He's the voice. He's the one in the photo on the scissor lift with his arms out.

Adam opens his mouth.

I put the file on the walnut before anything comes out of it. It's a plain manila hanging file with the hook still on, *UNIT 001* on the tab in my old handwriting, and it makes a small flat sound on the wood that everybody hears. I open it and turn it around so the camera on the end of the table can see.

"A receipt from the steel yard on Market Avenue," I say, "for the frame stock for the first knee, made out to P. Sobczak. A receipt from Grand Rapids Glass for two lights with a polished edge, P. Sobczak. The counterman wrote *good luck w/ the corner* on it, you can read that yourself. A photo of the first room on sawhorses in my father's garage, and that's my father. And a lease. This company has rented that corner from me for a dollar a year since 2018."

The room goes still. It was already quiet; this is something else, the way the shop floor goes still for half a second when somebody drops a sheet of glass and everybody waits to hear if it broke.

On the wall, the old Lindqvists look down out of their oil paint.

Kurt leans toward his camera. "Can you hold the first one up?"

Ruth picks up the steel receipt by its corner and holds it to the lens, and Kurt reads it off his screen in Minneapolis, slowly, the way you read a name on a cake. "P. Sobczak." He writes something down. "All right."

"You don't have all of it yet," I say.

Kurt looks up from his notes. Down the table, Nadia's stylus stops.

"The knee, the corner, the lift points," I say. "The crane bay in this building, which every engineer on the job told me was vanity. The escrow rule your lawyers keep asking about. The name, too. I named the company in a garage on Garfield Avenue while he was outside on the phone with a bank, because we had two desks and nothing else." I keep my hands flat on the walnut. "Put it in your schedule, Kurt. If you're going to try to buy it, you should know whose it is."

Ruth doesn't say anything. Del Ortiz sits back in his chair and folds his hands on his stomach, like a man at a ball game whose team just did something he paid to see.

"Noted," Kurt says, and smiles, and writes it down.

I sit there with my hands flat on the black walnut on either side of the file, and I can feel the grain under my palms. I've been waiting since the spring before last for somebody in this building to say it out loud in a room with a table in it. Nobody was going to. So it's my voice, and a steel receipt held up to a camera, and a man in a turtleneck in Minneapolis who wants to buy it writing it down, and it doesn't feel like anything I thought it would. It feels like paying a bill.

Adam's eyes are on me. His mouth is shut. He doesn't add anything, and he doesn't do the face where he checks whether I liked it. He just holds still and waits to see what it costs.

"Gordon," Ruth says, "before we go on, I think the board needs the full cash picture. Including the facility."

Gordon closes his eyes for a second. Then he opens them and turns the laptop around, toward the table, which is pointless, because nobody can read a laptop screen from eight feet away. It's a gesture, a man showing you his hands.

"We have nine weeks of cash," he says, "at current burn."

The table waits.

"The deposits," Gordon goes on, "are held in escrow under the terms of sale. They're released to the company when steel is cut for the customer's unit. We can't use them for operations or for working capital. To start building the six thousand units in the order book, we would need to buy steel, glass and insulated panel in advance, and fund additional labor, for approximately six to eight weeks before the first escrow releases. The estimated requirement," and he swallows, "is seven point four million dollars."

"Which the company doesn't have," says Ruth.

"Which the company does not have."

"And the facility, Gordon."

He turns down the table to Adam. Adam nods, once, very small.

"In October," Gordon says, reading it off the screen carefully, like he's afraid he'll get it wrong in front of God, "to make payroll, Mr. Kerrigan borrowed nine million dollars personally from Northgate Capital in Chicago and lent it to the company, against all thirty-four percent of his shares. If our cash falls below six million on the test date, Northgate can call it and take them."

There's a sound in the room. I think it's me.

I'm not looking at Gordon. I'm looking at Adam, across the walnut, with the river behind him and the morning light coming in over his shoulders so his face is half in shadow. He doesn't look away. Whatever's on my face right now, he sits there and takes it, with his hands folded on the table in front of him and his espresso going cold.

October. In October we were in the kitchen. In October I made eggs and ate them over the sink. In October he came home in the middle of the night and went up the good stairs to the room across the hall, and he'd just borrowed nine million dollars with his whole life on the line, and he didn't knock on my door.

He called somebody, though. I know who he called. She's sitting at the side table with a black notebook, not looking at me, writing nothing.

"So the board understands the picture," Ruth says. Her voice is very calm, the voice for the end of a long run. "Halvard will fund the build. Halvard will fund working capital from signing, buy out the Northgate facility at close, and assume the payroll. Every one of those six thousand people gets their room. Six thousand rooms with no post, Pip. Kurt has agreed in principle to build the original for the existing order book." She lets that sit. "After that, they'll do what they'll do."

"They'll close Clyde Park," I say. My voice comes out of me low and level, and I don't know where it's coming from. "And put a post in it."

"In thirty-six months, in writing. I asked." Ruth folds her hands on the walnut. "Without Halvard, the company doesn't have the money to build the orders your video created. Without your vote, there's no Halvard. Without either, in nine weeks we stop making payroll, and Northgate takes Adam's shares, and a fund in Chicago gets a third of this company and a vote on what happens to it." She finds me down the length of the table. "You understand that your veto doesn't protect the plant anymore. It's starving it."

Six thousand orange dots on a gray map. *The one with no post. The one that can leave. I want the room she took.* Every one of them came in because I stood on Monroe Avenue in the dark with my hand on the glass. Every one of them is sitting in a bank in Cleveland, waiting for me to cut steel I can't pay for.

I made them, and I'm the reason they can't get built.

"Pip," Ruth says, not unkindly. "It's your call."

My phone is in my coat pocket with the whiteboard photo on it: Irene's numbers, four thousand one hundred a year, Darnell's paint booth, Benny's forklift, a box with four question marks, Benny's raise in Benny's handwriting. *Money before the money.* I was going to stand up and show it to them. I was going to show it to him first, at seven, in the room, and I was late.

"I want thirty days," I say.

"For what, Pip?"

"To find the money before the money."

"From where?"

"I don't know yet."

Ruth holds my eyes. Next to her, Nadia writes something on her iPad with her stylus, tiny, tidy.

"Halvard's exclusivity runs to the end of February," Ruth says slowly. "Kurt?"

"We can live with thirty days," says Kurt from the screen, smiling. "We're very patient people in Minnesota."

"Thirty days," says Ruth. "Del?"

"Thirty days," says Del Ortiz, and peers at me over his glasses, and taps his UAW pin once, absently, with one finger, like he's touching wood.

"Thirty days," says Adam, to me.

Ruth nods and makes a note. Then she turns a page on her tablet. "Item four," she says, "the motion to censure."

I'd forgotten. I'd honestly forgotten I was item four.

"Moved by me," Ruth says, "seconded by Nadia. Any discussion? No. All in favor."

Ruth raises her hand, and Nadia raises hers.

"Opposed."

Del Ortiz raises his hand.

Then Adam raises his.

I watch him do it. He does it slowly, without any face at all, his hand coming up off the walnut and stopping at about the height of his ear, as if he's asking a teacher a question he already knows the answer to. Ruth turns her head and stares at him. He doesn't look at Ruth. He looks at me.

"Two in favor, two opposed," says Ruth, after a moment, very evenly. "Pip, you're recused, so the motion fails." She turns to the screen. "Thank you, Kurt. We'll be in touch."

The screen goes dark.

I sit in my chair and look at the black walnut between my hands. Thirty days. Seven point four million dollars. A thermos rolling in the cup holder of my truck. *Is she in yet.*

"Thirty days," I say again, to the table, to nobody, and stand up, and go.
