## Chapter 18 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

On Thursday morning at six-fifteen I call my husband from the truck in the plant lot and ask him to come to Monroe with me.

I've had the phone in my hand since six. I've written the sentence four ways in my head. *I need you at the mill. Gil's a talker, you're a talker. Simone says ask.* In the end I just call, and he picks up on the first ring, wide awake, and I say, "I'm driving to Great Lakes Specialty at eight to ask Gil Chamberlain for net sixty on steel, and I want you to come, and I'm telling you before, not after."

There's a pause on the line.

"Okay," he says.

"Don't say okay like that."

"I'm not saying it like anything. I'm saying okay." Another pause. "Can I drive?"

"No."

"You drive like a nun."

"I drive like a person who's going to live to see seventy."

"I'll bring coffee," he says, and hangs up before I can tell him not to.

He's at the plant at seven-forty with two paper cups, both from the gas station on Clyde Park Avenue, both terrible in an ordinary way. He gets in on the passenger side of my truck and hands me one and buckles his seat belt and looks straight ahead through the windshield, and we drive two hours across the bottom of Michigan in the snow without either of us saying much of anything, and it's the best two hours I've spent with him in a long time. I don't know what that says about us. I don't think about it on I-94.

Great Lakes Specialty Steel is a long low building on the water in Monroe, between a power plant and a marina full of boats wrapped in white plastic for the winter like leftovers. Gil Chamberlain meets us in the lobby in a fleece vest and a hard hat with his name on it. He's sixty-one and has sold me every stick of steel in every room I've ever built, starting with the frame stock for Unit 001 in 2014, when I drove down here in my father's Buick with eleven hundred dollars in cash in an envelope and he looked at me like I was a kid selling candy for a school.

"Pip," he says, and hugs me, which he's never done. "I saw the video. My wife cried. She said, *Gil, that's the girl with the envelope*."

"That's me."

"And you must be the husband." He shakes Adam's hand, wary. "I've heard a lot about you."

"All bad?" says Adam.

"Mostly from the business pages."

We sit in his office, which has a window onto the rolling mill where a long orange bar of steel goes by on rollers every few minutes, glowing, like a sunset being delivered. Gil offers coffee. We decline. Gil sits behind his desk with his hands folded on his stomach and says, "So what can I do for you," in the voice of a man who already knows.

I open my mouth. I have it all ready. The tape from Simone's calculator is in my coat pocket, rolled up with its rubber band. Steel tonnage. Weeks. Escrow at cut. I'm going to say it the way I'd explain a weld.

Adam puts a hand on my arm. Lightly. Just two fingers, just above the wrist.

"Can I?" he says.

I look at him. He's asking. He's asking me in front of Gil Chamberlain, in Monroe, with steel going by in the window.

"Go ahead," I say.

And he tells Gil the truth.

Not a pitch. I keep waiting for the pitch. I know every pitch he's got; I've sat through them at a hundred dinners. I wait for the nose voice, the Rotary voice, the voice for the poodle lady. It doesn't come. He sits forward in his chair with his elbows on his knees and tells Gil Chamberlain, a man he's met four minutes ago, that the company is nine weeks from not making payroll. He tells him about Nashville, that it was his idea and his mistake. He tells him there's a buyer from Minneapolis who'll fix all of it and probably close the plant in three years, and that his wife has a plan to fix it without the buyer, and the plan needs Gil to wait sixty days for his money instead of thirty, and if the plan fails, Gil might wait a lot longer than sixty days.

"I'm telling you that part," Adam says, "because you should know it before you decide. Not after."

He doesn't look at me when he says that. He doesn't have to.

Gil sits there for a long time with his hands folded on his stomach. Outside the window, another bar goes by, orange, glowing.

"Huh," Gil says finally.

"I know," says Adam.

"Nobody's ever told me the bad part first." Gil looks at me. "He always like this?"

"No," I say.

"Huh," says Gil again. He unfolds his hands and picks up a pen. "Net sixty. Eighteen months. I want the first four hundred knees' worth of tube on one PO so I can schedule the rolling. And I want a photo of me standing in one of those rooms with no post. My wife'll put it on the fridge."

On the way out, in the lobby, Adam stops and looks at a framed photograph on the wall of a man in a fedora standing next to a pile of steel in 1946. He looks at it a long time.

"That was good," I say. "In there."

"I didn't do anything."

"You told him the bad part first."

"I've been practicing." He doesn't look away from the photo. "In the car. In my head. I've been telling people the bad part first in my head for two days. It's horrible, Pip. It's like taking a cold shower on purpose."

"Get used to it," I say. "It's how welders talk."

Holland Glazing gives us net forty-five that afternoon, because they heard Great Lakes gave us sixty and they hate Great Lakes. Grand Rapids Glass matches it by four o'clock, because they've been mad at Holland since a softball game in 2011 that nobody will tell me about. Simone texts me at four-thirty: *Glass matched. Your hole is now 2.3.* And under it, a second text, a minute later: *I am not asking how. I assume someone told the bad part first.*

I show it to Adam in the truck. He reads it and laughs, the real one, and then stops laughing and looks out the window at the snow on I-94.

"She's very good," he says.

"She's the best person in that building."

"I know."

"You should have hired her for something besides listening to you at one in the morning."

He doesn't say anything for a mile. Then: "I know," again, quieter.

Back at Clyde Park it's almost six and second shift's been on for three hours. I drop Adam at his car by the fence. He stands there in the snow with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the room on its cribbing in the yard, the light on inside, the glass corner glowing.

"I'll see you at home," he says.

"Okay."

"Don't say okay like that."

I almost laugh. I don't. I go into the plant.

Lolo's waiting for me at the time clock with her arms crossed.

"Break room," she says. "Now."

There are four people in the break room: Lolo, Benny, Irene, and Darnell, back from three days off with the baby, holding a paper coffee cup and looking like a man who hasn't slept since Sunday. There's a piece of printer paper on the table between them. Somebody printed it out. It has the Halvard logo at the top.

"Darnell's wife's sister," Lolo says, before I can ask. "Cleaning the glass room on four. It was on the table. It's a draft."

I pick it up. *Project North — Workforce Retention Framework (DRAFT — NOT FOR DISTRIBUTION)*. A retention pool for Clyde Park employees who stay through the transition. Eighteen months of base pay, paid as a lump sum at the end of month eighteen, for anyone who's still there.

Eighteen months of base pay. I do the arithmetic for Benny without meaning to. For Lolo. For Irene.

"Is it real?" says Lolo.

"It's a draft."

"Is it real, hon?"

"I think Kurt would pay it," I say. "Yes."

Lolo nods slowly. She takes her glasses off and lets them fall on their beaded chain, and I wait for the look, the one my father used to warn about, and it doesn't come. She just looks tired. She looks sixty-three.

"Then I want to tell you something straight," she says. "You're not going to like it."

"Okay."

"I'm sixty-three. I've got a sister in Port Charlotte with a spare room and a lanai. I've been working a line since I was nineteen years old. Lindqvist, then the cabinet place, then here. Forty-four years. My knees are shot. My hands go numb at night." She holds one up. It's steady, but the knuckles are swollen and gray. "Eighteen months of base. You know what that is to me? That's Florida. That's the lanai. That's me getting out of here before my hands go all the way."

I don't say anything. I can't.

"And your plan," Lolo says, very gently, "is Saturdays."

"Lolo—"

"I know what it is. I did the math on the board for you. I'm not stupid. I know it's a good plan. I know it keeps the plant open. I know Halvard's going to close it in three years and put a post in your corner, and I know that's a sin. But I'm telling you, because you're your daddy's girl and he'd want me to tell you straight." She puts her glasses back on. "If they put it to me, I take the money. I'll train your second shift. I'll work your Saturdays till you get your answer. But if somebody asks me, Lolo, plan or pool, I'm saying pool."

Benny's looking at the floor. Irene's looking at the printout. Darnell's looking at his coffee like there's a baby at the bottom of it.

"Okay," I say, and my voice comes out wrong, and I clear my throat. "Okay. Thank you for telling me straight."

"Somebody has to," Lolo says. "You married a man who doesn't."

I go out to the room in the yard at seven. I sit at my desk with the lights off and the yard light coming in through the glass corner, white on the snow. I'm still sitting there at eight-fifteen when the knock comes, one knuckle, on the glass.

He's carrying a white paper bag from the Polish deli on Leonard. I can smell it from here. Kiełbasa. Rye bread. He doesn't hand it to me. He puts it on the corner of the desk and stands there.

"I wasn't going home," he says. "I didn't think you were either."

"Lolo's going to vote for Halvard."

He sits down in the chair by the radiator. He doesn't put his feet up. "She told you that?"

"Straight. She said somebody has to."

He takes that. "She's not wrong."

"She's not wrong about anything." I put my head back against the chair and look at the ceiling, cedar, the boards I picked one at a time in a lumberyard in Cedar Springs. "She wants Florida. She's earned Florida. And I'm asking her for Saturdays."

"You're not asking her for Saturdays. You're asking her to bet on you."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing." He leans forward. "Gil bet on you today. For sixty days. Holland bet on you because they hate Gil. Simone's betting on you at one in the morning with a calculator. That's not Saturdays. That's what you've got that Kurt doesn't. People will bet on you if you ask them."

"You didn't."

It comes out before I can stop it. It hangs there in the cold room.

"No," he says. "I didn't. I bet on me. Nashville. Northgate. All of it. I bet on me, and I didn't ask you, and it's the worst bet I ever made."

I look at him. In the white light from the yard he looks older than he did in October. He looks like a man who's been taking cold showers on purpose for two days.

I want to say something, and I don't know what, and I don't trust anything I'd say, so I don't say anything. I get up instead. I come around the desk and stand in front of the chair by the radiator. He looks up at me.

"Can I—" I say.

"Yes," he says.

He says it before I finish the sentence.

I've been hearing that sentence in my head for three weeks. *He said yes before I finished the sentence.* In a navy sweater, in my room, with a ficus on my desk. It's been sitting in me like a splinter. And now I'm standing in front of him in the yard in the dark and he's said it to me, the same way, before I got to the end, and the splinter comes out.

I kiss him.

It's not graceful. I'm standing and he's sitting and the chair creaks and his hand comes up and finds the back of my neck, careful, like he's checking a weld, and he tastes like gas-station coffee and cold air, and I forgot. I forgot what he feels like. Two years in the next room and I forgot the exact pressure of his hand, and now my body remembers it all at once, the way your feet remember stairs in a house you grew up in.

When I pull back he's still holding the back of my neck. His eyes are closed.

"You didn't let me finish the sentence," I say.

He opens his eyes. "What was the end of it?"

"*Can I ask you something.*"

He stares at me. Then he starts to laugh, helplessly, with his forehead against my stomach, and I stand there with my hands in his hair in my office in the yard and let him, and I think, very clearly, the way you think the name of a part: *I love him*. I've been carrying it around in my pocket for two years like a lead weight and I didn't know what it was until it got lighter.

"Ask me," he says into my sweater.

"Later," I say. "Eat your kiełbasa."
