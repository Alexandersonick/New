## Chapter 18 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

On Thursday morning, in the dark, I call my husband from the truck in the plant lot and ask him to come to Monroe with me.

I've had the phone in my hand for half an hour, and I've written the sentence four ways in my head, and every one of them sounds like a memo. In the end I just call, and he picks up on the first ring, wide awake, and I say, "I'm driving to Great Lakes Specialty at eight to ask Gil Chamberlain for net sixty on steel, and I want you to come, and I'm telling you before, not after."

There's a pause on the line, long enough for a semi to go by out on Clyde Park.

"Okay," he says, very carefully, like the word might break.

"Don't say okay like that."

"I'm not saying it like anything, I'm saying okay." Then, after another pause: "Can I drive, at least?"

"Absolutely not, it's my truck."

"You drive like a nun."

"I drive like somebody who plans to see seventy."

"I'll bring coffee," he says, and hangs up while I'm still telling him not to.

He's at the plant twenty minutes early with two paper cups, both from the gas station on Clyde Park Avenue, both terrible in an ordinary way. He gets in on the passenger side of my truck and hands me one and buckles his seat belt and looks straight ahead through the windshield, and we drive two hours across the bottom of Michigan in the snow without either of us saying much of anything, and it's the best two hours I've spent with him in years. I don't know what that says about us, and I don't think about it on I-94.

Great Lakes Specialty Steel is a long low building on the water in Monroe, between a power plant and a marina full of boats wrapped in white plastic for the winter like leftovers. Gil Chamberlain meets us in the lobby in a fleece vest and a hard hat with his name on it. He's sixty-one and has sold me every stick of steel in every room I've ever built, starting with the frame stock for Unit 001 in 2014, when I drove down here in my father's Buick with eleven hundred dollars in cash in an envelope and he looked at me like I was a kid selling candy for a school.

"Pip," he says, and hugs me, which he's never done. "I saw the video, and my wife cried. She said, *Gil, that's the girl with the envelope*."

"That's me, the envelope girl."

"And you must be the husband." He shakes Adam's hand, wary. "I've heard a lot about you."

"All of it bad?" says Adam.

"Mostly from the business pages."

We sit in his office, which has a window onto the rolling mill where a long orange bar of steel goes by on rollers every few minutes, glowing, like a sunset being delivered. Gil offers coffee and we both decline, holding up our gas-station cups. Gil sits behind his desk with his hands folded on his stomach and says, "So what can I do for you," in the voice of a man who already knows.

I open my mouth with it all ready. The tape from Simone's calculator is in my coat pocket, rolled up with its rubber band, steel tonnage, weeks, escrow at cut, and I'm going to say it plainly, in order.

Adam puts a hand on my arm, lightly, just two fingers above the wrist.

"Can I?" he says.

He's asking me, in front of Gil Chamberlain, in Monroe, with steel going by in the window.

"Go ahead," I say, and sit back.

And he tells Gil the truth.

I keep waiting for the pitch. I know every pitch he's got; I've sat through them at a hundred dinners. I wait for the nose voice, the voice for the poodle lady, and it doesn't come. He sits forward in his chair with his elbows on his knees and tells Gil Chamberlain, a man he met four minutes ago, that the company is nine weeks from not making payroll. He tells him about Nashville, that it was his idea and his mistake. He tells him there's a buyer from Minneapolis who'll fix all of it and probably close the plant in three years, and that his wife has a plan to fix it without the buyer, and the plan needs Gil to wait sixty days for his money instead of thirty, and if the plan fails, Gil might wait a lot longer than sixty days.

"I'm telling you that part," Adam says, "because you should know it before you decide."

He doesn't look at me when he says that, and he doesn't have to.

Gil sits there with his thumbs going around each other. Outside the window, another bar goes by, orange, glowing, and then another.

"Huh," Gil says finally, to the window.

"I know it's a lot," says Adam.

"Halvard's been buying tube from a mill in Indiana for ten years," Gil says. "Eight thousand rooms of knee frame is the biggest single order I'll write this year, and I'd rather it went through my rolling mill than theirs." He turns his chair to me. "And you paid cash in an envelope in 2014. I remember who pays."

"Huh," says Adam.

Gil unfolds his hands and picks up a pen. "Net sixty, for eighteen months. I want the first four hundred knees' worth of tube on one PO so I can schedule the rolling. And I want a photo of me standing in one of those rooms with no post. My wife'll put it on the fridge."

On the way out, in the lobby, Adam stops in front of a framed photograph on the wall of a man in a fedora standing next to a pile of steel in 1946, and stays there with his hands in his pockets.

"That was good in there," I say.

"Gil said yes because of eight thousand rooms of tube and an envelope from 2014," he says, without looking away from the photo. "I just sat next to the envelope."

"You didn't do the nose voice."

"I'm out of practice," he says, and doesn't smile, and doesn't move away from the man in the fedora for a while.

Holland Glazing calls my phone with net forty-five that afternoon, because they heard Great Lakes gave us sixty and they hate Great Lakes. Grand Rapids Glass matches it by the time we're past Jackson, because they've been mad at Holland since a softball game in 2011 that nobody will tell me about. Simone texts me a little after that: *Glass matched. Your hole is now 2.3.* And under it, a second text, a minute later: *I am not asking how. I assume Holland still hates Great Lakes.*

I show it to Adam in the truck. He reads it and laughs out loud, and then stops laughing and looks out the window at the snow on I-94.

"She's very good," he says.

"She's the best person in that building."

"I know she is."

"You should have hired her for something besides listening to you after midnight."

He goes a mile without answering, then says "I know" again, quieter.

Back at Clyde Park it's almost six and second shift's been on for three hours. I drop Adam at his car by the fence. He stands there in the snow with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the room on its cribbing in the yard, the light on inside, the glass corner glowing.

"I'll see you at home," he says.

"Okay."

"Don't say okay like that."

I almost laugh, and then I don't, and I go into the plant.

Lolo's waiting for me at the time clock with her arms crossed.

"Break room," she says, and doesn't wait to see if I follow.

There are four people in the break room: Lolo, Benny, Irene, and Darnell, back from three days off with the baby, holding a paper coffee cup, with the eyes of somebody who hasn't slept since Sunday. There's a sheet of printer paper on the table between them with the Halvard logo at the top.

"Darnell's wife's sister cleans the glass room on four," Lolo says, without being asked. "It was sitting on the table, and it's a draft."

I pick it up. *Project North — Workforce Retention Framework (DRAFT — NOT FOR DISTRIBUTION)*. A retention pool for Clyde Park employees who stay through the transition. Eighteen months of base pay, paid as a lump sum at the end of month eighteen, for anyone who's still there.

I do the arithmetic without meaning to, for Benny, and for Lolo, and for Irene.

"Is it real?" says Lolo.

"It's a draft."

"Is it real, hon?"

"Yes, I think Kurt would pay it," I say.

Lolo nods slowly and takes her glasses off and lets them fall on their beaded chain, and I wait for the look, the one my father used to warn about, and it doesn't come. She just looks tired.

"Then I want to tell you something straight," she says. "You're not going to like it."

I pull out a chair and sit down across from her.

"You know about my sister in Port Charlotte, and the lanai. I've been working a line since I was nineteen years old, Lindqvist, then the cabinet place, then here, forty-four years. My knees are shot and you know about my hands." She holds one up, and it's steady, but the knuckles are swollen and gray. "Eighteen months of base. You know what that is to me? That's Florida, that's the lanai, that's me getting out of here before my hands go all the way."

I open my mouth and nothing comes out of it.

"And your plan," Lolo says, very gently, "is Saturdays."

"Lolo—"

"I know what it is, I did the math on the board for you, I'm not stupid. I know it's a good plan and it keeps the plant open. I know Halvard's going to close it in three years and put a post in your corner, and I know that's a sin. But I'm telling you, because you're your daddy's girl and he'd want me to tell you straight." She folds her arms. "If they put it to me, I take the money. I'll train your second shift, and I'll work your Saturdays till you get your answer. But if somebody asks me, Lolo, plan or pool, I'm saying pool."

Benny's looking at the floor. Irene's looking at the printout. Darnell's looking at his coffee like there's a baby at the bottom of it.

Irene is the one who moves. She picks up the printout and folds it in half and in half again, small and square, and puts it in her shirt pocket.

"My other sister's in Fort Myers," she says. "I'd rather work Saturdays than live with her."

"I'm just listening, boss," Benny says to the floor. "Somebody in here should."

"I'll do Saturdays," says Darnell, "if somebody comes and holds the baby."

"Bring her in," Lolo says. "We'll teach her to tack."

"Okay," I say, and my voice comes out wrong, and I clear my throat. "Thank you, all of you, for telling me straight."

"Somebody has to," Lolo says. "You married a man who doesn't."

I go out to the room in the yard and sit at my desk with the lights off and the yard light coming in through the glass, white on the snow. I'm still sitting there an hour later when the knock comes, one knuckle, on the glass.

He's carrying a white paper bag from the Polish deli on Leonard, and I can smell it from the desk, kiełbasa and rye bread. He doesn't hand it to me; he sets it on the corner of the desk and stands there.

"I wasn't going home," he says. "I didn't think you were either."

"Lolo's going to vote for Halvard."

He sits down in the radiator chair without putting his feet up. "She told you that?"

"Straight to my face. She said somebody has to."

He takes that in, nodding at the floor. "She isn't wrong about that."

"She's not wrong about anything." I put my head back against the chair and look at the ceiling, cedar, the boards I picked one at a time in a lumberyard in Cedar Springs. "She wants Florida and she's earned Florida, and I'm asking her for Saturdays."

"You're not asking her for Saturdays. You're asking her to bet on you."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing." He leans forward with his hands between his knees. "Gil bet on you today, for sixty days, and Holland bet on you because they hate Gil. Simone's betting on you with a calculator at her kitchen counter. That's what you've got that Kurt doesn't: people will bet on you if you ask them."

"You didn't."

It's out of my mouth too fast to stop, and it hangs there in the cold room.

"No," he says, "I didn't. I bet on me, Nashville, Northgate, all of it. I bet on me, and I didn't ask you, and it's the worst bet I ever made."

In the white light from the yard he looks older than he did in October, gray under the eyes, and his collar's loose.

I want to say something, and I don't know what, and I don't trust anything I'd say, so I get up instead. I come around the desk and stand in front of his chair, and he looks up at me.

"Can I—" I say.

"Yes," he says.

He says it before I finish the sentence.

I've been hearing that sentence in my head for three weeks. *He said yes before I finished the sentence.* In a navy sweater, in my room, with a ficus on my desk. It's been sitting in me ever since, a splinter. And now I'm standing in front of him in the yard in the dark and he's said it to me, the same way, before I got to the end, and the splinter comes out.

I kiss him.

It's not graceful. I'm standing and he's sitting and the chair creaks and his hand comes up and finds the back of my neck, careful, like he's checking a weld, and he tastes like gas-station coffee and cold air, and I forgot. I forgot what he feels like. All that time in the next room and I forgot the exact pressure of his hand, and now my body remembers it all at once, the way your feet remember stairs in a house you grew up in.

When I pull back he's still holding the back of my neck, and his eyes are closed.

"You didn't let me finish the sentence," I say.

He opens his eyes and blinks at me. "What was the end of it?"

"*Can I ask you something.*"

He stares at me, and then he starts to laugh, helplessly, with his forehead against my stomach, and I stand there with my hands in his hair in my office in the yard and let him, and I think, very clearly, like reading a part number off a print: *I love him*. I've been carrying it around in my pocket all this time, and I didn't know what it was until it got lighter.

"Ask me," he says into my sweater.

"Later," I say. "Eat your kiełbasa."
