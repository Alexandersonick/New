## Chapter 8 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

By Thursday the intern has moved into my plant.

He shows up just after seven in a new coat, an actual Carhartt, brown duck, so stiff it stands up when he takes it off, with the price sticker still on the sleeve. He carries his laptop under his arm. He asks Irene at the glazing table where I am, and Irene points with her chin, and he comes across the floor through the noise toward my bench with the face of a man walking into a church he wasn't raised in.

"Morning," I say. "That's a nice coat you've got."

"Lolo took me to Farm and Fleet." He sets the laptop on my bench, next to the vise. "She said if I was going to keep coming out here I couldn't dress like a European."

"And are you going to keep coming out here?"

"Marketing doesn't need me," he says, opening the laptop. "Marketing has a war room now. They have a whiteboard that says *VIRAL MOMENT, CAPITALIZE*. Somebody drew a crane on it, and the crane is bad, and I'd rather be here." He turns the screen toward me. "Also I built this, and I wanted you to see it before anybody made it into a slide."

It's a dashboard, a map of the United States in gray, with little orange dots on it. There are a lot of orange dots. There are so many orange dots around Grand Rapids and Chicago and Minneapolis and Detroit that they've run together into orange puddles. There's one in Juneau, and one in Key West, and one, absurdly, in the middle of Nevada where I'm pretty sure there isn't anything but a bomb range and a very good diner.

Down in the corner it says *2,247*. While I'm reading it, it says *2,251*.

"Each dot's a deposit," Tavi says. "For a Corner, the real one. It refreshes every ninety seconds."

"I know what a dot is, Tavi."

"Sorry," he says, and doesn't sound sorry. He sounds like a kid who built a train set. "You can click them and read the notes."

I don't want to click them. I click one, in Nevada.

*Bought this for my wife. She's been saying for twenty years she needs a room of her own. I watched the video and thought, she's right, she does. Please don't put a post in it.*

I click one in Kalamazoo.

*I want the room she took.*

I close the laptop, not hard, just closed.

"Thank you," I say, and mean it. "That's beautiful work."

"I can make you a version that just shows the number."

"Make me a version that doesn't exist."

He thinks about that seriously, nodding. Then he goes off to find Lolo, who's not even on shift yet, because apparently Lolo is his person now. That happens to people around Lolo. She adopts them by insulting their clothes.

The crew knows about the dots by nine. By ten they know the number, and by eleven it's a game. Every time somebody walks past the break room where Tavi's set the laptop up on the microwave cart, they call out the count. Two thousand three hundred. Two thousand three hundred forty. Darnell writes it on the whiteboard by the time clock where we usually write safety days.

*DAYS SINCE LOST-TIME INJURY: 211*
*ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP STOLE ONE: 2,388*

"Erase *stole*," I tell him.

"It's accurate, boss," Darnell says, capping the marker. "It was on Channel 6, and they had a graphic."

"I didn't steal anything, it's on a lease, and I'm the landlord."

"So what do you want, *evicted*?"

"Darnell, Tavi photographs everything in this building, so write something nicer."

Irene comes over from glazing, pulling off her cut gloves one finger at a time, and reads the board with her head tilted, the way she reads a pane for ripple. "*Relocated*, that's what we'd put on a work order," she says.

"*Relocated* is boring," Darnell says. "Nobody's going to make a video about *relocated*."

"Nobody's supposed to make a video about any of it," I say.

He erases *stole* with the side of his fist and writes *liberated*. I let that one go.

Irene doesn't go back to the glazing table. She stands there with her gloves in one hand, looking at the number, and her mouth moves a little while she does the math.

"My glass guy called from Holland," she says. "He saw it on the news like everybody. He wants to know does he hold float time for us."

"How much float time is he talking about?"

"He didn't say how much. He said the line books up by March, and if he doesn't hold it for us he's selling it to the people who make shower doors in Ohio." She folds the gloves over. "I told him I'd ask you first."

Two thousand three hundred and ninety-something rooms, each with two glass walls and no post. I know what a float line is. I know what it means when a man in Holland calls a glazier directly instead of calling purchasing, and it means he's heard something purchasing hasn't.

"Tell him to hold it," I say. "Tell him to hold it and not to send a bill."

"He's going to ask who's paying."

"Then tell him to call me, and I'll tell him about my mother's pierogi until he forgets the question."

Irene gives me a long flat look, the one she gives a pane with a bubble in it, and goes back to the glazing table to call Holland.

At lunch Benny finds me at my bench. He's got his sandwich in one hand and his earplugs hanging around his neck on their orange cord.

"Boss, got a question for you," he says.

I tell him to go ahead.

"Are we getting sold?"

I put my file down.

Benny's fifty-eight, and he's been at Clyde Park since the day we opened it in 2017, and before that he was twenty-two years at Lindqvist on the line, with my father and with Lolo, until Lindqvist moved the line to North Carolina and then moved North Carolina to Vietnam. He has a daughter at Grand Valley and a son who did a tour in Kuwait and a wife who does my mother's hair. He doesn't ask questions he doesn't need the answer to.

"Who told you that?" I ask, and keep my hand on the file.

"Darnell's wife's sister cleans offices downtown, Lindqvist building, nights. There's a word on a whiteboard up on four that says *North*." He takes a bite of sandwich. "Nobody calls a project *North* unless it's going to Minneapolis."

I think about slide eleven. Phase two, months six to eighteen. Holland, Michigan, and Monterrey, Mexico. The footnote in eight-point type.

I could tell him; he's standing right there. I could say: *There's an offer. I'm the no. As long as I'm the no, it doesn't happen.* That's true, all of it.

And then he'd ask how long I can be the no, and I wouldn't know, and he'd go back out on the floor with that, and by afternoon break the whole line would have it, and by Friday half of them would be on Indeed.

"Nobody's selling this plant, Benny," I say.

He keeps his eyes on me for a while, chewing. Benny knew me when I was eleven, sitting on a bucket by my father's welder, eating his potato chips.

"Okay," he says finally, wiping mustard off his thumb. "Your dad used to say a thing about the plant."

"Which thing?"

"*If you hear it in the break room, it's already true somewhere.*"

"My dad said a lot of things."

"He did," Benny says, and goes back to the forklift, chewing.

In the middle of the afternoon a woman named Carla Ruiz from Channel 6 calls my cell. I don't know how she got my cell. She's very nice about it. She's done a story on the video for the evening news, and she says into the phone that she'd love a follow-up, live, in the yard, by the room, the human angle, a founder and her—

"No," I say.

"Just five minutes. You wouldn't have to say anything about the company, just the room, and what it means to you."

"It's a room, Ms. Ruiz."

"Two point one million people watched it come out of a building," Carla Ruiz says, warmly. "I don't think it's just a room, Ms. Sobczak."

"Call Adam Kerrigan, he loves a camera," I say.

"I called him first, and he said to call you."

That stops me, and I don't know why it stops me. I tell her no again and hang up and sit there with the phone in my hand, thinking about Adam, who has never in his life turned down a camera, telling a reporter to call me.

Saturday morning my father comes to the yard.

He drives himself, in the 2004 Buick LeSabre he refuses to replace, with the vinyl roof peeling off in strips like the bark of a birch, and he parks it crooked across two spaces by the fence because he's seventy-four and can no longer be bothered with lines. He gets out slow, one leg and then the other. Stan Sobczak was a tool-and-die man for forty-one years, and his hands look it, big and nicked and gray at the knuckles, and his back looks it too, a little bent forward, like he's always about to look at something closer.

He's wearing his Lindqvist jacket and carrying a white paper bag from Marge's.

"Dzień dobry," he says, and then, "You stole your room."

"I took my room, Tato."

"Your mother says you stole it. Your mother says it was on the television." He walks past me to the room on its cribbing and stands in front of it with his hands in his pockets, looking up at it with the face he used to make at a part that came off the press perfect, the face of a man in church who's decided God's doing okay. "Huh."

"What's that *huh* supposed to mean?"

"It held up," he says.

"Of course it held up, you welded it."

"You designed it," he says, and goes around to the corner, the glass corner, and crouches down slowly to look at the joint at the base, where the knee frame goes under the floor. He puts his finger on it. "That was a bitch to weld."

"I know it was. I held the light for you."

"Three tries, it took us."

"It took four, and you know it took four."

"Four," he agrees. "You burned your eyebrow on the third one."

"I remember, Tato, I was there."

"I'm just saying," he says, and straightens up, one hand on his knee. "I'm saying it held up."

He doesn't go in yet. He stands with his finger still on the corner, and then he turns and squints across the yard at the plant, at the rolling door, at the sound of the line coming out of it.

"Who's welding your knees now?"

"The same four as always, Tato."

"Four," he says, and holds up his hand and looks at it like it belongs to somebody he used to work with. "I got one more good knee in me."

"Tato, you shake when you sign a birthday card."

"So I don't sign the card. I hold the stinger," he says, lowering the hand. "Your mother would kill me. She'd kill me and then she'd come out here and kill you for letting me."

"So you're telling me that's a no."

"That's a no from your mother," he says. "From me it's a maybe."

We go inside, and he sits down in the other chair, the radiator chair, the one where nobody sits, and opens the bag, and takes out two paczki and hands me one on a napkin, and eats his in about four bites, the powdered sugar going all down the front of the Lindqvist jacket. He chews and takes in the room, the desk, the cedar, and then the inside of the door.

At the door he stops chewing.

"Ha," he says.

"What's so funny about my door?"

"*Nobody decides alone*," he says, pointing with the last of his paczek. "That one was mine."

"That one was all of ours."

"No, no. That was mine. I said that in the garage. You wrote it down like the Ten Commandments." He shakes his head, delighted with himself. "I meant your mother and the thermostat. Your mother would turn it to sixty-two and I'd turn it to sixty-eight and we'd do that all winter, back and forth, like a war. *Nobody decides alone*. I said it about the thermostat, and you two kids put it on a door."

"It's still a good rule."

"It's a great rule for a thermostat." He brushes sugar off his front, and it doesn't come off. "Him, his writing's the third one?"

"His is the note under it, *Not seven-fifteen*."

He laughs at that, one bark. "He was always late."

"He was early, for a while."

My father turns from the door to me. He has my eyes, or I have his, gray and small and set deep, like they're staying out of the weather. For a second I think he's going to ask, and I don't know what I'll say if he does. My father and I don't talk about things. We talk about welds. We've had our whole relationship about welds. When I was nine he told me I was a good girl by handing me a rod of 6011 and letting me run a bead on a piece of scrap, and that was better than any hug I ever got in my life.

"You two used to have coffee in here," he says. "Your mother told me, every morning at seven o'clock."

"That was ages ago, Tato."

"Two years isn't ages."

"How do you know how long it's been?"

He shrugs, and his eyes go back to the door and stay there.

"Your mother knows everything," he says. "She just doesn't say it to you. She says it to me, in the car, on the way home from church. She talks the whole way. I'm a captive audience."

"So what does she say, in the car?"

He thinks about it, and then he reaches over and takes the rest of my paczek out of my hand, the half I haven't eaten, and eats it.

"She says you should eat," he says, with his mouth full.
