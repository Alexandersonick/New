## Chapter 8 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

By Thursday the intern has moved into my plant.

He shows up at seven-ten in a new coat, an actual Carhartt, brown duck, so stiff it stands up when he takes it off, with the price sticker still on the sleeve. He carries his laptop under his arm like a pizza box. He asks Irene at the glazing table where I am, and Irene points with her chin, and he comes across the floor through the noise toward my bench with the face of a man walking into a church he wasn't raised in.

"Morning," I say. "Nice coat."

"Lolo took me to Farm and Fleet." He sets the laptop on my bench, next to the vise. "She said if I was going to keep coming out here I couldn't dress like a European."

"Are you going to keep coming out here?"

"Marketing doesn't need me." He opens the laptop. "Marketing has a war room now. They have a whiteboard that says *VIRAL MOMENT — CAPITALIZE*. Somebody drew a crane on it. It's bad. The crane is bad. I'd rather be here." He turns the screen toward me. "Also I built this, and I wanted you to see it before anybody made it into a slide."

It's a dashboard. A map of the United States, gray, with little orange dots on it. There are a lot of orange dots. There are so many orange dots around Grand Rapids and Chicago and Minneapolis and Detroit that they've run together into orange puddles. There's one in Juneau. There's one in Key West. There's one, absurdly, in the middle of Nevada where I'm pretty sure there isn't anything but a bomb range and a very good diner.

Down in the corner it says *2,247*. While I'm looking it says *2,251*.

"Each dot's a deposit," Tavi says. "For a Corner. The real one. It refreshes every ninety seconds."

"I know what a dot is, Tavi."

"Sorry." He doesn't sound sorry. He sounds like a kid who built a train set. "You can click them. You can read the notes."

I don't want to click them. I click one. Nevada.

*Bought this for my wife. She's been saying for twenty years she needs a room of her own. I watched the video and thought, she's right, she does. Please don't put a post in it.*

I click Kalamazoo.

*I want the room she took.*

I close the laptop. Not hard. Just closed.

"Thank you," I say. "That's beautiful work."

"I can make you a version that just shows the number."

"Make me a version that doesn't exist."

He thinks about that seriously, nodding. Then he goes off to find Lolo, who's not even on shift yet, because apparently Lolo is his person now. That happens to people around Lolo. She adopts them by insulting their clothes.

The crew knows about the dots by nine. By ten they know the number, and by eleven it's a game. Every time somebody walks past the break room where Tavi's set the laptop up on the microwave cart, they call out the count, like a deer camp tally. Two thousand three hundred. Two thousand three hundred forty. Darnell writes it on the whiteboard by the time clock where we usually write safety days.

*DAYS SINCE LOST-TIME INJURY: 211*
*ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP STOLE ONE: 2,388*

I make him erase *stole*. He writes *liberated*. I let that one go.

At lunch Benny finds me at my bench. He's got his sandwich in one hand and his cigarette still behind his ear, unlit, like a pencil.

"Boss," he says. "Question."

"Go."

"Are we getting sold?"

I put my file down.

Benny's fifty-eight. He's been at Clyde Park since the day we opened it in 2017, and before that he was twenty-two years at Lindqvist on the line, with my father and with Lolo, until Lindqvist moved the line to North Carolina and then moved North Carolina to Vietnam. He has a daughter at Grand Valley and a son who did a tour in Kuwait and a wife who does my mother's hair. He doesn't ask questions he doesn't need the answer to.

"Who said that?" I ask.

"Darnell's wife's sister cleans offices downtown. Lindqvist building, nights. There's a word on a whiteboard up on four. Says *North*." He takes a bite of sandwich. "Nobody calls a project *North* unless it's going to Minneapolis."

I think about slide eleven. Phase two, months six to eighteen. Holland, Michigan. Monterrey, Mexico. The footnote in eight-point type.

I could tell him. He's standing right there. I could say: *There's an offer. I'm the no. As long as I'm the no, it doesn't happen.* That's true. That's all true.

And then he'd ask how long I can be the no, and I wouldn't know, and he'd go back out on the floor with that, and by two o'clock the whole line would have it, and by Friday half of them would be on Indeed.

"Nobody's selling this plant, Benny," I say.

He looks at me for a while. Benny knew me when I was eleven, sitting on a bucket by my father's welder, eating his potato chips.

"Okay," he says. "Your dad used to say that thing. About the plant."

"Which thing?"

"*If you hear it in the break room, it's already true somewhere.*"

"My dad said a lot of things."

"He did," Benny says, and goes back to the forklift, chewing.

At two o'clock a woman named Carla Ruiz from Channel 6 calls my cell. I don't know how she got my cell. She's very nice about it. She's done a story on the video for the five o'clock. She'd love a follow-up. She'd love it live, in the yard, by the room, the human angle, a founder and her—

"No," I say.

"Just five minutes. You wouldn't have to say anything about the company. Just the room. What it means to you."

"It's a room."

"Two point one million people watched it come out of a building," Carla Ruiz says, warmly. "I don't think it's just a room, Ms. Sobczak."

"Call Adam Kerrigan," I say. "He loves a camera."

"I did. He said to call you."

That stops me. I don't know why that stops me. I tell her no again and hang up and sit there with the phone in my hand, thinking about Adam, who has never in his life turned down a camera, telling a reporter to call me.

Saturday morning my father comes to the yard.

He drives himself, in the 2004 Buick LeSabre he refuses to replace, with the vinyl roof peeling off in strips like the bark of a birch, and he parks it crooked across two spaces by the fence because he's seventy-four and can no longer be bothered with lines. He gets out slow. Stan Sobczak was a tool-and-die man for forty-one years, and his hands look it, big and nicked and gray at the knuckles, and his back looks it too, a little bent forward, like he's always about to look at something closer.

He's wearing his Lindqvist jacket. He has a white paper bag from Marge's in one hand.

"Dzień dobry," he says. "You stole your room."

"I took my room, Tato."

"Your mother says you stole it. Your mother says it was on the television." He walks past me to the room on its cribbing and stands in front of it with his hands in his pockets, looking up at it with the face he used to make at a part that came off the press perfect, a face like a man in church who's decided God's doing okay. "Huh."

"What?"

"It held up."

"Of course it held up. You welded it."

"You designed it." He goes around to the corner, the glass corner, and crouches down slowly to look at the joint at the base, where the knee frame goes under the floor. He puts his finger on it. "That was a bitch to weld."

"I know."

"Three tries."

"Four."

"Four," he agrees. "You burned your eyebrow."

"I remember, Tato. I was there."

"I'm just saying." He straightens up, one hand on his knee. "I'm saying it held up."

We go inside. He sits down in the other chair, the one by the radiator where nobody sits, and opens the bag, and takes out two paczki and hands me one on a napkin, and eats his in about four bites, the powdered sugar going all down the front of the Lindqvist jacket. He looks around the room while he chews. At the desk. At the cedar. At the inside of the door.

At the door he stops chewing.

"Ha," he says.

"What?"

"*Nobody decides alone*." He points with the last of his paczek. "That was mine."

"It was ours."

"No, no. That was mine. I said that in the garage. You wrote it down like the Ten Commandments." He shakes his head, delighted with himself. "I meant your mother and the thermostat. Your mother would turn it to sixty-two and I'd turn it to sixty-eight and we'd do that all winter, back and forth, like a war. *Nobody decides alone*. I said it about the thermostat, and you two kids put it on a door."

"It's a good rule."

"It's a great rule. For a thermostat." He brushes sugar off his front. It doesn't come off. "Him, his writing's the third one?"

"The note under it. *Not seven-fifteen*."

"Ha." He likes that. "He was always late."

"He was early. For a while."

My father looks at me. He has my eyes, or I have his, gray and small and set deep, like they're staying out of the weather. For a second I think he's going to ask, and I don't know what I'll say if he does. My father and I don't talk about things. We talk about welds. We've had our whole relationship about welds. When I was nine he told me I was a good girl by handing me a rod of 6011 and letting me run a bead on a piece of scrap, and that was better than any hug I ever got in my life.

"You two used to have coffee in here," he says. "Your mother told me. Every morning, she said. Seven o'clock."

"That was a long time ago."

"Two years isn't a long time."

"How do you know it's two years?"

He shrugs. He looks at the door again. He looks at it a long time.

"Your mother knows everything," he says. "She just doesn't say it to you. She says it to me, in the car, on the way home from church. She talks the whole way. I'm a captive audience."

"What does she say?"

He thinks about it. Then he reaches over and takes the rest of my paczek out of my hand, the half I haven't eaten, and eats it.

"She says you should eat," he says, with his mouth full.
