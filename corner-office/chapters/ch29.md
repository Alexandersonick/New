## Chapter 29 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

He goes to Nashville on Sunday morning, and he tells me before.

He tells me Saturday at noon, in the yard, standing by the room while the knee line runs behind us for the first voluntary Saturday in the history of the company. Sixty-eight people stayed. I counted. I didn't mean to count. I count things when I'm afraid of what they mean.

"Odell's telling them Monday morning," Adam says. "At shift start. He asked me to come down and stand next to him and not talk."

"Can you do that?"

"Not talk?" He thinks about it seriously. "I've been practicing."

"You talked for four minutes this morning in front of a hundred and forty people."

"Three minutes forty. Gordon timed it." He looks at the plant, at the open rolling door, at the yellow curtains on the knee line glowing with the arcs behind them. "Odell said, *Boss, you can stand there and look sorry. You're good at it now.*"

"You are good at it now."

"I've had a lot of practice this month." He puts his hands in his coat pockets. "I'll be back Monday night. Late. I'll text you when I land."

"Before or after?"

"Both," he says.

He flies out of Grand Rapids at seven on Sunday morning. At 7:15 he texts me from the gate, a photo of a de-icing truck spraying a regional jet orange, and under it: *Boarding. Didn't sleep. Odell says bring a tie. I don't own a tie that isn't for a pitch.*

I text back: *Wear the wrinkled shirt.*

On Monday morning, the eighth, the morning Nashville's line stops, I'm at the plant at five-thirty. I don't go to my bench. I don't go to the room. I stand in the break room with a cup of vending-machine hot chocolate the temperature of a bath, looking at the whiteboard.

*YES: 97. NO: 41. ABSTAIN: 3.*

Above it, Darnell's count: *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 8,206.*

At 8:02 my phone buzzes. It's a photo. Odell's floor in La Vergne, the polished concrete and the long white skylights and sixty people in gray T-shirts standing in a half-circle, and at the front, Odell in his tucked polo with his hands behind his back, talking. And next to him, half a step back, in a wrinkled white shirt and no tie, Adam, with his hands at his sides.

He's not talking. You can see it in the photo. His mouth's closed.

Under it, from a number I don't know: *Mr. Haynes asked me to send you this. He's doing ok. — Kiana*

Adam lands at 10:50 on Monday night. He texts me from the jet bridge, before: *Landed. Going to the room.*

I read it standing in the kitchen at my end of the counter.

I think about it for a long time. Then I type: *Come home.*

The dots come and go. Come and go. Four times.

*Tomorrow*, he writes. *At seven. In the room. If you'll be there.*

I stand at the counter with the phone. He wants it done where it stopped. Not a text at eleven at night from my end of the counter; the room, at seven, out loud.

*OK*, I write. Then, a second later: *I'll be there at five to.*

On Tuesday morning at six-forty-five I'm in my room in the yard at Clyde Park with the heater on and the moka pot going on a hot plate I brought from home. I'm standing at my desk because I can't sit. The sleeping bag's gone from under the desk. The toothbrush is gone from the windowsill. The two wrinkled shirts are gone from the coat hook. He took them all Sunday morning before he flew. He told me he would.

The afghan's still on the shelf, corners square.

The yard's black. First shift isn't in yet. The maples along Clyde Park are just starting to come up out of the dark, one branch at a time.

At six-fifty-five, headlights come down Clyde Park Avenue. Not the church lot. They turn in at the gate. I hear the code beep, my birthday, and the gate roll back on its track, and the car crosses the yard and stops by the fence, and the headlights go off.

He doesn't knock.

He stands outside the glass corner in his long gray coat with a paper cup in one hand, the brass eagle on the sleeve, and he waits, where I can see him, in the yard light. He doesn't come to the door. He stands where the post would be if there were a post.

I go to the door. I open it. The cold comes in.

"Hi," I say.

"Hi."

"It's six fifty-six."

"I know. I'm early." He looks at the paper cup in his hand. "I didn't bring yours. I figured you'd made it."

"I made it."

"In your pot."

"In my pot. It's terrible."

"I know," he says. "I'd like some."

I stand in the door of my room with one hand on the brass pull my father turned on his lathe in 2014, and I look at my husband standing in the snow in the yard of my plant, early, with one coffee, waiting to be asked.

"Adam," I say. "Do you want to come in?"

"Yes," he says. He doesn't say it before I finish the sentence. He waits for the whole thing, all of it, every word, and then he says it.

He comes in. He takes his coat off and hangs it on the hook by the door, where the shirts were. He sits down in the chair by the radiator and puts his feet up, crossed at the ankle, his good shoes with the salt lines, and the chair makes its creak, and the room settles a quarter inch. I pour the moka-pot coffee into the second mug, the one I brought from home too, the chipped one from the Polish Falcons fish fry, and hand it to him, and he holds it in both hands and drinks it and makes a face like a man drinking a tire.

"God," he says. "That's terrible."

"You've always hated it."

"I've always hated it," he agrees, and drinks some more.

I sit down at my desk. My chair. Set low, at my height, my feet flat on the floor. We sit there in the room with the heater ticking and the yard going gray outside the glass corner, him with his feet on the radiator and me at the desk, and neither of us says anything for a while. The light comes up. The first car turns in at the gate. Benny's Silverado, with its one bad headlight.

"Okay," I say. "What are we doing today?"

He looks at me over the mug.

That was his line. *Okay, boss, what are we doing today?* Every morning for four years, at five to seven, he'd come in and put his feet up and say that, and I'd tell him. I'd tell him the whole day. And he'd listen, and argue, and then go out and sell it.

I've just said it to him.

He doesn't say anything for a long moment. His face does something I don't have catalogued, and I'm not going to catalogue it. I'm going to let it be new.

"I'd like to go home," he says. "Tonight. If you'll have me. I'd like to sleep in our room, not the one across the hall. I'd like to fix the room with the ladder, finally, or at least take the ladder out of it so it's just a room. I'd like to be out here at five to seven, every working day I'm in town, in this chair, and then drive downtown and do my job, which is selling what you build. Honestly. Out loud." He sets the mug on the radiator. "And I'd like you to tell me, every morning, at seven, what you're going to do before you do it. And I'll tell you. Even if we don't agree. Especially if we don't. Like your mother and the thermostat."

"Who told you about my mother and the thermostat?"

"Your father. On Sunday. He called me while I was at the gate in Grand Rapids." He almost smiles. "He's never called me in eleven years. He said, *Kerrigan, it's Stan. I'm telling you before, I'm going to tell you a story about a thermostat.* And then he did. For twenty minutes. I almost missed the flight."

I put my hand over my mouth.

"He said to tell you he's turning it to sixty-eight," Adam says, "and your mother doesn't know yet."

I laugh. It comes up out of me from all the way down, from the bottom of two years, and I put my head down on the desk the way I did the night of the bowling hat, and laugh until I can't breathe, and he laughs too, and somewhere out in the yard Benny's slamming the door of his Silverado and looking at the room with the lights on and both of us in it, and I don't care, I don't care at all who sees.

When I lift my head he's still in the chair. He hasn't moved toward me. He's waiting.

"All of it?" I say. "Home. The ladder. Seven o'clock."

"All of it. You can say no to any of it. The ladder room's a big ask. There's a desk in there under the tax returns I'm going to have to look at. It's a very ugly desk."

"I know. I built it."

He blinks. "You built my old desk?"

"In the garage. 2014. Out of a door from the Habitat ReStore and two sawhorses I welded. You've had it in the room with the ladder for six years under a pile of tax returns." I look at him. "You didn't know I built it?"

"I thought you bought it at a yard sale."

"You thought I bought you a desk at a yard sale."

"It looked like a yard-sale desk, Pip."

"It's a *door*, Adam."

"I know that *now*."

I get up. I come around the desk. He watches me come. I stand in front of the chair by the radiator, and he looks up at me, and doesn't reach, and doesn't say anything. He waits. He's gotten so good at waiting. It's the hardest thing I've ever watched him learn.

"Yes," I say. "To all of it. Home. Seven o'clock. The ladder." I put my hand on his face. It's cold from the yard. "I'm telling you before."

"Before what?"

"Before this," I say, and I ask him with my eyes, and he nods, once, very small, and I kiss him.

It's not like the first time, three weeks ago, in the dark, when I forgot what he felt like. I remember now. I remember all of it. It's like the second weld on a joint you already know will hold, when you're not testing anything, you're just running a bead because the work is good and your hands know where to go. His hand comes up to the back of my neck. The mug falls off the radiator and doesn't break, because it's from the Polish Falcons, and those mugs have survived worse.

When I pull back, there's coffee on the floor and on my mother's rug. Neither of us looks at it.

"I love you," he says. Into my hair. Quietly. Not a pitch. Not a reframe. Just the thing, set down.

"I know," I say. "I love you too. You still have to iron the shirts."

He laughs against my neck.

Out in the yard, the knee line's coming on. Through the open rolling door I can hear the arcs start up under the yellow curtains, one, then another, the long crackle of the first beads of the day. The press brake goes *chunk*.

Benny's still standing by his truck in the snow, watching the room with his lunch cooler in his hand. He catches me looking at him through the glass corner.

He lifts his chin, once, and goes in to work.
