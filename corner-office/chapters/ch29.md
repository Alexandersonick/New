## Chapter 29 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

He goes to Nashville on Sunday morning, and he tells me a day ahead.

He tells me Saturday at noon, in the yard, standing by the room while the knee line runs behind us for the first voluntary Saturday in the history of the company. Sixty-eight people stayed. I counted them on my fingers against my leg, though I didn't mean to; I count things when I'm afraid of what they mean.

"Odell's telling them Monday morning at shift start," Adam says. "He asked me to come down and stand next to him and not talk."

"Can you do that, Adam?"

He thinks about it seriously, the way he thinks about a term sheet. "I've been practicing not talking."

"You talked for four minutes this morning in front of a hundred and forty people."

"Three minutes forty, Gordon timed it." He looks at the plant, at the open rolling door, at the yellow curtains on the knee line glowing with the arcs behind them. "Odell said, *Boss, you can stand there and look sorry. You're good at it now.*"

"You are good at it now."

"I've had a lot of practice this month." He puts his hands in his coat pockets. "I'll be back Monday night, late. I'll text you when I land."

"Text me when you take off, too."

"Both ends of the flight," he says.

He flies out of Grand Rapids early on Sunday morning. From the gate he texts me a photo of a de-icing truck spraying a regional jet orange, and under it: *Boarding. Didn't sleep. Odell says bring a tie. I don't own a tie that isn't for a pitch.*

I text back: *Wear the wrinkled shirt.*

On Monday morning, the eighth, the morning Nashville's line stops, I'm at the plant at five-thirty, and I don't go to my bench or to the room. I stand in the break room with a cup of vending-machine hot chocolate the temperature of a bath, looking at the whiteboard. Darnell's tally is still up, *YES: 97, NO: 41, ABSTAIN: 3*, and above it his count: *ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 8,206.*

Benny comes in at six with his lunch cooler, and Irene behind him with her thermos.

"Is it today, down in Tennessee?" Benny says.

"Eight o'clock our time," I say. "Seven theirs."

Irene unscrews her thermos lid and pours without looking. "Where are twenty-three people going to live? You can't put twenty-three people from Tennessee in that room in the yard. Your husband barely fits."

"I don't know yet."

Benny sets his cooler on the counter. "My cousin's got two duplexes off Clyde Park, empty since October. He complains about it every Wigilia, right through the fish."

"Would he rent to them?"

"He'd rent to a raccoon if it paid first and last." Benny opens the cooler and looks into it like there might be a better lunch in there today. "I'll call him. He owes me for a transmission."

He calls right there from the counter, and I get one side of it, mostly Polish, mostly swearing, and when he hangs up he says, "Two units, eight beds, and he wants a reference from you."

"From me?"

"He says he don't rent to Tennesseans without a priest or a Sobczak vouching."

Just after eight my phone buzzes. It's a photo of Odell's floor in La Vergne, the polished concrete and the long white skylights and sixty people in gray T-shirts standing in a half-circle, and at the front, Odell in his tucked polo with his hands behind his back, talking. Next to him, half a step back, in a wrinkled white shirt and no tie, stands Adam, with his hands at his sides.

Irene leans over my shoulder to see. "He's not talking," she says. "Look at his mouth. It's shut."

Under the photo, from a number I don't know: *Mr. Haynes asked me to send you this. He's doing ok. — Kiana*

Benny reads it over my other shoulder. "He looks like he's at a wake," he says. "Good. That's the right face for it."

At noon Gordon texts me, which he has never done: *Ruth sent a fruit basket. To Odell, per Adam. Odell wants to know what a persimmon is for.* I send back a photo of the box on the whiteboard with *NASH* in it, and a while later Gordon sends a thumbs-up, which I'd guess took him ten minutes to find.

Adam lands near eleven on Monday night and texts me from the jet bridge: *Landed. Going to the room.*

I read it standing in the kitchen at my end of the counter. I hold the phone a while with my thumb over the keys, and then I type: *Come home.*

While the dots are going, I take the chipped Polish Falcons mug down from the cupboard over his coffee maker, wash it, and set it by my keys so I won't forget it in the morning.

The dots come and go, and come and go again.

*Tomorrow*, he writes. *At seven, in the room, if you'll be there.*

I stand at the counter with the phone. He wants it done where it stopped, in the room, at seven, out loud.

*OK*, I write. Then, a second later: *I'll be there at five to.*

On Tuesday morning, in the dark, I'm in my room in the yard at Clyde Park with the heater on and the moka pot going on a hot plate I brought from home. I'm standing at my desk because I can't sit. I've wiped the desk twice already, and refolded the afghan, which didn't need it, and pushed the radiator chair an inch toward the heat and then back. The sleeping bag's gone from under the desk, and the toothbrush from the windowsill, and the two wrinkled shirts from the coat hook. He took them all Sunday morning on his way to the airport, and he told me he would.

The afghan's still on the shelf, corners square.

The yard's black and first shift isn't in yet. The maples along Clyde Park are still only shapes.

At five to seven, headlights come down Clyde Park Avenue and, instead of going on to the church lot, turn in at the gate. The code beeps, my birthday, and the gate rolls back on its track, and the car crosses the yard and stops by the fence, and the headlights go off.

He doesn't knock, and he doesn't come to the door. He stands outside the glass corner in his long gray coat with a paper cup in one hand, the brass eagle on the sleeve, and he waits where I can see him, in the yard light. He stands where the post would be if there were a post.

I go to the door and open it, and the cold comes in.

"Hi," I say, and he says it back.

"It's six fifty-six," I tell him.

"I know, I'm early." He lifts the paper cup an inch. "I didn't bring yours, I figured you'd made it."

"I made it in my pot, and it's terrible."

"I know it is," he says. "I'd like some of it anyway."

I stand in the door of my room with one hand on the brass pull my father turned on his lathe in 2014, and there's my husband standing in the snow in the yard of my plant, early, with one coffee, waiting to be asked.

"Adam," I say, "do you want to come in?"

He waits for the whole sentence, every word of it, and then he says, "Yes."

He comes in, takes his coat off and hangs it on the hook by the door, where the shirts were. He sits down in the radiator chair and puts his feet up, crossed at the ankle, his good shoes with the salt lines, and the chair makes its creak, and the room settles a quarter inch. I pour the moka-pot coffee into the second mug, the one I brought from home too, the chipped one from the Polish Falcons fish fry, and hand it to him, and he holds it in both hands and drinks it and makes a face like a man drinking a tire.

"God, that's terrible," he says.

"You've always hated my coffee."

"I've always hated it," he agrees, and drinks some more.

I sit down at my desk, in my chair, set low, at my height, my feet flat on the floor. We sit there with the heater ticking and the yard going gray outside the glass, him with his feet on the radiator and me at the desk, and neither of us says anything for a while. The light comes up, and the first car turns in at the gate, Benny's Silverado with its one bad headlight.

"Okay," I say, "what are we doing today?"

He goes still over the mug.

That was his line, *Okay, boss, what are we doing today?* Every morning for four years he'd come in just before seven and put his feet up and say that, and I'd tell him the whole day, and he'd listen, and argue, and then go out and sell it.

I've just said it to him.

He's quiet for a while. His face does something I don't have catalogued, and I leave it that way and let it be new.

"I'd like to go home," he says. "Tonight, if you'll have me. I'd like to sleep in our room, not the one across the hall. I'd like to fix the room with the ladder, finally, or at least take the ladder out of it so it's just a room. I'd like to be out here at five to seven, every working day I'm in town, in this chair, and then drive downtown and do my job, which is selling what you build, honestly, out loud." He sets the mug on the radiator. "And I'd like you to tell me, every morning, at seven, what you're going to do before you do it. And I'll tell you, even if we don't agree, especially if we don't, like your mother and the thermostat."

"Who told you about my mother and the thermostat?"

"Your father, on Sunday. He called me while I was at the gate in Grand Rapids." He almost smiles. "He's never called me in eleven years. He said, *Kerrigan, it's Stan. I'm telling you before, I'm going to tell you a story about a thermostat.* And then he did, for twenty minutes, and I almost missed the flight."

I put my hand over my mouth.

"He said to tell you he's turning it to sixty-eight," Adam says, "and your mother doesn't know yet."

The laugh comes up out of me from all the way down, from the bottom of everything since Nashville, and I put my head down on the desk the way I did the night of the bowling hat, and laugh until I can't breathe, and he laughs too, and somewhere out in the yard Benny's slamming the door of his Silverado and looking at the room with the lights on and both of us in it, and I don't care, I don't care at all who sees.

When I lift my head he's still in the chair, and he hasn't moved toward me. He's waiting.

"Home, the ladder, seven o'clock, all of it?"

"All of it, and you can say no to any of it. The ladder room's a big ask. There's a desk in there under the tax returns that I'm going to have to look at, and it's a very ugly desk."

"I know, I built it."

He blinks at me over the mug. "You built my old desk?"

"In the garage in 2014, out of a door from the Habitat ReStore and two sawhorses I welded. You've had it in the ladder room for six years under a pile of tax returns. You didn't know I built it?"

"I thought you bought it at a yard sale."

"You thought I bought you a desk at a yard sale."

"It looked like a yard-sale desk, Pip."

"It's a *door*, Adam, it's still got the hinge holes."

"I know that *now*," he says.

I get up and come around the desk, and he tracks me the whole way. I stand in front of his chair, and he tips his head back to see me, and doesn't reach, and doesn't say anything. He's gotten so good at waiting. It's the hardest thing I've seen him learn.

"Yes," I say, "to all of it, home and seven o'clock and the ladder." I put my hand on his face, cold from the yard. "I'm telling you before."

"Before what?"

"Before this," I say, and I ask him with my eyes, and he nods, once, very small, and I kiss him.

Three weeks ago, in the dark, I'd forgotten what he felt like, and now I remember all of it. It's the second bead on a joint you already know will hold, when you're running it because the work is good and your hands know where to go. His hand comes up to the back of my neck. The mug falls off the radiator and doesn't break, because it's from the Polish Falcons, and those mugs have survived worse.

When I pull back, there's coffee on the floor and on my mother's rug. Neither of us looks at it.

"I love you," he says into my hair, quietly, and then he stops talking.

"I know," I say, with my mouth against his temple. "I love you too, and you still have to iron the shirts."

He laughs against my neck.

Out in the yard the knee line's coming on, and through the open rolling door the arcs start up under the yellow curtains, one, then another, the long crackle of the first beads of the day. The press brake goes *chunk*.

Benny's still standing by his truck in the snow with his lunch cooler in his hand, and he catches me looking at him through the glass.

He lifts his chin, once, and goes in to work.
