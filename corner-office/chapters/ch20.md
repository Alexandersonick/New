## Chapter 20 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The story goes up on the *Furnishings Weekly* website just after six on Thursday morning, which is a strange hour to publish news about furniture unless you want it to be the first thing a lot of people in Grand Rapids read with their coffee.

HALVARD NEARS DEAL FOR VIRAL BACKYARD-OFFICE MAKER TWODESK, the headline says. Under it, a photo of Kurt Lindahl in a turtleneck at a trade show in Milan, and under that, the article. *Sources close to the transaction*. *Final approvals expected within weeks*. *Halvard is expected to consolidate Twodesk manufacturing into its existing North American footprint as part of a broader integration*. And, in the fourth paragraph, the one that matters: *A person familiar with Halvard's plans said the company's Clyde Park facility in Wyoming, Michigan, is likely to close within two years.*

Two years, when Ruth told the whole board thirty-six months, *in writing, I asked*.

I read it in my truck in the plant lot ahead of the shift bell, twice, and then I lay the phone face down on the dash and sit there with my hands on the wheel while first shift comes in across the lot under the lights: Benny's Silverado, Irene's Malibu, Darnell's minivan with the new baby seat in the back that he showed everybody on Monday like it was a trophy.

I can tell from the way they're walking that they've already read it.

By the time the knee line should be striking its first arc, there's no work on the floor. The machines are on, and the press brake is going *chunk* because Benny turned it on by habit, but the knee line's standing under the yellow curtains with their hoods pushed up on their heads, talking. The glazing table's empty, because Irene's in the break room with Darnell and half of first shift, standing in their coats around the microwave cart where Tavi used to set up his laptop with the dots. Somebody's printed out the article and taped it to the whiteboard by the time clock, right over the count.

*ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 7,519.*

And under it, in Darnell's handwriting, in red: *DAYS UNTIL THEY CLOSE US: ???*

I stand in the doorway, and every face in the room turns to me.

"Is it true?" says one of the new kids from glazing, a nineteen-year-old named Trey who started in November. "Are they closing us?"

"No, they're not," I say.

"It says it right there in the fourth paragraph."

"It says *a person familiar*."

"So who's the person?"

"Somebody who wants you scared," says Lolo, from the back, by the coffee machine. She's not on shift and she came in anyway, in her bowling jacket with SPARE PARTS on the back, holding a Styrofoam cup in both hands and not looking at me. "Somebody in Minneapolis who wants Pip to fold. That's what that is."

"So it's not true?" Trey says.

Lolo doesn't answer him. She turns her eyes on me instead, and so does everybody else.

"My landlord read it," Irene says, with her arms crossed over her apron. "He texted me while I was still in my socks, asking if I'm renewing in May."

"My wife read it to me out loud in the car," says Darnell, "with the baby in the back."

"The kids are getting texts, boss." That's Benny, from the door, with the press brake still going *chunk* behind him because nobody's turned it off. "There's a cabinet shop in Holland already texting the glazing kids. Twenty-two an hour and a signing bonus."

"I got one," Trey says, and holds up his phone like evidence.

"Don't answer it," I say.

"Why not? Nobody upstairs is answering anything."

"I'm answering you right now."

"You're answering *no*," Trey says. "My mom says no like that when the answer's yes."

"Nobody's quit yet," Benny says from the door. "Yet's a long word, boss."

Darnell points the red marker at the whiteboard, at the article taped over his count. "Kurt gets a magazine, and we get a printout over the count. Who's going to say something, Pip? Out loud, where Minneapolis can hear it?"

"Your room got on the news," Irene says. "Get us on the news."

I could tell them. I could say: *There's a deal. I'm the no. I've got twenty days, maybe less, to find two point three million dollars, and if I don't, Northgate calls the loan and takes Adam's shares and sells them to Halvard, and then I'm not the no anymore.* I could say all of that, and it's all true.

I haven't told them any of it in a month. I told Benny *nobody's selling this plant*, and I told Darnell to erase *stole*, and I decided what they could carry.

"I'm handling it," I say.

"Handling it how?" says Trey.

"I'm handling it, Trey. Go back to work, all of you. Nobody's closing this plant."

Nobody moves for a second. Then Darnell caps the red marker and puts it in the tray, and leaves the question marks up.

"Then say it somewhere besides the break room, boss," he says, and goes.

They go back to work, slowly, in twos and threes, Trey last, with his phone still in his hand. Lolo stays by the coffee machine with her cup, and when the room's empty she drinks the rest of her coffee and throws the cup away and comes past me in the doorway.

"*I'm handling it*," she says. "That's what your dad used to say when your mother asked about the furnace."

"Did he handle it?"

"Every time, and every time she'd have rather known." She zips her jacket to the chin. "I'm going home to sleep. Text me if you need a training crew."

My phone buzzes all morning. Adam, first thing: *Saw it, not from us. Kurt's pressuring, calling Ruth now.* Then, mid-morning, my phone again: *Ruth says not her either, and she's furious, she thinks Kurt leaked it.* Then: *Want me to come out?* Simone, a little later: *The "two years" is not in any Halvard document I have seen. It is a lie or it is a new plan. Either way it was placed. I am finding out which.*

I don't answer any of them. I'm at the knee line with my hood down, running beads on a training coupon, because when I can't think I weld, and I can't think.

Around noon Irene comes over from glazing and stands at my elbow until I flip my hood up.

"The TV lady called the front office," she says. "They put her through to the floor phone, and I told her you'd call her back. Are you calling her back?"

"I'm welding, Irene."

"You're welding a coupon. Nobody needs that coupon." She picks it up with her gloves, looks at my bead, and sets it down again. "It's a nice bead. Call her back."

Carla Ruiz from Channel 6 calls after lunch, and again an hour later. Then a text lands on my phone: *We're doing the Halvard story on the early news. We'd love the founder's side. We're coming out either way. We'd rather have you than the gate.*

A little after four the live truck pulls into the lot and parks by the fence, right next to my room: a white van with a satellite dish on the roof folded down like a hand, a camera guy, a producer with a headset, and Carla Ruiz, who turns out to be about thirty, with very good hair that doesn't move in the wind and a long red coat that's exactly right for television and wrong for the yard. She gets out and looks at the room on its cribbing in the snow with the light on inside, and her face does what everybody's face does.

"Oh, it's smaller than on TV," she says.

"It's ten by twelve, same as on TV."

"It looks bigger coming out of a building." She shakes my hand, and hers is cold because she didn't keep it in her pocket, and I like her a little for that. "Ms. Sobczak, thank you. We go at five-oh-two, live, ninety seconds. I'll ask you about the report, and you can say anything you want."

"I haven't said I'll do it."

"I know you haven't." She looks past me, to the open rolling door of the plant, where first shift is standing, not working, thirty of them, maybe forty, in their coats and hoods and safety glasses: Benny with his arms folded on his belly, Irene with hers crossed, Darnell, Trey with his hood up. All of them are watching the yard.

"I think they'd like you to," Carla says gently.

"Is there anything you won't answer?" she asks, while I'm still looking at them.

"Anything about my husband."

"Then I won't ask about your husband," she says, and writes something on her hand with a pen.

While the camera guy runs cable through the snow, Darnell comes out of the rolling door and across the yard, in no hurry, with his hands in his coat pockets. He stops beside me and looks at the van instead of at me.

"You don't have to say anything fancy, boss," he says. "Just say we're not closing."

"I said it in the break room."

"You said it to us, and we already believed you." He jerks his chin at the camera, and at Minneapolis somewhere behind it. "Say it to them."

He walks back to the door and takes his place in it again, next to Irene.

Twenty minutes before air, I'm in the room with the door shut and my phone in my hand.

I should call him. Since the parking ramp he's been telling me everything before he does it, without anybody asking him to, badly, saying um, forgetting names, texting me Kurt's calls like a kid reporting to a teacher. He knocked on my door.

I should call him and say: *Channel 6 is in the yard. They're going live at five. I'm going to say something, and here's what I'm going to say.* And he'd say something back, and Simone would say something about numbers, and we'd decide.

And while we decided, forty people would stand in that rolling door in the cold watching a news van, thinking about *two years*.

His name is right there on the screen, ADAM, with the photo of him asleep at a rest stop with pretzels on his chest. Out in the yard the producer holds up ten fingers at my glass, and a while later five, and the screen goes dark and I wake it up again.

*I'm handling it.*

I put the phone in my pocket.

When the producer starts counting, I'm standing in the snow in front of my room in the white blast of the camera light, with the glass corner glowing behind me and Carla Ruiz beside me in her red coat with a microphone. The producer counts down with her fingers, three, two, and behind the camera, at the rolling door, forty people have gone completely quiet.

"I'm here at the Clyde Park plant of Twodesk, the Grand Rapids company whose video of a room being craned out of its own headquarters has been seen by more than eleven million people," Carla says to the lens. "This morning a trade publication reported that Minneapolis furniture giant Halvard is close to acquiring the company, and that this plant could close within two years. With me is Twodesk co-founder Philippa Sobczak, who designed the room you see behind me." She turns to me with the microphone. "Ms. Sobczak, is Twodesk being sold?"

The light is so bright I can't see the rolling door anymore, only the snow coming down through it, big and slow, each flake lit up like a spark from a grinder.

"No," I say, into the light.

"The report says—"

"The report's wrong." It comes out in my floor voice, the one that carries over a press brake. "We're not selling, not to Halvard, not to anybody. We've got seven thousand five hundred people who put money down on a room with no post in the corner, and we're going to build every one of them here, in this building, with these people."

I point behind me without looking, because I don't have to.

"So there's no deal?" Carla says.

"There's no deal," I say. "There's a plant. It's open. It's staying open. That's all I've got to say."

There's a second where nothing happens. Then, somewhere behind the camera, from the rolling door, somebody whoops, high and loud, like a kid at a ballgame, and then somebody else does, and then they're clapping, forty people in coats in the snow clapping, and Carla Ruiz is smiling at me with her very good hair not moving, and she turns back to the lens.

"A defiant answer from Philippa Sobczak," she says. "We've reached out to Halvard and to CEO Adam Kerrigan for comment. Back to you, Tom."
