## Chapter 20 — Pip

<!-- POV: Pip -->

The story goes up on the *Furnishings Weekly* website at 6:02 on Thursday morning, which is a strange hour to publish news about furniture unless you want it to be the first thing a lot of people in Grand Rapids read with their coffee.

HALVARD NEARS DEAL FOR VIRAL BACKYARD-OFFICE MAKER TWODESK, the headline says. Under it, a photo of Kurt Lindahl in a turtleneck at a trade show in Milan, and under that, the article. *Sources close to the transaction*. *Final approvals expected within weeks*. *Halvard is expected to consolidate Twodesk manufacturing into its existing North American footprint as part of a broader integration*. And, in the fourth paragraph, the one that matters: *A person familiar with Halvard's plans said the company's Clyde Park facility in Wyoming, Michigan, is likely to close within two years.*

Two years. Not thirty-six months. Not *in writing, I asked*.

I read it in my truck in the plant lot at six-forty. I read it twice. Then I put the phone face down on the dash and sit there with my hands on the wheel, watching first shift come in across the lot under the lights. Benny's Silverado. Irene's Malibu. Darnell's minivan with the new baby seat in the back that he showed everybody on Monday like it was a trophy.

I watch them come in, and I know, because I can see it in the way they're walking, that they've already read it.

By seven-thirty there's no work on the floor. The machines are on. The press brake is going *chunk* because Benny turned it on by habit. But the knee line's standing under the yellow curtains with their hoods up on their heads, talking. The glazing table's empty because Irene is in the break room. Darnell's in the break room. Half of first shift is in the break room, standing, in their coats, around the microwave cart where Tavi used to set up his laptop with the dots. Somebody's printed out the article and taped it to the whiteboard by the time clock, right over the count.

*ROOMS SOLD SINCE PIP LIBERATED ONE: 7,519.*

And under it, in Darnell's handwriting, in red: *DAYS UNTIL THEY CLOSE US: ???*

I stand in the break room door. Everybody looks at me.

"Is it true?" somebody says. One of the new kids from glazing, a nineteen-year-old named Marcus who started in November. "Are they closing us?"

"No," I say.

"It says two years."

"It says *a person familiar*."

"Who's the person?"

"Somebody who wants you scared," says Lolo, from the back, by the coffee machine. She's not on shift. She came in anyway. She's in her bowling jacket with SPARE PARTS on the back, holding a Styrofoam cup in both hands, and she's not looking at me. "Somebody in Minneapolis who wants Pip to fold. That's what that is."

"So it's not true?"

Lolo doesn't answer him. She looks at me.

Everybody looks at me.

I could tell them. I could say: *There's a deal. I'm the no. I've got twenty days, maybe less, to find two point three million dollars, and if I don't, Northgate calls the loan and takes Adam's shares and sells them to Halvard, and then I'm not the no anymore.* I could say all of that. It's all true.

I've been doing that for a month. Not telling them. Telling Benny *nobody's selling this plant*. Telling Darnell to erase *stole*. Deciding what they could carry.

"I'm handling it," I say.

"How?" says Marcus.

"I'm handling it, Marcus. Go back to work. All of you. Nobody's closing this plant."

They go back to work, slowly, in twos and threes. I watch them go. Lolo stays by the coffee machine with her cup and watches me watch them, and when the room's empty she drinks the rest of her coffee and throws the cup away and comes past me in the doorway.

"*I'm handling it*," she says. "That's what your dad used to say when your mother asked about the furnace."

"Did he handle it?"

"Every time. And every time she'd have rather known." She zips her jacket. "I'm going home to sleep. Text me if you need a training crew."

My phone buzzes all morning. Adam, at seven-fifty: *Saw it. Not from us. Kurt's pressuring. Calling Ruth.* Then at nine: *Ruth says not her either. She's furious. She thinks Kurt leaked it.* Then at ten-fifteen: *Want me to come out?* Simone, at ten-thirty: *The "two years" is not in any Halvard document I have seen. It is a lie or it is a new plan. Either way it was placed. I am finding out which.*

I don't answer any of them. I'm on the floor. I'm at the knee line with my hood on, running beads on a training coupon, because when I can't think I weld, and I can't think.

Dana Ruiz from Channel 6 calls at one. Then at two. At two-thirty she texts: *We're doing the Halvard story at 5. We'd love the founder's side. We're coming out either way. We'd rather have you than the gate.*

At four-fifteen the live truck pulls into the lot.

It parks by the fence, right next to my room. A white van with a satellite dish on the roof folded down like a hand. A camera guy, a producer with a headset, and Dana Ruiz, who turns out to be about thirty, with very good hair that doesn't move in the wind and a long red coat that's exactly right for television and wrong for the yard. She gets out and looks at the room on its cribbing in the snow with the light on inside, and her face does what everybody's face does.

"Oh," she says. "It's smaller than on TV."

"It's ten by twelve."

"It looks bigger coming out of a building." She shakes my hand. Her hand is cold. She didn't keep it in her pocket. I like her a little for that. "Ms. Sobczak. Thank you. We go at five-oh-two. Live. Ninety seconds. I'll ask you about the report. You can say anything you want."

"I haven't said I'll do it."

"I know." She looks past me. Behind me, in the open rolling door of the plant, first shift is standing. Not working. Standing. Thirty of them, maybe forty, in their coats and hoods and safety glasses, watching the live truck. Benny with his cigarette behind his ear. Irene with her arms crossed. Darnell. Marcus. All of them watching the yard.

"I think they'd like you to," Dana says gently.

At four-forty I'm in the room with the door shut. I've got my phone in my hand.

I should call him. I know I should. I told him in the parking ramp, *before, not after*. I told him *you don't decide anything about this company without me*. I stood in the snow on level three and said that to his face. And he's been doing it, for two weeks, badly, saying um, forgetting names, texting me Kurt's calls like a kid reporting to a teacher. He knocked on my door.

I should call him and say: *Channel 6 is in the yard. They're going live at five. I'm going to say something. Here's what I'm going to say.* And he'd say something back, and Simone would say something about numbers, and we'd decide.

And while we decided, forty people would stand in that rolling door in the cold watching a news van, thinking about *two years*.

I look at the phone. At his name. ADAM, and the photo of him asleep at a rest stop with pretzels on his chest.

*I'm handling it.*

I put the phone in my pocket.

At five-oh-one, I'm standing in the snow in front of my room in the white blast of the camera light, with the glass corner glowing behind me and Dana Ruiz beside me in her red coat with a microphone. The producer counts down with her fingers. Three, two. Behind the camera, at the rolling door, forty people have gone completely silent.

"I'm here at the Clyde Park plant of Twodesk, the Grand Rapids company whose video of a room being craned out of its own headquarters has been seen by more than eleven million people," Dana says to the lens. "This morning a trade publication reported that Minneapolis furniture giant Halvard is close to acquiring the company, and that this plant could close within two years. With me is Twodesk co-founder Philippa Sobczak, who designed the room you see behind me." She turns to me. "Ms. Sobczak. Is Twodesk being sold?"

The light is very bright. I can't see the rolling door anymore. I can only see the light, and the snow coming down through it, big and slow, each flake lit up like a spark from a grinder.

"No," I say.

"The report says—"

"The report's wrong." I hear my voice. It's the voice I use on the floor, the one that carries over a press brake. "We're not selling. Not to Halvard, not to anybody. We've got seven thousand five hundred people who put money down on a room with no post in the corner, and we're going to build every one of them. Here. In this building. With these people."

I point behind me. I don't look. I don't have to.

"So there's no deal?" Dana says.

"There's no deal," I say. "There's a plant. It's open. It's staying open. That's all I've got to say."

There's a second where nothing happens. Then, somewhere behind the camera, from the rolling door, somebody whoops, high and loud, like a kid at a ballgame, and then somebody else does, and then they're clapping, forty people in coats in the snow clapping, and Dana Ruiz is smiling at me with her very good hair not moving, and she turns back to the lens.

"A defiant answer tonight from one of the founders of Twodesk," she says. "We've reached out to Halvard and to Twodesk CEO Adam Kerrigan for comment. Live in Wyoming, I'm Dana Ruiz. Back to you, Tom."
