## Chapter 7 — Adam

<!-- POV: Adam -->

Simone has moved into the glass conference room, which is like moving into an aquarium.

It's the one in the middle of the fourth floor, the one we built in 2019 when a consultant told us transparency was a value. Four walls of glass, a long white table, twelve chairs, a screen. Everybody on the floor can see in. Simone has set up at the far end of the table with her laptop and her black notebook and her pens, which she retrieved from Pip's stack of boxes Tuesday morning and lined up again in a row, smallest to largest. The ficus is on the credenza behind her. The lighthouse is leaning against the wall, face out, because she hasn't found a hook.

"You could hang that," I say Wednesday morning, from the door.

"I could."

"Facilities has hooks."

"Facilities is busy," Simone says, without looking up. "Facilities spent yesterday answering questions from the Grand Rapids Ledger about whether our building is structurally sound."

"Is it?"

"It's been standing since 1922. It has survived a great deal." She turns a page. "Sit down. We have eleven things."

I sit down. We have eleven things. Simone always has a number of things, and the number is always exact, and she always writes it at the top of a fresh page and circles it. She has a way of making a day look like it has edges.

"One," she says. "Deposits."

"How many?"

"One thousand eight hundred and six. As of seven a.m. Tavi has a dashboard. It refreshes every ninety seconds. The marketing team has been watching it like it is a horse race."

"Is it going up?"

"It is going up." She looks at me over her reading glasses. "Every one of them is for the original Corner. Not one for the Lite. People are writing in the notes field. Would you like to hear some?"

"No."

"*The one from the video.* *The one with no post.* *The one that can leave.*" She says them flatly, like a list of part numbers. "There is one from a woman in Kalamazoo that says, *I want the room she took*."

I rub my eyes. "Two."

"Two is Halvard. Bryce emailed at five-fourteen. He is, his word, *energized*. He wants to accelerate. He would like the founders' approvals by the end of the month."

"He knows we don't have Pip."

"He believes we will." She makes a small mark. "He believes the video is a sign of founder passion that will translate into founder alignment. He used the phrase *passion asset*."

"God help us."

"Three is Ruth."

She doesn't have to say anything else about three, because my phone is lighting up on the table between us with RUTH BASKIN – LAKESHORE, and Simone slides it toward me with one finger like it's a check at a restaurant.

I pick up. I put it on speaker. Simone and I do that now. I'm not sure when we started.

"Adam." Ruth sounds like she's on a treadmill, which she probably is. Ruth runs four miles every morning at six in a gym in Ann Arbor and takes calls the whole time, breathing evenly, like a woman who has made peace with the idea of being chased. "We love the energy. We'd love it more in a different building."

"Ruth."

"I've watched it. Everybody's watched it. My daughter sent it to me. My *daughter*. She's at Oberlin. She doesn't know what I do." A breath. "It's a beautiful piece of film. It's also your co-founder removing a heritage asset from the premises in the middle of the night without notice, during a live transaction, with a city permit for HVAC units."

"It's her room, Ruth. It's on a lease. She owns it."

"I know. Diane read me the lease. Diane laughed. Diane is our general counsel and I have never heard her laugh." Treadmill, treadmill. "The board meets on the nineteenth. Pip is on the agenda. Item four. The motion is censure."

"Ruth, I don't think—"

"It's a word, Adam. It's a symbolic word. It goes in the minutes and it tells Halvard this board is functional. You and I both know the deal is the only item on that agenda that matters. I need her yes by the end of the month, and I need it without any more cranes."

"She's not going to say yes."

"Then you need to find out what she'll say yes to," Ruth says. "That's your job. You're the closer. Close."

She hangs up. Ruth always hangs up first. I think it's in the Lakeshore Ventures employee handbook.

Simone has written something down. I lean over to see. It says *4. Censure (symbolic)*, and next to it, very small, a question mark.

"Four," she says. "Gordon."

Gordon Pyle comes in at nine-fifteen with his laptop held against his chest the way you'd hold a baby in a burning building. Gordon is fifty-two, our CFO, a good man from Holland, Michigan, who wears a cardigan in all seasons and sweats in all of them too. He's the only person in the company who knows the actual number, and the number has been wearing him down since the summer like water on a step.

He sits. He opens the laptop. He looks at me, then at Simone, then at the door, which is glass, which everybody on the floor can see through.

"Can we pull the blinds?" he says.

"There are no blinds," says Simone. "It's a transparency room."

Gordon closes his eyes for a moment. Then he turns the laptop around.

It's the cash report. I know what it says. I've known what it says since August. But Gordon makes me look at it every Wednesday anyway, the way a dentist makes you look at the X-ray.

"Eleven weeks," he says. "At current burn. Nashville is costing us four hundred and twenty thousand a week. Clyde Park is cash positive on the Corner and negative on everything else. And the deposits"—he scrolls—"are in escrow."

"All of them?"

"All of them. Every dollar."

"Why?"

Gordon looks at me like I'm a man who asked why the sun comes up.

"Because of the door, Adam."

Rule two. *Never spend a customer's money before you've cut their steel.* Pip's rule. She wrote it on the inside of the door in 2014 with a carpenter's pencil, after a guy we bought lumber from in Holland went under owing forty families their deposits. It's in the bylaws now. It's in the terms of sale. Eighteen hundred people have sent us ten percent of a backyard room in the last two days and we can't touch a penny of it until somebody at Clyde Park cuts the first piece of steel for their unit.

Which is the right thing. It's the most Pip thing in the whole company. It's why people trust us. And in eleven weeks it's going to sit there in a bank in Cleveland and watch us run out of money.

"And Northgate," Gordon says. He says *Northgate* like the name of a disease in his family.

"What about Northgate?"

"They called. Somebody at Northgate watched the video. They'd like to know if the—" he reads off a sticky note—"*founder dispute*—is *material to the covenant*."

Simone doesn't look up. She knows about Northgate. She's the only one besides Gordon who does.

Here's what nobody else knows. In October, when we were three weeks from missing a payroll for the first time in the history of the company, I borrowed nine million dollars from Northgate Capital in Chicago. Personally. Me, Adam Kerrigan, with my own name on it. I put up my shares as collateral, all thirty-four percent of them, and I lent the money to the company, and the company made payroll, and two hundred and ninety people got paid on a Friday and never knew why the Friday before it I didn't sleep.

The loan has a covenant. If the company's cash falls below six million on the test date in March, Northgate can call the loan. If I can't pay it, Northgate takes my shares.

I didn't tell Pip.

That's a pitch, the way I just said that. I didn't tell Pip like it's a fact about the weather. Strike it. Here's the true version. I sat at the kitchen counter at two in the morning in October with the Northgate papers in front of me and the pen in my hand, and Pip was asleep upstairs, across the hall, and I thought: if I go up those stairs and wake her up and tell her, then she'll know. She'll know the thing I've been carrying since August, that the company I sold to everybody as the future of work is three weeks from not making payroll, and that I opened Nashville, and Nashville is eating us. And then it'll be real. And she'll look at me the way she looked at me the morning after Nashville, and then not look at me at all.

So I signed it at the counter. And then I picked up my phone and called the one person I knew would be awake, who had the numbers already, who wouldn't look at me any way at all.

"Tell Northgate the founders are aligned," I say to Gordon.

"Are they?"

"They will be."

Gordon writes it down. He doesn't believe it either. He closes his laptop and holds it against his chest again and goes out through the glass door, and everybody on the floor watches him go, and nobody pretends they aren't.

"Five," Simone says.

"Can we skip to eleven?"

"Eleven is lunch." She puts her pen down. She almost never puts her pen down. "Adam. How do you get her to yes?"

"I don't know."

I hear it come out of my mouth. *I don't know.* I don't say that. I don't say it in meetings, I don't say it to investors, I don't say it to my mother on the phone in Sarasota when she asks if I'm happy. Simone hears it too. Her eyebrows go up a sixteenth of an inch, which on Simone is a fireworks display.

"You could ask her," she says.

"I asked her."

"You told her. Through a door, with a slide." She takes her glasses off. "I was in the room Monday. I heard you. You did not ask her anything. You told her she barely uses it."

"She doesn't."

"Neither do you."

There's no expression on her face when she says it. It's just another item, five or six, a part number. She picks her pen back up.

"You say yes to me before I finish the sentence," she says, writing. "You have done it since May. I ask for a budget line and you say yes. I ask for headcount and you say yes. I asked for the corner office as a joke, Adam, I asked because it was empty and I was tired of taking calls in the stairwell, and you said yes before I got to the word *office*." She underlines something. "I did not think about why until Monday. I think you say yes to me so fast because nothing I ask for costs you anything."

I don't say anything. I look past her through the glass wall at the northwest corner of the floor, where there's nothing now but brick and two big windows over the river and four steel bolt plates in the maple floor, bright and clean, where somebody's set a little orange safety cone so nobody trips.

Somebody put a cone there. I didn't ask for a cone.

"Six," says Simone.

I keep looking at the cone. I'm thinking about the room. Not the video, the room. The way the light came in at seven in the morning in winter, low over the river, and lay across her desk in a long gold bar, and she'd have her feet up on the radiator in her wool socks and a cup of moka-pot coffee that tasted like a tire fire, and I'd come off the elevator at five to seven with my espresso and say *Okay, boss, what are we doing today?* and she'd tell me.

When was the last time I went up there at seven?

I know the answer. I know it to the morning. I'm just not going to think about it in a glass room in front of everybody.

"Six," Simone says again, gently.

"What's six?" I say.
