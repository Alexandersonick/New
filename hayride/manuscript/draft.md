# Two Tickets to the Haunted Hayride


## Chapter 2

<!-- POV: Gus -->

His mother had labeled the toaster.

Gus found it on Sunday morning on the kitchen counter, unplugged and wrapped in a dish towel, with a strip of masking tape across the top that said TOASTER (JOAN'S) in red marker. Beside it sat a box labeled KITCHEN MISC / GR, and beside that a box labeled KITCHEN MISC / AUGUST, which held one frying pan, a can opener, and the ceramic owl cookie jar he'd made in seventh-grade art and that his mother had kept on top of the refrigerator for twenty years, out of either love or penance.

"You're giving Aunt Joan the toaster," he said.

"Joan's toaster caught fire in August." Ruth VanderWal came in from the dining room with a roll of packing tape on her wrist like a bracelet. "You have a toaster."

"I have a toaster in Chicago. Had. It's in a storage unit in Cicero with my couch and my good drill."

"Then you have a toaster in Cicero." She tore a strip of tape with her teeth, which she had told him all his life never to do. "Eat your eggs."

He ate his eggs. He ate them standing at the counter because the kitchen chairs were already labeled for Grand Rapids, and his mother studied him over the top of a box of cookbooks with the expression she'd had since August, when he'd pulled into the driveway with everything he owned that fit in a 2011 Ford Ranger and said, *I need to stay a while,* and she had said, *How long is a while,* and he had said, *Can I buy the house?*

She had laughed at him. Then she had sat down on the porch steps and cried, and then she had named a price that was too low, and he had argued her up eight thousand dollars, and the bank in Paw Paw had argued him back down six.

"You don't have to do it," Ruth said now.

"Do what?"

"Don't." She pointed the tape gun at him. "Don't do the face. The Supper. The wagon. You don't have to ride beside her for ten nights so the whole town can watch. You could call Dot and say your back went out."

"My back's fine."

"Backs go out. It's a thing backs do. Your father's went out every time my sister visited."

"Mom."

"I'm saying it's available to you."

He put his plate in the sink. The sink was not labeled, because the sink was staying, because he was staying. Out the window over it, past the clothesline and the dead tomato cages, stood the pole barn at the back of the lot: forty by sixty, steel sides, a concrete floor his father had poured himself in 1998 and gotten slightly wrong, so that a marble set down at the north door would roll all the way to the south one and stop against the drain. Gus had tested this the day he closed on the house. Then he had stood in the empty barn for a long time and said, out loud, to the rafters, "VanderWal Scenic," just to hear what it sounded like with an echo.

It had sounded ridiculous. It had also sounded like his.

"Dot asked me," he said. "In August. Before the Supper. She said she was short a guide, and I said yes before I thought about what that meant."

"That's how you've always said yes to things," his mother said, not unkindly.

"I know."

"That's how you said yes to Chicago."

He didn't answer that. His mother didn't need him to. She went back to the cookbooks and wrote GR on another box, and after a while she said, without looking up, "She pinned you, didn't she? At the Supper. Lorna's mother told me she pinned your ticket in Walt's barn coat."

"Everyone pins."

"I know everyone pins, August. I was a guide for eleven years. I pinned your father." She smoothed the tape flat with her thumb. "I'm only saying I'd like to know which side of the coat."

* * *

On Tuesday afternoon, Dot Teague drove her Buick across the field between their two farms instead of going around by the road, because she was eighty-one and had decided at some point that roads were a suggestion.

Gus was in the barn, ripping plywood on a borrowed table saw, when the Buick came through the open south door at about four miles an hour and stopped a respectful six feet from his lumber rack. He shut off the saw. The silence came down with sawdust in it.

"Mrs. Teague."

"Augie." She rolled down the window rather than get out. "You've got a floor that slopes."

"Dad poured it."

"I know he did. I watched him. He was singing the whole time, which I told him was a mistake." She held a three-ring binder out the window. "Here. I'm the guide coordinator, which is a fancy word for the person who has to hand out binders. Rosalind would do it, but Rosalind would sooner eat that binder than drive it over here, and I don't blame her."

He took it. It was the Guide Handbook, laminated pages in sheet protectors, a pumpkin clip-art sticker on the cover that had to be Bev's. There was a Post-it stuck on the first page in Dot's cursive: *Read 1, 7, and the back.*

"What's in the back?"

"Your part." Dot looked past him at the barn. "What are you building in here?"

"Flats. For a college opera in March. Lakeshore College. They're doing something by Mozart where everybody's in disguise."

"Oh, I love a disguise." She seemed delighted. "Is that your job now? Building fake walls?"

"That's my job if I can find one more school that needs fake walls before January. Otherwise my job is fixing your gutters."

"My gutters are fine."

"Your gutters have a sapling in them, Mrs. Teague. I can see it from my mom's kitchen."

Dot laughed. It was the same laugh she'd had when he was fifteen and had pulled her Woodlot ghost so early that it came down the line before the wagon even stopped, and the entire 8:20 ride had watched a bedsheet arrive in the trees, wait politely, and then get yanked back up into the dark like a man who'd gotten off at the wrong floor.

"You're going to read the back," Dot said, "and you're going to want to call me. Don't call me. I'll tell you what I tell everybody about that story. It's her story. She wrote it, and the committee approved it, and people come from Kalamazoo for it. If you want to ride, you ride with the story."

"I know the story."

"You've heard the story. You haven't sat on the bench." Dot rolled the window halfway up and then down again. "And Augie. The switch is on your side."

"What switch?"

But she had already put the Buick in reverse, and she backed out of his barn as calmly as she'd come in, and the last thing he saw was the dog collar on her wrist as she lifted a hand off the wheel.

He opened the binder on the table saw.

Rule 1 said guides stayed belted from barn to barn. Rule 7 he'd heard read out loud by Danny Pietrowski in front of ninety people, which was a way of learning a rule he did not recommend. He flipped to the back, where there was a single laminated page titled FINALE: TEAGUE'S POND: APPROVED SCRIPT, with a date at the bottom (committee vote, Sept. 2020) and the initials R.P.

He read it standing up, and then he sat down on an upturned bucket and read it again.

*There was a girl on Hollins Road who sewed her own veil, and a boy on Hollins Road who bought a ticket for the 6:10 to Chicago. Two weeks before the wedding he took it.*

He had not taken the 6:10. There was no 6:10. He had driven the Ranger out of Carrow on a Saturday morning with a mattress tied to the roof, and he had stopped at the Marathon station on M-40 for coffee and sat in the parking lot for forty minutes with the engine running, and then he had kept going, which was worse than any train.

*She walked down to this pond with a lantern to wait for the train to bring him back, and she's still waiting.*

She had not sewn her veil either. He remembered the veil. It was from a shop in Kalamazoo and it had a price tag on a plastic loop, and she had held it up in her kitchen and said, *Is this ridiculous, be honest,* and he had said *Yes,* and she had laughed so hard she sat down on the floor.

At the bottom of the page, set in brackets in a different font, there was a stage direction.

*(Front-bench guide: WHISTLE on "that's the 6:10.")*

He sat on the bucket in his father's barn, which sloped, and looked at that line for a long time.

"Well," he told the table saw. "That's my cue."

* * *

The walk-through was Thursday at six, and the pond lane was a swamp.

Gus wore good boots. He parked Delores (the Ranger, so named by a roommate in 2020 for reasons that were never explained) by the Teague barn behind a line of volunteer pickups, and walked down to the wagon yard in the long gold light, past the pumpkin field and the apple bins and the hand-painted sign that said HAUNTED HAYRIDE · FRI & SAT IN OCTOBER · NO CHAINSAWS EVER. Somebody had added, in smaller letters, *we mean it, Ed.*

The Big Wagon sat hitched in the yard. It was a 1971 flatbed hay wagon with plywood sides and two rows of bales with bench backs bolted in, painted barn red so long ago it had faded to the color of a ham. At the front, facing forward, was a padded bench for two with a pair of black nylon seat belts that looked like they'd been salvaged from a school bus, and between the two seats, screwed to the rail, was a gray electrical box the size of a lunch pail with two brass toggle switches on top.

One was labeled FOG. The other was labeled WHISTLE.

In front of the wagon, idling with the sound of a small and contented landslide, was a red 1966 Farmall 806, and on the Farmall sat Hank Ruiter, seventy years old, in a seed cap, with his hands folded on the wheel.

"Evening, Hank," Gus said. "Evening, Marguerite."

Hank looked at him for a while. Then Hank looked at the tractor, as if checking whether Marguerite intended to respond.

"Yup," said Hank.

That was the most Hank had said to him since 2019, and Gus decided to take it as a welcome.

"Bro!"

Danny Pietrowski came around the back of the wagon with a clipboard and a headlamp already on his forehead, though the sun wasn't down. He was taller than Gus remembered by about a foot and a half, which made sense, since the last time Gus had seen him Danny had been twelve and crying in the Teague barn loft and refusing to come down.

"Hey, Danny."

"You're actually here." Danny looked him up and down. "You came to the walk-through. In boots."

"I was told to wear boots."

"Yeah, by her. Nobody does what she says. That's like the whole thing about her." He tapped the clipboard against his leg. "I'm parking. And I drive Wagon Two now. The little tractor. You want to see it? It's a John Deere. It's green. It's fine."

"Maybe after."

"Yeah. Okay." Danny hesitated. Then, lower, "She's at the pond. She's checking the line. She said to tell you she'll do the cue box when she gets back and that you should, quote, sit down and not touch anything."

"Sit down and don't touch anything."

"Those were her words. I'm a messenger." Danny started backing away. "I'm just saying. Bro. It's good you came."

Gus climbed up onto the front bench and sat down and did not touch anything.

He sat there while the sun went down over the corn, and the volunteers drifted past with extension cords and plastic skeletons and a bale of straw for the scarecrow, and every single one of them glanced at him on the bench and then away fast, the way you look at somebody in a hospital waiting room. He sat there while Lorna Bakker walked by carrying a coffee urn and said, "Hey, Gus, welcome to the fishbowl," and kept walking. The light went copper and then gray and the first cold of the evening came up off the field, and it got under his jacket, where her name was pinned over his heart in Dot Teague's cursive.

Then she came up the pond lane out of the dark.

She was in the barn coat, the sleeves rolled, a coil of fishing line over one shoulder and a pair of needle-nose pliers in her back pocket. She walked the way she'd always walked when she was carrying something, like the thing might try to get away. She didn't look at him. She came around the front of the wagon, climbed up onto the bench with one step, and sat down on the left side, a full foot of padded vinyl between them.

"Belt," she said.

He found the belt and fastened it. She fastened hers.

"FOG is mine," she said, to the cue box, not to him. "You don't touch FOG. FOG runs a chiller and a pump, and if you flip it at the wrong time the Woodlot ghost comes down in a cloud nobody can see, and I've built that ghost four times since 2009."

"Okay."

"WHISTLE is yours. Front-right guide. It's wired to a speaker in the willows on the far bank. You flip it once at the pond on the line in the script, and you flip it back, and you don't flip it any other time."

"On 'that's the 6:10,'" he said.

There was a pause about one second long. She was looking straight ahead at Hank's back, at the dark shape of the corn beyond the yard, and her jaw did something small.

"You read the back," she said.

"Dot told me to."

"Dot tells everybody to read the back." She put her hand on the FOG toggle, not flipping it, just resting her fingertips there, the burn scar on the back of her hand catching the yard light. "It's an approved script. It's been approved since 2020. The committee votes on changes, not the guides."

"I know."

"So you know your cue."

"I know my cue, Rosalind."

She nodded once, still not looking at him, and climbed down off the bench, and walked back toward the barn with the fishing line over her shoulder, calling for Danny about an extension cord.

Gus stayed where he was, belted in, in the dark.

After a while he put his thumb on the brass WHISTLE toggle. It was cold and worn shiny at the tip from six Octobers of somebody else's thumb.

He rested it there and didn't push.
