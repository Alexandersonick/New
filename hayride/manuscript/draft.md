# Two Tickets to the Haunted Hayride

## Chapter 1

<!-- POV: Roz -->

Roz Pietrowski had written the rule herself, which was the worst part.

She had written it on the back of a Bakker's Diner placemat last October, in the middle of the Seat War, while two grown women argued over a wagon bench as if it were the last lifeboat off the *Titanic*. She had laminated it on the teachers' lounge laminator. Now it lay face up on the folding table beside the jar, and the jar was the same blue Ball canning jar the Carrow Haunted Hayride had used since 1987, quart-sized, gone faintly green with age, with a strip of masking tape on the side that said GUIDES in Dot Teague's handwriting.

Eleven folded ticket halves sat inside it, one for every volunteer who had signed up to guide. One of them had his name on it.

Seven years ago, two weeks before their wedding, August VanderWal had driven out of Carrow for a job in Chicago. The next summer she had written him into a ghost story, and the whole county had paid fifteen dollars a head to hear it ever since.

"Nobody touch the jar," Roz said into the microphone.

The Hoekstra boys, who had drifted up to the edge of the little stage with frosting on their fingers, froze. The microphone squealed. Ninety people at the long tables of the Grange Hall winced together.

"Turn it down, Rosie!" somebody's grandpa called.

"I'm turning it down, Mr. Kuiper."

"You're turning it *up*."

She turned it the other way. The squeal died. The room laughed, and she let it, because a laugh this early was a good sign. You wanted them loose before the scary part.

Lorna Bakker leaned in at the edge of the stage with a coffee mug in each hand and set one down by Roz's elbow like a cornerman with a water bottle.

"Okay, so," Lorna said, low and fast, "that's a lot of town looking at you."

"They're looking at the jar."

"They're looking at the jar and then at the back of the hall and then at you, in a triangle, it's very geometric, I've been watching it for twenty minutes." Lorna sipped from her own mug. "Do you want me to faint? I can faint. I can faint into the apple crisp and we can do this in November."

"Nobody's fainting."

"He's sitting with his mom. He's eaten one ham bun in two hours. One. That's a man in distress, Roz."

"That's a man with a mother who made the ham buns."

"Ruth did not make those ham buns, Ruth buys those at Meijer and everyone knows it." Lorna lowered her voice even further. "You don't have to look at him."

"I'm not looking at him."

"I know. That's what I'm saying. You're not looking at him so hard it's making noise."

Roz picked up the coffee and drank some, mostly so her hands would have a job. At six-fifteen the door by the coat hooks had opened and ninety heads had very carefully not turned, and the whispers had started under the ham buns, low and fast as a fuse. *He's back for good, Ruth says. Bought her house off her, barn and all. Does she know he's here? She wrote him into the ghost story, didn't she? She knows. Look at her.* She knew where he was the way you know where a stove is.

"Wagon Two first," she said into the microphone, and Lorna retreated to the diner table with both mugs.

She shook the jar once, reached in without looking, and handed the half-ticket to Danny, who stood beside her holding the laminated Guide Handbook against his chest like a hymnal. Danny was nineteen and working for leftover apple crisp, and he read every name like a heavyweight bout.

"Lorna," he boomed. "BAKKER."

Lorna whooped and lifted both mugs. The second ticket came out Clyde Mulder. Clyde, at table six, raised one hand an inch off the tablecloth, which for Clyde was a parade.

"Lorna and Clyde," Roz said. "Wagon Two, front bench. You'll hate each other by the ninth."

"I hate him already!" Lorna called.

"Mutual," said Clyde.

The laugh came right on time, and Roz's shoulders came down half an inch. She had been on the hayride since she was thirteen, building things that sat up out of the dark, and the trick of a room was the trick of a scare: find where they're looking, then put the thing somewhere else.

She shook the jar again. "Wagon One."

"Chair's in there too?" Ed Brinks called from the Brinks table. Ed had played the farmer at the Corn Stop for thirty years and believed this gave him heckling rights.

"Chair's in there too, Ed. Random. Forever. I'll hold myself to it."

"She said that last year," someone told someone.

"She laminated it," someone else said.

Roz reached in. She picked a ticket from the bottom, because the bottom felt fairer, which was not how probability worked, and she taught probability to thirteen-year-olds every spring. She unfolded it.

It was her own name, Rosalind Pietrowski, in Dot's tidy cursive.

"ROZ," Danny announced. "PIETROWSKI."

"Rigged!" yelled Ed.

"It's a *jar*, Ed."

Another laugh, and she rode it straight back into the glass, because she had built the whole evening around this next part without admitting she had built it. Ten names left. One of them was his. She had stood under a lot of clear skies and stayed dry.

She drew, and unfolded it with her thumb.

*August VanderWal.*

She looked at it for one second and it felt like a week.

Then she did what she had always done when something came at her out of the dark. She kept her face exactly where it was.

"Danny," she said, holding it out. "Read the next one, please, I've got jar on my hands."

Danny leaned over, and his whole body stopped. He looked at the ticket, and then at her, and then at the back of the hall, and then he said, much too loudly and in his own voice, "You drew him? You drew *HIM*?"

The Grange Hall went quiet.

She knew that quiet; she had built it on purpose at Teague's Pond every October for six years. A fork touched a plate. The radiator ticked twice.

"August VanderWal," Roz read into the microphone, in her taking-attendance voice. "Wagon One, front bench."

At the side of the stage, a folding chair scraped.

Bev Oosterhouse, vice-chair, ten years chair before Roz, came up the two steps to the stage, set her casserole carrier on the corner of the table, and put her palm flat on the lid of the jar. She had a pumpkin brooch on her quilted vest and a clipboard under her arm, and Roz had never in her life seen Bev without at least one of the clipboard or the casserole carrier.

"Now," Bev said. "Rosalind. Let me fix this."

"There's nothing to fix, Bev."

"Honey." Bev lowered her voice, which accomplished nothing, because the microphone was eight inches from her pumpkin brooch. "It's you and him on a bench for ten nights in the dark in front of the whole county. Put it back. Draw again. Nobody will say a word."

Ninety people sat very still to show Bev exactly how many words they would say.

"Rule seven," Roz said.

Danny looked down at the handbook against his chest as if it had spoken. Then, because he was nineteen and her brother and therefore built to make things worse, he flipped it open and read.

"'Rule seven. Guide pairs are drawn at the Harvest Supper. No swaps. A guide who leaves her draw leaves the season.'" He turned the page. "Oh. There's another one."

"I know there's another one, Danny."

"'Rule twelve. A chair who breaks a rule steps down.'" He shut the handbook. "Roz. You wrote this."

"I wrote this."

"You wrote this *about yourself*."

"I wrote it about everybody. I'm part of everybody." She set the ticket on the table, his name face up, between her hand and Bev's. "Bev. If I put it back, the Seat War was for nothing. Next year everyone puts one back. Arlene puts one back."

At table four, Arlene Doornbos, who had been half of the Seat War, sat up straighter and folded her arms.

"Arlene would never," said Arlene.

"Arlene would absolutely," said three people at once.

Bev did not laugh. Bev looked at Roz for a long moment with an expression Roz had last seen on her in the Pietrowski kitchen in October of 2019, the week he left, over a pound cake.

"I'm trying to help you," Bev said quietly.

"I know you are." And she did know it, which made it harder. "It's handled."

"It is not handled. It's a hayride."

"It's handled *and* it's a hayride."

Bev took her hand off the jar. She picked up her casserole carrier. She went back down the two steps to her folding chair and sat and set the carrier on her knees and folded both hands over the lid of it like a lid on a lid.

"Well," Bev announced to the room, "I hope it's a warm October."

That was the most quotable thing anyone said all night, and everyone in Carrow knew it, and Roz could see Lorna across the hall mouthing it to Clyde so she would have it right for the diner in the morning.

Roz drew the rest: the alternates, in pairs. She did them on time and in order and she made the joke she always made about the no-chainsaws rule, and it landed, though not as well as usual. She thanked the Teagues for the land. She announced the walk-through, Thursday at six. The room clapped and the noise came back up, like a radio when you finally find the station.

Then the last part of the ritual arrived, and she had forgotten about it until it was standing in front of her.

"Okay!" Danny shouted over the noise. "Guides! Come get your person! Pin 'em inside your coat, left side, Dot's rules!"

"Arlo's rules," Dot called from her table. "Arlo was the sentimental one. I just did the handwriting."

The guides were already lining up for their halves. Clyde had brought his cigar box, the way he did every year, and he held it open for the Hoekstra boys to see the tickets inside, thirty-one seasons of partners in faded pencil and ballpoint.

"Every one of these people," Clyde told them, "I sat beside for ten nights in the dark."

"Did any of them scream?" asked the younger Hoekstra.

"All of them," said Clyde. "Every year. Lorna screams at the scarecrow and she built half the scarecrow."

"I *painted* the scarecrow," Lorna said, arriving at the table. "Painting is not building. Give me my man."

Lorna took Clyde's half and kissed it, loudly. Clyde took Lorna's and put it in his shirt pocket without looking at it.

"Romance," Lorna said.

"Paperwork," said Clyde.

Danny handed out everyone's halves but hers. Roz kept her own in her fingers, her name in Dot's handwriting, waiting to give it away.

He came up last. She could have told you he would. A man who sat at the back for two hours and ate one ham bun was a man who would stand at the end of the line. She could have built a scare around it.

She looked at him, because there was nowhere else to look.

He was taller than she remembered, or he stood straighter, or her memory had been leaning on him. His hair was the color of wet sand and needed cutting. There was a carpenter's pencil behind his right ear, at a church supper, as if a measurement might break out at any moment. He wore a brown work jacket with sawdust in the seams, and his left pinky was crooked in a way it had not been seven years ago.

August VanderWal. Gus, to everybody in Carrow since kindergarten, except Bev and the Diocese and, as of tonight, her. He looks like somebody let him out of a coat, she thought. Like he'd hung on a hook a long time and wasn't sure yet how to stand without it.

"Rosalind," he said.

Nobody called her Rosalind but Bev and the Diocese of Kalamazoo. He said it like a word he had practiced in a truck.

"August."

Danny looked from one to the other like a man watching a tennis match he'd bet his car on.

Roz held out her ticket. He didn't take it, not right away, and she knew with a terrible, professional clarity what he was doing: giving her one more second to pull it back, standing very still so that if she took her hand away, nobody would see.

Her thumb pressed her own name flat against the tablecloth. She hated that he knew how.

"Take it," she said. "I've got a jar full of people waiting to see if you do."

"I could say I've got a conflict," he said, very low. "Fridays, Saturdays, all of October. I could say it's a work thing and nobody would have to watch you do this."

"Do you have a conflict?"

"I have a barn full of lumber and a mother who's moving to Grand Rapids. No."

"Then take it, August, before Bev comes back up here with a lasagna."

He took it by the very edge, the way you'd take a slide out of a microscope, and his fingers didn't touch hers. Then he unzipped the work jacket halfway, reached inside to the left, and pinned her name to the lining with a safety pin he had brought in his pocket.

He'd brought a pin.

"Thursday," she said. "Six o'clock, walk-through. Wear boots."

"I own boots, Rosalind."

"Good boots. The pond lane's a swamp after the first of the month and Hank won't wait for you to find a dry spot."

"I remember the pond lane."

He said it plainly, no weight on it. She took his ticket from Danny, August VanderWal in Dot's cursive, and pinned it inside her father's barn coat, the brown canvas one with the sleeves rolled twice, without looking at what her hands were doing. Her hands had been pinning things in the dark since she was a teenager. They knew the way.

"Thursday," Gus said, and touched the zipper of his jacket, over the pin, as if checking that it held.

He turned to go. He made two steps toward the coat hooks before the table nearest the stage, where the Teagues and the Ruiters and three generations of Brinkses sat, made a sound like a drawer being pulled open.

It was Dot Teague. Dot was eighty-one and had not stood up at a Harvest Supper since her hip, and she didn't stand now. She turned in her chair, a small woman in a red cardigan with an old dog collar buckled around her wrist, and looked up at him with the same pale, delighted blue eyes she'd had in 2008, when she hired two teenagers to scare strangers in her woods.

The hall, which had come back to life, went still again to listen.

"Augie VanderWal," Dot said. "You still pull early?"

## Chapter 2

<!-- POV: Gus -->

His mother had labeled the toaster.

Gus found it on Sunday morning on the kitchen counter, unplugged and wrapped in a dish towel, with a strip of masking tape across the top that said TOASTER (JOAN'S) in red marker. Beside it sat a box labeled KITCHEN MISC / GR, and beside that a box labeled KITCHEN MISC / AUGUST, which held one frying pan, a can opener, and the ceramic owl cookie jar he'd made in seventh-grade art and that his mother had kept on top of the refrigerator for twenty years, out of either love or penance.

"You're giving Aunt Joan the toaster," he said.

"Joan's toaster caught fire over Labor Day." Ruth VanderWal came in from the dining room with a roll of packing tape hooked over one wrist. "You have a toaster."

"I have a toaster in Chicago. Had. It's in a storage unit in Cicero with my couch and my good drill."

"Then you have a toaster in Cicero." She tore a strip of tape with her teeth, which she had told him all his life never to do. "Eat your eggs."

He ate his eggs. He ate them standing at the counter because the kitchen chairs were already labeled for Grand Rapids, and his mother studied him over the top of a box of cookbooks with the expression she'd had since the end of summer, when he'd pulled into the driveway with everything he owned that fit in a 2011 Ford Ranger and said, *I need to stay a while,* and she had said, *How long is a while,* and he had said, *Can I buy the house?*

She had laughed at him. Then she had sat down on the porch steps and cried, and then she had named a price that was too low, and he had argued her up eight thousand dollars, and the bank in Paw Paw had argued him back down six.

"You don't have to do it," Ruth said now.

"Do what?"

"Don't." She pointed the tape gun at him. "Don't do the face. The Supper. The wagon. You don't have to ride beside her for ten nights so the whole town can watch. You could call Dot and say your back went out."

"My back's fine."

"Backs go out. It's a thing backs do. Your father's went out every time my sister visited."

"Mom."

"I'm saying it's available to you."

He put his plate in the sink. The sink was not labeled, because the sink was staying, because he was staying. Out the window over it, past the clothesline and the dead tomato cages, stood the pole barn at the back of the lot: forty by sixty, steel sides, a concrete floor his father had poured himself in 1998 and gotten slightly wrong, so that a marble set down at the north door would roll all the way to the south one and stop against the drain. Gus had tested this the day he closed on the house. Then he had stood in the empty barn for a long time and said, out loud, to the rafters, "VanderWal Scenic," just to hear what it sounded like with an echo.

It had sounded ridiculous. It had also sounded like his.

"Dot asked me," he said. "Back in the summer. Before the Supper. She said she was short a guide, and I said yes before I thought about what that meant."

"That's how you've always said yes to things," his mother said, not unkindly.

"I know."

"That's how you said yes to Chicago."

He didn't answer that. His mother didn't need him to. She went back to the cookbooks and wrote GR on another box, and after a while she said, without looking up, "She pinned you, didn't she? At the Supper. Lorna's mother told me she pinned your ticket in Walt's barn coat."

"Everyone pins."

"I know everyone pins, August. I was a guide for eleven years. I pinned your father." She smoothed the tape flat with her thumb. "I'm only saying I'd like to know which side of the coat."

* * *

On Tuesday afternoon, Dot Teague drove her Buick across the field between their two farms instead of going around by the road, because she was eighty-one and had decided at some point that roads were a suggestion.

Gus was in the barn, ripping plywood on a borrowed table saw, when the Buick came through the open south door at about four miles an hour and stopped a respectful six feet from his lumber rack. He shut off the saw. The silence came down with sawdust in it.

"Mrs. Teague."

"Augie." She rolled down the window rather than get out. "You've got a floor that slopes."

"Dad poured it."

"I know he did. I watched him. He was singing the whole time, which I told him was a mistake." She held a three-ring binder out the window. "Here. I'm the guide coordinator, which is a fancy word for the person who has to hand out binders. Rosalind would do it, but Rosalind would sooner eat that binder than drive it over here, and I don't blame her."

He took it. It was the Guide Handbook, laminated pages in sheet protectors, a pumpkin clip-art sticker on the cover that had to be Bev's. There was a Post-it stuck on the first page in Dot's cursive: *Read 1, 7, and the back.*

"What's in the back?"

"Your part." Dot looked past him at the barn. "What are you building in here?"

"Flats. For a college opera in March. Lakeshore College. They're doing something by Mozart where everybody's in disguise."

"Oh, I love a disguise." She seemed delighted. "Is that your job now? Building fake walls?"

"That's my job if I can find one more school that needs fake walls before January. Otherwise my job is fixing your gutters."

"My gutters are fine."

"Your gutters have a sapling in them, Mrs. Teague. I can see it from my mom's kitchen."

Dot laughed. It was the same laugh she'd had when he was fifteen and had pulled her Woodlot ghost so early that it came down the line before the wagon even stopped, and the entire 8:20 ride had watched a bedsheet arrive in the trees, wait politely, and then get yanked back up into the dark like a man who'd gotten off at the wrong floor.

"You're going to read the back," Dot said, "and you're going to want to call me. Don't call me. I'll tell you what I tell everybody about that story. It's her story. She wrote it, and the committee approved it, and people come from Kalamazoo for it. If you want to ride, you ride with the story."

"I know the story."

"You've heard the story. You haven't sat on the bench." Dot rolled the window halfway up and then down again. "And Augie. The switch is on your side."

"What switch?"

But she had already put the Buick in reverse, and she backed out of his barn as calmly as she'd come in, and the last thing he saw was the dog collar on her wrist as she lifted a hand off the wheel.

He opened the binder on the table saw.

Rule 1 said guides stayed belted from barn to barn. Rule 7 he'd heard read out loud by Danny Pietrowski in front of ninety people, which was a way of learning a rule he did not recommend. He flipped to the back, where there was a single laminated page titled FINALE: TEAGUE'S POND: APPROVED SCRIPT, with a date at the bottom (committee vote, Sept. 2020) and the initials R.P.

He read it standing up, and then he sat down on an upturned bucket and read it again.

*There was a girl on Hollins Road who sewed her own veil, and a boy on Hollins Road who bought a ticket for the 6:10 to Chicago. Two weeks before the wedding he took it.*

He had not taken the 6:10. There was no 6:10. He had driven the Ranger out of Carrow on a Saturday morning with a mattress tied to the roof, and he had stopped at the Marathon station on M-40 for coffee and sat in the parking lot for forty minutes with the engine running, and then he had kept going, which was worse than any train.

*She walked down to this pond with a lantern to wait for the train to bring him back, and she's still waiting.*

She had not sewn her veil either. He remembered the veil. It was from a shop in Kalamazoo and it had a price tag on a plastic loop, and she had held it up in her kitchen and said, *Is this ridiculous, be honest,* and he had said *Yes,* and she had laughed so hard she sat down on the floor.

At the bottom of the page, set in brackets in a different font, there was a stage direction.

*(Front-bench guide: WHISTLE on "that's the 6:10.")*

He sat on the bucket in his father's barn, which sloped, and looked at that line for a long time.

"Well," he told the table saw. "That's my cue."

* * *

The walk-through was Thursday at six, and the pond lane was a swamp.

Gus wore good boots. He parked Delores (the Ranger, so named by a roommate in 2020 for reasons that were never explained) by the Teague barn behind a line of volunteer pickups, and walked down to the wagon yard in the long gold light, past the pumpkin field and the apple bins and the hand-painted sign that said HAUNTED HAYRIDE · FRI & SAT IN OCTOBER · NO CHAINSAWS EVER. Somebody had added, in smaller letters, *we mean it, Ed.*

The Big Wagon sat hitched in the yard. It was a 1971 flatbed hay wagon with plywood sides and two rows of bales with bench backs bolted in, painted barn red so long ago it had faded to the color of a ham. At the front, facing forward, was a padded bench for two with a pair of black nylon seat belts that looked like they'd been salvaged from a school bus, and between the two seats, screwed to the rail, was a gray electrical box the size of a lunch pail with two brass toggle switches on top.

One was labeled FOG. The other was labeled WHISTLE.

In front of the wagon, idling with the sound of a small and contented landslide, was a red 1966 Farmall 806, and on the Farmall sat Hank Ruiter, seventy years old, in a seed cap, with his hands folded on the wheel.

"Evening, Hank," Gus said. "Evening, Marguerite."

Hank looked at him for a while. Then Hank looked at the tractor, as if checking whether Marguerite intended to respond.

"Yup," said Hank.

That was the most Hank had said to him since 2019, and Gus decided to take it as a welcome.

"Bro!"

Danny Pietrowski came around the back of the wagon with a clipboard and a headlamp already on his forehead, though the sun wasn't down. He was taller than Gus remembered by about a foot and a half, which made sense, since the last time Gus had seen him Danny had been twelve and crying in the Teague barn loft and refusing to come down.

"Hey, Danny."

"You're actually here." Danny looked him up and down. "You came to the walk-through. In boots."

"I was told to wear boots."

"Yeah, by her. Nobody does what she says. That's like the whole thing about her." He tapped the clipboard against his leg. "I'm parking. And I drive Wagon Two now. The little tractor. You want to see it? It's a John Deere. It's green. It's fine."

"Maybe after."

"Yeah. Okay." Danny hesitated. Then, lower, "She's at the pond. She's checking the line. She said to tell you she'll do the cue box when she gets back and that you should, quote, sit down and not touch anything."

"Sit down and don't touch anything."

"Those were her words. I'm a messenger." Danny started backing away. "I'm just saying. Bro. It's good you came."

Gus climbed up onto the front bench and sat down and did not touch anything.

He sat there while the sun went down over the corn, and the volunteers drifted past with extension cords and plastic skeletons and a bale of straw for the scarecrow, and every single one of them glanced at him on the bench and then away fast, the way you look at somebody in a hospital waiting room. He sat there while Lorna Bakker walked by carrying a coffee urn and said, "Hey, Gus, welcome to the fishbowl," and kept walking. The light went copper and then gray and the first cold of the evening came up off the field, and it got under his jacket, where her name was pinned over his heart in Dot Teague's cursive.

Then she came up the pond lane out of the dark.

She was in the barn coat, the sleeves rolled, a coil of fishing line over one shoulder and a pair of needle-nose pliers in her back pocket. She walked the way she'd always walked when she was carrying something, like the thing might try to get away. She didn't look at him. She came around the front of the wagon, climbed up onto the bench with one step, and sat down on the left side, a full foot of padded vinyl between them.

"Belt," she said.

He found the belt and fastened it. She fastened hers.

"FOG is mine," she said, to the cue box, not to him. "It runs a pump on the far bank. You leave it alone."

"Okay."

"WHISTLE is yours. Front-right guide. It's wired to a speaker in the willows on the far bank. You flip it once at the pond on the line in the script, and you flip it back, and you don't flip it any other time."

"On 'that's the 6:10,'" he said.

There was a pause about one second long. She was looking straight ahead at Hank's back, at the dark shape of the corn beyond the yard, and her jaw did something small.

"You read the back," she said.

"Dot told me to."

"Dot tells everybody to read the back." She put her hand on the FOG toggle, not flipping it, just resting her fingertips there, the burn scar on the back of her hand catching the yard light. "It's an approved script. It's been approved since 2020. The committee votes on changes, not the guides."

"I know."

"So you know your cue."

"I know my cue, Rosalind."

She nodded once, still not looking at him, and climbed down off the bench, and walked back toward the barn with the fishing line over her shoulder, calling for Danny about an extension cord.

Gus stayed where he was, belted in, in the dark.

After a while he put his thumb on the brass WHISTLE toggle. It was cold and worn shiny at the tip from six Octobers of somebody else's thumb.

He rested it there and didn't push.

## Chapter 3

<!-- POV: Roz -->

A scare is just a promise you keep two seconds late, and Roz had been keeping them since she was thirteen.

The first Friday of October came in clear and cold, with a hard blue sky that went orange over the corn at a quarter to seven and then purple. By then the line for the Carrow Haunted Hayride ran from the ticket table past the pumpkin scale and halfway down the gravel drive, and the parking field was already two-thirds full of minivans with Kalamazoo plates.

"That's a lot of Kalamazoo," Danny said, coming past with his clipboard and his headlamp. "For a Friday."

"It's always a lot of Kalamazoo."

"It's not always this much Kalamazoo." He lowered his voice. "Bev's telling people at the table. Not, like, telling them. Just saying 'Wagon One's got a special crew this year' in her church voice."

Roz looked across the yard. Bev sat at the folding ticket table under a string of orange bulbs with her cash box and her clipboard, and as Roz watched, she leaned toward a family of four in matching flannel and said something, and all four of them turned and looked at the Big Wagon.

"Great," Roz said. "Fantastic. Go park people."

"Going."

She walked the length of the wagon once, the way she always did before the first ride, touching things. The bale backs. The hand rail. The belts on the front bench. Under the bench, the black battery box for the cue system, wired with marine connectors because she'd learned in 2017 what a single rainy October did to alligator clips. She crouched and checked the terminals by headlamp. She checked them again.

Gus was already sitting on the right side of the bench, belted in, with his hands on his knees.

"You're early," she said, to the battery box.

"Hank said six-forty."

"Hank said six-forty to you? Out loud?"

"He pointed at his watch and then at the bench and then at me. I took it as a sentence."

She stood up. She did not laugh, because she had decided at the walk-through that she would not laugh at anything he said for ten nights, the way you decide to give up sugar for Lent. She climbed up and sat down on the left and belted in and put her hands on the clipboard in her lap. The call sheet for the night was on top: seven rides, two wagons, every stop cued, every volunteer's name and walkie channel. FINALE: AS APPROVED, she had typed at the bottom, the way she did every year.

"Seven o'clock," she said. "We go out at the gate, left at the orchard. Scarecrow on the right. That's Kenny Hoekstra on the air hose. Then the Corn, that's Ed, then the Woodlot, that's the ghost."

"Who's pulling the ghost?"

"Kenny's cousin. Brielle. She's fifteen."

"Does she pull early?"

"Nobody pulls early," Roz said, "anymore."

The first riders were coming across the yard. Mrs. Salas was at the front with Mateo, who was nine and wore a black hoodie with a skeleton printed on it and had held a season pass every year since he was six. He climbed up the wagon's wooden step with great dignity and sat in the first row of bales, directly behind the front bench, which was where he always sat, because it was the closest you could get to the guides without being one.

"Hi, Miss P," he said.

"Hi, Mateo. Hi, Mrs. Salas."

"Head of Scene Shop," said Mitch. "Crew of eight to start. Twelve by year two. Salary's eighty-four, benefits, pension, a parking space with your name on it, which in Chicago is like being knighted. Starts January fourth." He drank his coffee. "They asked me who. I said you. They looked you up. They saw *Othello*. They saw the submarine. They want to meet you. They want you."

Gus held the paper cup. He looked at the Gerstner. Then he looked around the shop, at the orange tags and the paint frame and the flats leaning in the dark.

Eighty-four thousand dollars was more money than he had ever made in a year. It was more than double what VanderWal Scenic would make in its first year if VanderWal Scenic got its second contract, which it had not.

"I've got a barn," he said.

"I know you've got a barn. You told me. You've got a barn and a hedge." Mitch put his coffee down on the band saw table. "Gus. I'm going to say this once, because I've known you since you were a twenty-five-year-old kid from the sticks who showed up at my loading dock with a mattress tied to a Ford Ranger and asked if we had any jobs that didn't require a degree. You were the best carpenter I ever hired. You were a better shop foreman than I am a production manager, and I'm very good. You can build anything. You've got a gift." He spread his hands. "And you went home to build hedges in a barn with a floor that slopes."

"How do you know the floor slopes?"

"Because you told me on the phone. You said 'the floor slopes, Mitch, a marble rolls from one end to the other, it's great.' You sounded like a guy describing his kid's first steps." Mitch picked up his coffee again. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying here's the other door. Wide open. Parking space. Think about it."

"When do they need an answer?"

"Monday. The board meets Tuesday. If it's not you, they've got a guy from the Guthrie who wants it bad, and he's fine. He's fine. He's not you." Mitch finished his coffee. "Monday. Call me. Don't email, I don't read email."

* * *

They loaded the Gerstner into Delores with a furniture dolly and two ratchet straps, Gus and Mitch and a kid named Teo who'd been on Gus's crew for two seasons and had come down from the paint frame when he heard Gus's voice.

"Boss," said Teo, and hugged him, a big rough hug with paint on it. "You coming back? Mitch says Calumet."

"Mitch says a lot of things."

"Mitch says you're the guy. He told Calumet's board you could build a staircase out of a sentence." Teo stepped back. "Come back, boss. Calumet's going to need a crew. I'd go. Rosa'd go. We'd all go if it was you."

Gus didn't say anything. He tightened the second strap. He checked it. He checked it again.

Teo looked at him, then at the Gerstner in the truck bed, then at the Michigan plate.

"You got a girl out there," Teo said. It wasn't a question.

"I've got a hayride," said Gus.

* * *

He drove home on I-94 with the Gerstner in the back and the radio off.

He drove past the Indiana steel mills and the dunes and the long flat stretch of Michigan farmland after the state line, the fields gone brown and gold, the corn cut down to stubble in some places and standing dry and pale in others. He drove past the exit for the Marathon station on M-40 without taking it. The light went long and orange and then purple, the way it had over the Teague corn on the first Friday of October while he sat on the bench with his hands on his knees and waited for her to come up the pond lane out of the dark.

He thought about the shop on Ninety-Fifth. Two paint frames. A CNC router the size of his truck. Eight carpenters, then twelve, and a parking space with his name on it. He let himself think about it properly, the whole thing, the way you'd walk through a set on the first day of load-in before anybody else was in the building. He let himself want it. It was a good thing to want. He'd been good at that life. He'd been happy in it, some days, in the way you can be happy in a room you've decided not to look at too hard.

He thought about VanderWal Scenic. One contract. A college opera with hedges. A floor that sloped. Walt Pietrowski's toolbox on the end of the workbench, and a box that said AUGUST, DON'T THROW OUT, and forty lanterns, and a sign he hadn't painted yet on a door he hadn't hung.

He thought about her.

He made himself do that last, and on purpose, because he knew if he started there he'd never think about anything else. He thought about her on the porch steps. Lorna had texted him at 6:51 on Monday night: a single picture, taken from a moving car, of the Pietrowski porch in the dark, with the light on and a woman in a barn coat sitting on the top step under it with her hands in her lap. No words. Just the picture. He'd looked at it for a long time.

He knew what he wasn't going to do. He wasn't going to call her. He'd done this once before, in a truck, at night, with a decision in front of him: sat with it, and gone quiet, and let the not-saying do the talking. He'd let her tell him *go* because it meant he didn't have to tell her anything. That was the thing he'd hated for seven years, and it was the thing he was not going to do again, even by accident, even by waiting too long.

He also wasn't going to call her and tell her on the phone. Not this. This was a thing you said with your face where somebody could see it.

So: Friday. Before the ride, or after. In person. He'd tell her the offer and the number and the date and the guy from the Guthrie, all of it, plain, and he'd tell her he hadn't decided, because that was true. He had not decided. He wasn't going to pretend to her that he had.

He came off I-94 onto US-131 and then onto the two-lane county roads in the full dark. At the turn onto Hollins Road his headlights swept across the Teague corn, and in the distance, past the barn, he could see the yard lights on, orange, where she'd left the battery chargers running for Friday.

A quarter mile farther east, on the left, on Teague Road, if you knew where to look across the field, there was a porch light.

He didn't slow down. He drove home and backed Delores up to the barn door and sat in the cab with the engine off for a minute, in the dark, with the Gerstner in the bed behind him and the porch light across the field.

"Friday," he told the truck.

Delores ticked as she cooled, and didn't argue.
