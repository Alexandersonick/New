## Chapter 20

<!-- POV: Roz -->

The Tractor Supply in Paw Paw on a Sunday afternoon in late October was, Roz decided, the least romantic place in the state of Michigan, and she had never been so glad to be anywhere.

"Hank wrote it down," Gus said, holding up a scrap of feed-sack paper. "In pencil. I think it says 'one-inch hitch pin, Grade 5, with clip.' Or it says 'one-inch fish pie.' His handwriting's not great."

"Let me see."

He handed it to her. It said, in Hank's square, enormous letters: 1" PIN. GOOD ONE. NOT CHINA.

"That's all it says," said Roz.

"That's a lot for Hank."

"That's a novel for Hank." She folded the paper and put it in her coat pocket next to his ticket half, where she kept everything now. "Aisle nine. Hitches and towing."

They walked down aisle nine. It was full of things she'd known the names of since she was six: clevises and drawbars and receiver hitches and chain binders, and a whole wall of hitch pins in clamshell packs, every diameter, some of them good ones and some of them from China. A teenage employee in a red vest watched them from the end of the aisle with the profound disinterest of someone who'd been told to say *can I help you find anything* and had decided against it.

Gus held up a pack. "Grade 5. One inch. Made in Ohio."

"Ohio's not China."

"Hank might feel differently about Ohio." He turned it over and read the back. "Rated for twelve thousand pounds."

"Marguerite pulls about six with a full wagon."

"So it'll hold her twice."

"It'll hold her twice," Roz agreed. She took the pack out of his hand and looked at it, at the fat bright steel pin in its plastic shell. "Hank's going to put it in and look at it for an hour."

"Then he'll say 'yup.'"

"He'll say 'yup' and then he'll look at it for another hour."

They stood in aisle nine holding the hitch pin between them. It was nothing. It was an errand. Hank had pointed at the sheared pin on Saturday night after the last ride and then at Gus and then at the car, and Gus had said *I'll go to Paw Paw tomorrow*, and Roz had said, from the other side of the wagon, before she'd thought about it, *I'll go with you, I need fog fluid*, and she did not need fog fluid. She had four gallons in the Lab. He knew she had four gallons in the Lab. He'd carried two of them in from the Explorer on Tuesday.

"Do you need fog fluid?" he said.

"Aisle fourteen," said Roz, and walked away from him toward aisle fourteen, which was horse supplies, and she stood there in front of a wall of fly masks and hoof picks for a full minute with her face burning before she came back.

He was still standing in aisle nine with the hitch pin. He hadn't moved.

"They don't have it," she said.

"Fog fluid."

"At Tractor Supply. Strange."

"Very strange," said Gus. "You'd think a farm store would carry theatrical glycol."

She took the hitch pin out of his hand again and walked to the register, and paid for it with the hayride's debit card, and the teenager in the red vest rang it up and said "Have a good one" in a voice that hoped they would not, and they walked out into the parking lot.

It was a gold afternoon, cold and bright, the kind of October day that happened about four times a year and made you forgive the other twenty-seven. The sky was the hard clean blue of a gas flame. Delores was parked at the end of the row, because Gus had insisted on driving, because the Explorer's passenger seat had a broken recline and he said he'd rather die than ride an hour at a ninety-degree angle.

They got in. He didn't start the truck.

"Monday's the twenty-sixth," he said.

She looked out the windshield at the Tractor Supply sign.

"I know."

"I didn't know if you'd want me to say it. Or not say it. I went back and forth about it the whole drive over. I figured if I didn't say it, it would sit in the truck with us the whole way home, like a dog."

"It's sitting in the truck anyway."

"It is," Gus agreed. "It's sitting right there between us. It's got its head on the console."

She laughed. It surprised her, the way all of them surprised her lately. She'd stopped being able to tell when they were coming, and she'd been telling when things were coming for a living since she was thirteen.

"I'm not going to do anything," she said. "Tomorrow. I've got school. I've got a Halloween lab with twenty-six eighth graders and three pounds of dry ice. Then I'm going to go home and grade, and make a grilled cheese, and go to bed."

"Okay."

"Don't say okay."

"I know. I'm working on it. I bought a thesaurus." He started the truck. "I just wanted you to know I know. That's all. I'm not going to make it a thing. I just know."

He was looking over his shoulder, backing out, with one hand on the back of the bench seat behind her head, carefully, the way he'd backed out of every parking space in his life, as if every car in the lot might be somebody's grandmother.

"I know you know," she said.

* * *

Halfway home, on the county road outside Lawton, there was a hand-painted sign nailed to a fence post that said CIDER · DONUTS · PUMPKINS · LAST WEEKEND, and Gus put on his blinker without asking.

"What are you doing?"

"Stopping."

"Why?"

"Because it's the last weekend," said Gus, "and the sign says so, and I'm not going to be the one who drove past it."

It was a pole barn with the big doors rolled open and a cider press going in the back, and folding tables of gourds and Indian corn and a hand-lettered price list on a whiteboard, and a line of six people at a counter where a teenage girl in a flannel was dropping rings of dough into a fryer and pulling them out golden and rolling them in a steel bowl of cinnamon sugar. The whole barn smelled like hot oil and apples and something warmer underneath, clove and nutmeg and ginger, the smell of every pie Lorna's grandmother ever made.

"Pumpkin spice," Roz said, reading the whiteboard. "They've got pumpkin spice donuts."

"Of course they do."

"You say that like it's a crime."

"It's a candle flavor," said Gus. "It's what a candle tastes like in a dream."

"It's nutmeg and cinnamon and clove and ginger. Lorna would put you in the dunk tank for that."

"Lorna already did."

"Bev did," said Roz. "Lorna threw the pie one."

He bought a half dozen anyway. Three plain, three pumpkin spice, in a white paper sack that went translucent with grease before they got back to the truck, and two cups of cider, hot, in paper cups with cardboard sleeves. They sat in Delores in the gravel lot with the engine running and the heater on and the sack between them on the bench seat, and Roz ate a pumpkin spice donut in four bites, sugar on her fingers, on her coat, on her chin.

He was watching her. She could feel it on the side of her face like sun through a window.

She looked over. He didn't look away. It wasn't the quick steady look from the bench, the one that checked if a rig would hold. It was a second look, and a long one, and he let her catch him at it.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"You're looking at me."

"You have sugar on your chin," said Gus. "I was deciding whether to say."

She wiped her chin with the back of her hand. She missed. He reached over, slowly, the way he did everything with his hands, and brushed it off with his thumb, once, and took his hand back.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's a candle flavor," he said. "Somebody had to deal with it."

She reached into the sack and took out the second pumpkin spice donut and held it out to him.

"Try it."

"No."

"Try it, August."

He considered the donut. Then he took it and bit it, and chewed, and made a face of profound and dignified suffering, and ate the whole thing anyway, while she laughed at him in the warm truck with sugar on her coat, and outside in the gravel lot a little girl in a pumpkin costume ran past the windshield shrieking, chased by a father with a cider in each hand.

* * *

On Monday, October twenty-sixth, at 1:15 p.m., in room 114, Kaylee Dykstra dropped a fist-sized chunk of dry ice into a beaker of warm soapy water with a pair of tongs, and a bubble the size of a cantaloupe rose out of the beaker, full of white fog, trembling, and the whole class said *ohhhh* in one long note.

"Boo bubble," said Roz. "Who can tell me why it's foggy inside?"

Twenty hands went up. Owen Sterk's did not. Owen was sitting at the back lab bench, as always, watching the bubble with the calm, total attention he gave everything.

"Owen."

He looked up, mildly surprised. "I didn't raise my hand."

"I know. I'm asking you anyway."

"The dry ice is turning into gas without being liquid first," said Owen. "Sublimation. It's really cold, so it makes the water vapor in the air condense. The fog's the water, not the CO2." He paused. "My grandpa rode your hayride Saturday."

The class went quiet. Roz kept her face where it was.

"Did he?"

"The 9:40. He said he got a lantern." Owen looked at the bubble, which was swelling and shivering on the lip of the beaker. "He said it was about Mr. Teague. When he was little. He said his own dad walked in it, in 1958. My great-grandpa. He had the railroad lantern. The red one." He looked back up at her. "Grandpa said he cried. He said he hasn't cried since my grandma."

The bubble burst. A little puff of white fog rolled off the edge of the lab bench and down onto the floor, and spread, and slid away under the desks toward the door.

"That's a good lantern," said Roz, after a moment. "The red one."

"That's what he said."

"Okay," said Roz, to the room, and her voice came out almost level. "Kaylee. Do it again. Bigger chunk."

* * *

She graded until six-thirty. She did not make a grilled cheese, because she sat at her desk in the empty classroom after the buses and the custodian and the light going long and orange through the windows and found that she'd been holding a red pen over the same quiz for eleven minutes. So she put the quizzes in her bag and drove home.

It was dark by the time she turned onto Teague Road. The days were going fast now; it would be November in a week, and the clocks would change, and the hayride would be over, and the dark would come at five.

She turned into her driveway. She stopped.

The porch light was on.

It had been off since March of 2024. That was when the fixture had died, not the bulb, the fixture, some corroded wire inside the old brass lantern housing her father had put up in 1999. She'd put a new bulb in and it hadn't come on, and she'd put another new bulb in and it hadn't come on, and she'd thought about the ladder and the breaker and the wire nuts and had decided, in the specific way you decide things in the spring before your brother finishes high school and your life is mostly eighth graders and fog machines, that she'd do it later. Then it was summer, and then it was the hayride, and then it was the next year, and she came home in the dark every night and found her keys by the light of her phone and had stopped noticing.

Now it was on.

It was the same brass lantern housing. Same dent on the left side from the time Danny had hit it with a Wiffle ball. It was lit, a warm gold light, and it fell on the porch steps and the porch swing and the welcome mat Bev had given them in 2019 that said BLESS THIS MESS, and on the swing, folded on the seat, there was a scrap of feed-sack paper.

She got out of the Explorer. She walked up the steps. She picked up the paper.

The handwriting was square, neat carpenter's capitals, nothing like Hank's, in pencil sharpened to a chisel point.

FIXTURE WAS FINE. WIRE WAS CORRODED AT THE BOX. REPLACED + NEW WIRE NUTS. BREAKER 9 (LABELED). DIDN'T COME IN. DIDN'T NEED ANYTHING.

And under that, smaller:

*I know.*

Roz stood on the porch with the paper in her hand.

She didn't go inside. She sat down on the top step, under the light, in her coat, with her school bag beside her. She could see the light on the backs of her hands, and on her shoes, and on the bare maple in the yard. Across the dark field, a quarter mile east, past the black shape of the Teague barn, a single light was on in the VanderWal pole barn. She could see it from here. She had always been able to see it from here. She had spent seven years not looking.

Her phone buzzed. It was Lorna.

"Okay, so," said Lorna, when she answered. "Danny says your porch light's on."

"How does Danny know my porch light's on? Danny's at class."

"Danny's at class with Kenny Hoekstra, whose mom drove by your house at five-thirty and saw Gus VanderWal on a ladder on your porch with his head in your light fixture, and called Kenny, who told Danny, who told me." Lorna paused. "So I'm calling to confirm."

"It's on."

"Did you know he was going to do it?"

"No."

"Did you ask him?"

"No."

"Is there a note?"

Roz looked at the feed-sack paper.

"There's a note."

There was a long, deeply satisfied silence on the line.

"Okay," said Lorna. "Okay. So that's a three-pie development. That's sour cream raisin, plus I'm going to make a lattice, plus Clyde's going to have to eat his apple card." A pause. "Roz? You okay?"

"I'm sitting on the porch," said Roz.

"Under the light?"

"Under the light."

"Okay," said Lorna, very gently, as if she'd been waiting a long time to say something and had decided not to say it. "Okay. I'll let you go."

Roz put the phone down on the step. She sat under the porch light with the note in her hand for a long time, looking across the dark field at the one lit window in the VanderWal barn, until the cold came up through the step and she went inside.

She left the light on.
