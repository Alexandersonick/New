## Chapter 4

<!-- POV: Gus -->

By the second night, Gus had worked out that being the villain of a ghost story was mostly a parking problem.

Cars lined Hollins Road from the Teague drive to the VanderWal mailbox, nose to tail on the gravel shoulder, hazard lights blinking in the dusk like a runway. Danny had commandeered two orange traffic cones and a light-up wand from somewhere and was standing in the middle of the road waving minivans into a hayfield with the solemn authority of an aircraft carrier deck officer.

"Bro!" he yelled as Gus walked up from his own driveway, because Gus's own driveway was full. "Bro, there's a bus!"

"A what?"

"A church bus! From *Allegan!*" Danny pointed the wand toward the barn. "First Reformed Youth Group. Twenty-six kids. They called ahead. They asked if the groom would be riding tonight and Bev said, and I quote, 'He is contractually obligated.'"

"I don't have a contract."

"I *know*, that's what's so messed up about it." Danny waved a Subaru into the field. "Also there's a lady with a sign."

"What kind of sign?"

"I didn't read it. It's pink."

Gus walked on. Standby humiliation, he thought, in the voice of every stage manager he'd ever worked under, the flat calm voice that came over the headset when a set change was about to happen in front of eight hundred people. Standby humiliation. Humiliation, go.

The line for Wagon One went past the ticket table, past the pumpkin scale, past the porta-johns, and doubled back on itself along the barn wall. Wagon Two's line was eleven people long. As Gus came into the yard light, a ripple went down the Wagon One line, the way wind goes through tall grass, and twenty phones came up out of twenty coat pockets.

The lady with the sign was about sixty, in a pink fleece, holding a piece of pink poster board on which someone had written, in glitter glue, TEAM LANTERN BRIDE.

"I drove from Battle Creek," she told him, as he passed.

"That's a long way," said Gus.

"I've ridden every year since 2021. I cry every time. Every single time." She narrowed her eyes. "Did you really take the 6:10?"

"No, ma'am."

"Hmph," said the lady from Battle Creek, and raised her sign a little higher.

* * *

The Grange shed smelled like coffee and space heater. Bev sat behind the folding table with three cash boxes, which Gus had never seen her need before, and a roll of ones in each hand, like a bandit.

"Now," she said, without looking up. "You're late."

"It's six-thirty-eight."

"Hank said six-thirty-five."

"Hank pointed at his watch. I didn't get a time. I got a gesture."

"Six-thirty-five," said Bev, "is the gesture." She made change for a woman in a Western Michigan sweatshirt, and then she did look up, and she gave him a look he'd last gotten from her in eighth grade, when she'd caught him and Roz in the church basement eating the funeral luncheon's cream puffs before the funeral. "You can still say your back went out."

"Everyone keeps telling me that."

"Because everyone has eyes, August." She handed the woman two wristbands, orange, for Wagon One. "Some of us were at that wedding rehearsal. Some of us made the mints."

There had been no wedding rehearsal. The rehearsal had been scheduled for October twenty-fifth, the night before the wedding, and he had been in Chicago by then for thirteen days, sleeping on an air mattress in a studio in Pilsen with a radiator that clanked. He didn't say that. He had made himself a promise in the Ranger in August, with his mother's house getting bigger in the windshield, that he would not argue with anybody in Carrow about what happened in 2019. Not the town, not Bev, not a woman in pink fleece with a glitter sign. Arguing was for people who believed they had a case.

"The mints were very good, Mrs. Oosterhouse," he said.

Bev's mouth did something complicated, and she went back to her ones.

* * *

They went out at seven with a full wagon and a bus-load still waiting, and at the Orchard Gate, when Kenny Hoekstra's scarecrow sat up, the youth group from Allegan screamed with such enthusiasm that Marguerite herself seemed to flinch.

Roz sat on the left side of the bench with her clipboard. She had done her rules speech in a voice so level you could have set a drink on it. She had not looked at him since he climbed up, which was by now a skill he could admire on a technical level, the way you admire a good blackout cue. She managed to have him on the edge of her vision at all times and in the center of it never.

"They're very loud," he said, at the Corn, under the screaming.

"They're Reformed," said Roz. "They don't get out much."

It was the first thing she had said to him all night that wasn't a cue, and he held onto it all the way to the Woodlot.

At the Woodlot, Brielle pulled the ghost on time, and the bedsheet came floating down its line out of the black trees, and the wagon screamed, and in the third row, while everyone was looking up, a high school boy in a Carrow Cougars hoodie who was very definitely not with the Allegan youth group threw a handful of popcorn at the back of Gus's head.

It was kettle corn. He could tell by the sugar. It hit his collar and went down his jacket in a small sweet avalanche, and a few kernels bounced off the cue box, and one landed on Roz's clipboard.

The wagon laughed. Not everyone. But enough.

"Hank," said Roz into the walkie. "Stop."

Hank stopped. The ghost was still hanging in the trees above them, white and swaying, because Brielle didn't know what was happening and was holding her line. In the sudden quiet, you could hear the tractor's idle and an owl and somebody's teeth chattering.

Roz turned around on the bench. She did not unbuckle. She turned as far as the belt allowed, which was far enough, and she found the boy in the hoodie with her eyes in about one-fifth of a second, which Gus had seen her do to thirteen-year-olds who were passing notes in 2018, when he'd come to her classroom with lunch.

"Mason Kuiper," Roz said.

The hoodie went very still.

"Rule nine," she said. "Nobody touches a rider. I'm extending it tonight to guides. You will apologize, or you will walk back to the barn in the dark, by yourself, past Ed Brinks and his lantern, and you will explain to your grandfather at the ticket table why you're early."

"It was just popcorn," said Mason.

"So's the ghost," said Roz. "Just a sheet. Want to walk back under it?"

A long pause. The ghost swayed overhead. Up in the trees, Brielle, bless her, gave it a tiny extra dip, perfectly timed, and twenty-six Reformed teenagers squeaked.

"Sorry," Mason said, to the back of Gus's head.

"Sorry, *what*?" said Roz.

"Sorry, Mr. VanderWal."

"Thank you, Mason. Hank, go."

Marguerite lurched forward. Roz turned back around and sat and looked straight ahead. She brushed the single kernel of kettle corn off her clipboard with one finger, the way you'd brush a crumb off a lab table, and let it fall into the dark.

Gus sat with popcorn down his back all the way to the pond and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not make it worse.

At the pond, she told it as written. He hit the whistle. They turned.

* * *

It went like that all night. The Allegan bus went around twice. The Battle Creek woman went around three times and cried at the pond each time, holding her sign upside down in her lap. Between the 8:20 and the 9:00 the line on Hollins Road started chanting, a few kids at first and then more, the way a crowd catches anything, *SIX-TEN! SIX-TEN! SIX-TEN!*, until Bev went out and stood in front of them with her clipboard and said something nobody heard, and it stopped.

Lorna came past Wagon One between rides with a tray of cider cups for the line.

"Okay, so, the diner's going to need a new pie," she said to Gus, low. "Like a commemorative pie. I'm thinking something with a lattice."

"Please don't make a pie."

"I'm not making the pie, the town is making the pie. I'm just going to be there when it comes out of the oven." She gave him a cider. "You're doing great, by the way. You've got a very good sitting face."

"Is that a thing?"

"Some people sit like they're waiting for a bus. You sit like a man at his own trial who's decided to be polite to the judge." She glanced at the wagon, where Roz was on her knees by the back wheel doing something unnecessary to a bolt. "Has she asked you yet?"

"Asked me what?"

"Your thing. Your truth thing." Lorna raised her eyebrows. "Danny told me. Danny tells me everything, I give him free fries. One a night, true answers. It's very *Scheherazade*. I approve."

"She asked one last night."

"And?"

"And I answered it."

Lorna studied him for a moment, the way she studied a pie crust to see if it was done.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Ask me sometime about the night of the rehearsal dinner that wasn't. I've got the other half of that story. Not now. Sometime." She moved off with the tray, calling, "Cider! Hot cider! It's apple, it's hot, it won't fix anything!"

* * *

Mateo and Mrs. Salas rode the 9:00, the way they rode it every Saturday, and Mateo took his place in the front row directly behind Gus and was quiet for the entire trip to the Orchard Gate, which for Mateo meant he was thinking.

It was on the 9:00, on the way back, in the long dark stretch along the back of the corn, that Roz asked her question.

She had been silent since the pond. She sat with her hands folded on the clipboard. The riders behind them were talking, low and happy, about the bride and the whistle and the guy, *that's the guy*, which he had stopped hearing about the fourth time, the way you stop hearing a refrigerator. The yard lights showed through the stalks ahead. Somewhere off to the right a dog barked, and Hank's shoulders twitched in the tractor seat as if he had an opinion on dogs.

"Do you still have the ring?" Roz said.

She said it to the corn, the way she had said the first one.

He knew which ring. There was only one. His grandmother's garnet, from the Netherlands by way of a steamer trunk and a farm in Zeeland, a small dark red stone in a gold setting worn thin on one side, which he had taken out of his mother's jewelry box in December of 2017 and carried in his coat pocket for eleven days before he got up the nerve to climb into the Teague barn loft and ask.

She had given it back to him on October sixth, 2019. She had put it on the kitchen table in the Pietrowski house between the salt and the pepper, and she had said, *Go. Don't call. If you're going, go.*

He could tell her it was in a drawer. He could tell her he'd kept it. It would cost nothing; she would never check.

"No," said Gus. "I sold it. February of 2020. To a jeweler on Clark Street. Rent."

The wagon rattled over a rut.

"I was three hundred and forty dollars short," he said. "My roommate's girlfriend had moved in and he'd raised my share and I didn't have it. I could've called my mom. I didn't want to call my mom. The jeweler gave me five-ten for it, which was robbery, and I took it, and I made rent, and I bought a space heater with the rest." He paused. "I went back in June to buy it back. It was gone."

He stopped there, because that was the truth and the rest would just be him talking.

Roz didn't say anything for a while. The corn ended and the yard opened up in front of them, the orange bulbs and the barn and the line still doubled back along the wall, and Hank swung the wagon wide around the pumpkin scale. Gus did not look at her. He had decided at the Supper that he would never be the one who looked first, and he was going to hold to that even if it took ten nights.

So he wasn't looking, and he only heard it.

She laughed.

The laugh was short, one breath, like something jarred loose, and nobody would have called it happy. Then she said, quiet, to the clipboard: "That's the first thing anybody's told me about you in seven years that wasn't a ghost story."

Then the wagon stopped, and she unbuckled, and she climbed down to help the riders off, and she didn't say anything else for the rest of the night.

* * *

Mateo was the last one off the 9:00. Mrs. Salas had gone ahead to the porta-johns, and Mateo lingered on the bottom step of the wagon, holding the rail, studying Gus with the frank attention of a boy deciding whether to trade a baseball card.

"Grandma says you're the train guy," Mateo said.

"Your grandma's a smart lady."

"So *are* you? The train guy?"

Gus thought about it. Nine-year-olds deserved the same answer as everyone else.

"I'm the guy in the story," he said. "I didn't take a train. I drove a truck."

Mateo frowned. He looked at the dark lane to the pond, and then at the barn, and then at Gus, adding it up.

"You should've taken the train," Mateo said finally. "Trains are cool."
