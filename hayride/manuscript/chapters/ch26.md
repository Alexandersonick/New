## Chapter 26

<!-- POV: Gus -->

At seven-thirty on Halloween morning, Delores came up the drive with Danny Pietrowski at the wheel and parked herself neatly beside the barn, and Danny got out and slammed the door and walked over to where Gus was standing in the open barn door with a cup of coffee.

"Bro," said Danny. "You left your truck."

"I know."

"In the *field*."

"I know, Danny."

Danny held out the keys. Gus took them.

"That's all I'm saying," said Danny. "I'm not saying anything for anybody. I told her I'm not. I'm a truck delivery guy. That's my whole job this morning." He looked past Gus into the barn, at the slab of old barn door leaning against the workbench, gray weathered pine with the Brinks family's 1930s cattle brand burned into one corner, and at the box of hinges from Carrow Hardware on the table, and at Walt's red toolbox open beside them. "Are you hanging that?"

"I was going to."

"Now?"

"I've been up since four. I couldn't think of anything else to do with my hands."

Danny measured him the way his sister measured things.

"I'll hold it," he said. "It's heavy. You can't hang a door like that by yourself, you'll rack it."

"Where'd you learn 'rack it'?"

"Some guy," said Danny, "taught me a shelf."

* * *

It took them two hours.

The east wall of the barn had a rough opening, eight feet by ten, framed in two-by-sixes, that his father had always meant to put a door in and never had. For thirty years it had been covered with a sheet of OSB nailed up from the inside and painted barn red on the outside to match, so that from the road it looked like a door if you didn't look hard. Gus had stared at that sheet of OSB every morning since the end of summer. It was the kind of lie he'd made his living building. It held still from a distance, and up close it was nothing at all.

They took it down. They hung the Brinks door.

Danny held it while Gus set the hinges. Walt's nine-sixteenths wrench, Walt's square, Walt's folding rule with the brass joints, and a carpenter's pencil sharpened to a long chisel point the way Walt had sharpened them, because Gus had found three in the top tray and couldn't bring himself to sharpen them any other way. They hung it, and it was out of plumb by an eighth of an inch at the top, because the barn sloped, and they took it down and shimmed the bottom hinge and hung it again, and it swung shut on the oil-rubbed bronze ball bearings with a sound like a deep breath let out, and latched, and stayed.

Danny stood back. Gus stood back.

It was a door. Gray old wood with a cattle brand in the corner. It opened. It shut. Light came in around the edges of it in thin gold lines, because it was a barn door and not a refrigerator.

"Huh," said Danny. "That's a real door."

"That's a real door."

"You're going to paint it?"

"I'm going to paint it."

"With your name on it?"

"With the name of the shop on it," said Gus. "And the hours."

Danny nodded slowly. He looked at the door for a while longer. Then he said, not looking at Gus, "You know the hayride's at seven tonight. Last night. Halloween."

"I know."

"You're coming."

"Rule seven," said Gus. "A guide who leaves her draw leaves the season. I'm not leaving my draw, Danny."

Danny let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since the parking field. He nodded once, hard, like a man agreeing to a contract, and went to get his Civic, and drove away down Hollins Road without saying goodbye, which was how Gus knew he'd said everything he came to say.

* * *

He painted the sign first.

He did it freehand, the way Bettina had taught him on the paint frame at Lakefront, with a pencil line snapped across the boards and a long-handled liner brush and a can of white enamel. It took an hour and ten minutes. He made himself go slowly. He made the serifs on the V properly. He painted VANDERWAL SCENIC in letters six inches high across the middle of the gray boards, and under it, smaller, in three-inch letters, because he'd said he would:

BY APPOINTMENT · MON–SAT 8–5

Then he stood back from it, with paint on his fingers, in his father's barn, and looked at it.

It was a real door. It had a real name on it. Nobody was ever going to come during the hours.

He went inside and got his phone.

* * *

Mitch picked up on the second ring, because Mitch always picked up on the second ring, even on a Saturday, even at ten-fifteen in the morning on Halloween.

"Farmer," said Mitch. "It's not Monday."

"I know it's not Monday."

"You calling early? You calling to tell me you want it so bad you couldn't wait?"

"I'm calling to tell you no."

There was a silence on the line. Gus could hear the loading dock behind Mitch, the hydraulic whine of a lift gate, somebody yelling about a pallet jack.

"Okay," said Mitch, slowly. "Okay. You want to tell me why? Because I'd like to tell the board something better than 'he's got a hedge.'"

"I've got a shop," said Gus.

"You've got a barn."

"I've got a shop. I just hung the door. It's got the name on it. It's got the hours." He stood in the kitchen, which was empty now except for the sink and his coffee maker and the ceramic owl cookie jar on top of the refrigerator, which his mother had left on purpose, he was sure, the way you'd leave a nightlight on for somebody. "I've got one contract and a kid who sands for fifteen an hour and a toolbox that belonged to a man who used to laugh at me for stripping nine-sixteenths bolts. I want to build things that go out and come back. I don't want to run eight guys on Ninety-Fifth Street. I was good at it. I know I was good at it. I don't want it."

"Is this about a girl?"

He'd known Mitch would ask. He'd thought about it at four in the morning, sitting on the edge of his bed. He'd thought about what the true answer was, all of it, and found it, and turned it over, and it had held.

"It's not *about* her," Gus said. "She's in it. She's part of what I'd be leaving. But she told me to go, Mitch. Last night. In a parking lot. She told me to take it. If this was about her, I'd be packing."

Another silence. Longer.

"She told you to go," Mitch said.

"She did."

"And you're staying anyway."

"I'm staying because I want to stay. That's the difference. I've done it the other way. I know what it costs."

Mitch let out a long breath on the line. Gus could picture him exactly: standing on the dock by the vending machine in his Calumet fleece, rubbing the bridge of his nose under his reading glasses.

"Gus," Mitch said. "I want to tell you something, and I want you to hear it, because I'm not trying to change your mind. I'm just telling you what it costs. Okay?"

"Okay."

"Calumet's going to build that shop with or without you. They'll hire the Guthrie guy. And the plan was, the plan I pitched them, was you'd run the shop and they'd send their overflow to outside vendors. Small builds. Regional work. Stuff the main shop doesn't have time for. I was going to steer it to you even if you didn't take the job. I was going to call it VanderWal Scenic overflow and put it in the budget." He paused. "I can't do that now. If you turn down the job, the board's not going to send their overflow to the guy who turned down the job. That's not me. That's just how boards are. They'll take it personal. They'll send it to a shop in Indiana."

Gus stood very still in his empty kitchen.

"How much?" he said.

"Six, eight builds a year. Twenty, thirty grand. Enough to keep a barn open in January." Mitch's voice was quiet. "I'm not saying it to be mean. I'm saying it because you asked me once, when you were twenty-five, to always tell you the real number, and I always have."

"I know you have."

"So that's the number."

Gus looked out the kitchen window, past the clothesline and the dead tomato cages, at the barn. From here he could see the new door, gray, with the white letters across it, wet still, catching the light. VANDERWAL SCENIC. BY APPOINTMENT.

Thirty thousand dollars. Enough to keep a barn open in January. One contract for a hedge.

He found he wasn't scared. He'd thought he would be. He'd been scared for seven years, quietly, all the time, of being the kind of man who did the easy thing. He'd been scared on the bench, on the culvert, in the corn. Now, standing in his mother's empty kitchen with the real number in his ear, he found he was something else. He was a man looking at a door he'd hung himself, with his own name on it, knowing exactly what it weighed.

"Then that's the number," Gus said. "Thank you for telling me. Give the Guthrie guy my best. Tell him the Pullman warehouse has a leak on the north side, over by where they'll put the paint frame. I walked it in 2023 for a job we didn't get. Tell him to check the gutters before he hangs a drop."

Mitch laughed. It was a short, rough, unhappy laugh, and it sounded like it hurt a little.

"You're an idiot," said Mitch.

"I know."

"You're the best carpenter I ever hired and you're an idiot." A pause. "Send me a picture of the door."

"I will."

Mitch hung up without saying goodbye, the way he always did, as if the call would just keep going on its own somewhere.

* * *

The Grange shed was open when he got there at twelve-forty. He'd known it would be. She'd be there early on the last day; she was always early.

She was standing at the chalkboard with her back to the door. She'd erased everything under the season goal except the running totals, and in the space at the bottom she was chalking the night's schedule in her neat block capitals: SAT OCT 31. 7:00 7:40 8:20 9:00 9:40 10:20 11:00. BOTH WAGONS SOLD OUT. FINALE: THE SEARCH. She had chalk on her hands and on the cuff of her father's coat. She was wearing yesterday's clothes. She looked as if she hadn't slept at all.

She heard the door. She didn't turn around.

"Danny brought your truck," she said.

"He did."

"He said he didn't say anything for me."

"He didn't. He held a door."

She put the chalk down in the tray. She stood there for a moment with her hands at her sides and her back to him, looking at the board, at $29,350 in Bev's capitals and the small lantern somebody had drawn under it.

He stayed in the doorway. He didn't come in. He'd decided that on the drive over: he'd say it from the door, so she could close it if she wanted to.

"I called Mitch," he said. "This morning. I told him no."

She didn't move.

"I'm not telling you so you'll do anything," he said. "I'm not asking for anything for it. I just said I'd answer true, even when you don't ask, and you didn't ask, so." He stopped. Started again. "I turned it down. It cost me. Mitch was going to send me overflow work from Calumet even if I didn't take the job, twenty or thirty thousand a year, and now he can't. So the shop's got one contract going into winter. That's the real number. I wanted you to have the real number."

Still nothing. Her shoulders under the barn coat were very straight.

"And I didn't do it because you said go," Gus said. "And I didn't do it because you said stay, because you didn't. I did it because I hung a door this morning and painted my name on it and it's the first thing I've built in seven years that I didn't want to strike." He put his hands in his pockets. "I said no. Not for you." He paused, and made himself tell the rest of it, because it was true. "Well. Not only."

She turned around.

She had chalk on her face, a white streak across one cheekbone where she'd pushed her hair back. Her eyes were red at the rims. Her face was not where she'd put it. He had never, in all the years he'd known her, seen it so far from where she'd put it.

She looked at him in the doorway.

"Okay," Roz said.
