## Chapter 3

<!-- POV: Roz -->

A scare is just a promise you keep two seconds late, and Roz had been keeping them since she was thirteen.

The first Friday of October came in clear and cold, with a hard blue sky that went orange over the corn at a quarter to seven and then purple. By then the line for the Carrow Haunted Hayride ran from the ticket table past the pumpkin scale and halfway down the gravel drive, and the parking field was already two-thirds full of minivans with Kalamazoo plates.

"That's a lot of Kalamazoo," Danny said, coming past with his clipboard and his headlamp. "For a Friday."

"It's always a lot of Kalamazoo."

"It's not always this much Kalamazoo." He lowered his voice. "Bev's telling people at the table. Not, like, telling them. Just saying 'Wagon One's got a special crew this year' in her church voice."

Roz looked across the yard. Bev sat at the folding ticket table under a string of orange bulbs with her cash box and her clipboard, and as Roz watched, she leaned toward a family of four in matching flannel and said something, and all four of them turned and looked at the Big Wagon.

"Great," Roz said. "Fantastic. Go park people."

"Going."

She walked the length of the wagon once, the way she always did before the first ride, touching things. The bale backs. The hand rail. The belts on the front bench. Under the bench, the black battery box for the cue system, wired with marine connectors because she'd learned in 2017 what a single rainy October did to alligator clips. She crouched and checked the terminals by headlamp. She checked them again.

Gus was already sitting on the right side of the bench, belted in, with his hands on his knees.

"You're early," she said, to the battery box.

"Hank said six-forty."

"Hank said six-forty to you? Out loud?"

"He pointed at his watch and then at the bench and then at me. I took it as a sentence."

She stood up. She did not laugh, because she had decided at the walk-through that she would not laugh at anything he said for ten nights, the way you decide to give up sugar for Lent. She climbed up and sat down on the left and belted in and put her hands on the clipboard in her lap. The call sheet for the night was on top: seven rides, two wagons, every stop cued, every volunteer's name and walkie channel. FINALE: AS APPROVED, she had typed at the bottom, the way she did every year.

"Seven o'clock," she said. "We go out at the gate, left at the orchard. Scarecrow on the right. That's Kenny Hoekstra on the air hose. Then the Corn, that's Ed, then the Woodlot, that's the ghost."

"Who's pulling the ghost?"

"Kenny's cousin. Brielle. She's fifteen."

"Does she pull early?"

"Nobody pulls early," Roz said, "anymore."

The first riders were coming across the yard. Mrs. Salas was at the front with Mateo, who was nine and wore a black hoodie with a skeleton printed on it and had held a season pass every year since he was six. He climbed up the wagon's wooden step with great dignity and sat in the first row of bales, directly behind the front bench, which was where he always sat, because it was the closest you could get to the guides without being one.

"Hi, Miss P," he said.

"Hi, Mateo. Hi, Mrs. Salas."

"Hello, Rosalind." Inés Salas had brought her knitting. She always brought her knitting. She had knit through six seasons of the Lantern Bride and claimed she had never once dropped a stitch.

Mateo leaned forward over the bench back and studied Gus from a distance of about eight inches.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Gus," said Gus. "I'm new."

"You're old."

"I'm old and new. It happens."

Mateo considered this, and then sat back and accepted it, the way nine-year-olds accept most things, provisionally.

The wagon filled. Thirty-two people on hay bales, blankets on laps, phones out for the first ten minutes until they forgot about them. Roz stood up on the bench footrest, as far as her belt would let her, and turned to face them and did the speech, which she could have done in her sleep and once nearly had.

"Good evening and welcome to the Carrow Haunted Hayride. My name is Roz, and I'll be your guide. The man beside me is also your guide, and I apologize for that in advance." A laugh, small and nervous. Good. "A few rules. Stay seated. Keep your hands inside the wagon. Nothing on this hayride will touch you. Nothing on this hayride has a chainsaw. If you need us to stop, yell, and we'll stop. If you scream, we won't stop, because that means it's working."

A bigger laugh. She sat down and buckled her belt back to snug. She picked up her walkie.

"Hank. Wagon One rolling."

Marguerite answered for him with a long low cough of diesel, and the Big Wagon lurched, and the whole wagon made the noise every wagon made at that lurch, a collective *oh!* that turned into giggling. They rolled out the barn gate into the dark.

Most of a hayride was waiting. The scares were seconds long; the rest was a tractor at four miles an hour and the orchard going by in black rows, like people standing too still. You built the waiting, and then you broke it.

At the Orchard Gate, Kenny's scarecrow sat up on its post with a hiss of compressed air and swung its burlap head toward the wagon. The back row screamed. Mateo, who had seen it sit up about a hundred times, screamed anyway, out of loyalty.

At the Corn, Ed Brinks stood in the stubble at the edge of the maze with a lantern held up by his face and said, in his deep farmer voice, "Ain't nobody comes back out of my corn," which he had said every night for thirty years, and which was not true of anybody, including Ed.

At the Woodlot, Brielle pulled the ghost on time.

Roz's hands unclenched on the clipboard. The bedsheet came floating down its line out of the trees, white and slow and terrible, and stopped above the wagon, and hung there for a three-count, and went back up into the black. It was her oldest effect, built out of a fitted sheet and a fishing reel when she was fourteen, and every year it still made someone cry, and every year she was proud of it in a way she would never say out loud.

Then the trees opened, and there was the pond.

"Hank," she said into the walkie.

Hank stopped the tractor at the flat spot by the dock, where the lane widened and the willows came down to the water. He turned the key. Marguerite shuddered and went silent, and the silence came down over the wagon like a blanket thrown over a cage.

It was the best moment of the ride and she had built it on purpose. Thirty-two people sat in the dark. The pond lay in front of them, black and flat, with a little mist on it from the low ground. On the far bank, the willows hung like hair. Nobody spoke. Somebody's phone went off and they killed it fast.

Roz didn't use the walkie for the finale. She didn't stand up, either. She told it sitting down, belted in, in a voice not much above talking, because people leaned in to a quiet voice and leaned back from a loud one, and she wanted them leaning.

"Fifty years from now," she said, "somebody will tell you this happened a long time ago. It didn't."

She felt him beside her.

"There was a girl on Hollins Road who sewed her own veil. And a boy on Hollins Road who bought a ticket for the 6:10 to Chicago. Two weeks before the wedding, he took it."

Somewhere in the third row, a woman said, very softly, "Oh."

"She walked down to this pond with a lantern to wait for the train to bring him back. And she's still waiting." Roz reached down between them and flipped FOG, and on the far bank a low white sheet of mist slid out from under the willows and lay on the water. "Look across the water."

Out on the far side, a light came on.

It was a hurricane lantern with an LED candle, under a fall of old muslin, hung from a pulley on a monofilament line strung across the pond. Roz had built the trolley in 2020 out of a curtain track and a sewing machine motor, and from the wagon in the dark you could not see the line, or the trolley, or the motor. You saw a lantern lift from the far bank and begin to move across the water, very slowly, behind a veil, toward you.

The wagon held its breath. She could feel it hold. She could have told you to the second how long it would hold.

"Don't call to her," Roz said. "She doesn't answer anymore."

The lantern stopped in the middle of the pond. It hung over its own reflection.

"And if you hear a whistle," she said, "that's the 6:10."

The whistle came.

It came on the syllable, the way it was supposed to and never had, because every guide who'd ever sat on the right side of the bench had flipped it late. A train whistle came out of the far willows, long and low, two notes falling.

She heard him flip the toggle back. A small brass click.

"That's him," Roz said. Her voice stayed level. She had made sure, at the walk-through, that her voice would stay level. "Still choosing the city."

The lantern went out.

In the silence, before Hank could find the key, someone in the second row said, in a stage whisper that carried across the whole wagon and probably the pond, "Oh my gosh. That's *him*. That's the guy."

And thirty-two people turned their heads.

It happened all at once, the way a flock turns. They turned from the far bank where her lantern had just gone dark on the best cue she'd ever had, and they looked at the back of his head, and not one person on that wagon was looking at the water.

Roz sat in the turned silence and heard her own line go around the wagon a second time in other people's mouths, *still choosing the city*. It sounded the way it had sounded the summer she wrote it at her kitchen table: mean, and good, and only true-ish. She had known it was cruel when she wrote it. She had said it anyway, six Octobers running, because it sold, and she had just said it again with him sitting there.

He wasn't looking at the pond. For one second, in the dark, he was looking at her: a second look, quick and steady, the kind you give a rig to see if it held. Then he turned back to the water, and sat with his hands on his knees, and let them stare.

"Hank," Roz said into the walkie, and Hank started the engine, and the wagon rolled home.

* * *

There were twelve minutes between rides when the line was moving. Roz spent them on her knees under the wagon with a headlamp, checking the battery box, which was fine, which she knew was fine.

She heard his boots in the gravel. She heard him stop by Marguerite's big rear wheel, which came up to his chest. She stayed where she was.

"The trolley motor," he said. "Is that a sewing machine?"

"Singer. 1970s. Foot pedal's on a relay."

"That's very good."

She came out from under the wagon. She stood up and brushed the gravel off her knees and looked at him properly, the first real look since the Supper, in the orange light from the bulb strings, with the line of riders forty feet away pretending not to watch them.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't compliment the motor."

"Okay." He put his hands in his jacket pockets. "Can I say one other thing?"

"You used your thumb."

He blinked. "What?"

"On the toggle. Every guide since 2020 has used a finger. You used your thumb, and you kept your other hand on the rail the whole time, like the wagon might leave without you."

"Stagehand's habit," he said. "Four fingers on the rail, thumb on the cue. You never let go of anything to take a cue. That's how people fall off things."

She didn't know what to do with that, so she looked at the wheel.

"That wasn't the thing I wanted to say," he said.

"I know it wasn't."

He was quiet for a moment. Across the yard, Bev was making change and very obviously not looking at them, which in Bev took enormous effort and showed.

"Tell it," Gus said.

"What?"

"The story. Tell it like it's written. Every ride. I'll sit there and hit the whistle, and if anybody wants to talk to me about it, I'll talk to them, and I won't make a face." He looked at the wheel too, as if it were easier. "That's not a favor. You don't owe me anything for it. It's your story. They came for it."

"They came for you, apparently."

"Then they'll get me." He took a breath. "I'm sorry, Roz. Not for the story. For how I left. I'm not asking for anything for it. I just haven't said it out loud to you, and I'd like that done before the second ride."

She didn't answer. She couldn't find the part of herself that answered things.

"Okay," he said. "That's done. And every ride, you can ask me one thing. Anything. And I'll answer it true. Not nice. True."

She stared at him.

"Why would I want that?"

"I don't know if you want it." He shrugged, a small careful movement. "I just thought you might have some questions, and no way to ask them that doesn't look like asking. And there's a wagon full of people listening to everything we do anyway. Might as well give them something real."

"One a ride?"

"One a night. Ten nights. Seven rides a night is a lot of truth for one man."

The 7:40 riders were climbing up the wooden step. Mateo was back in the first row; he had gone around twice already and would go around until Mrs. Salas ran out of yarn. From the front of the wagon, Hank looked over his shoulder at them and lifted one finger off the steering wheel, which meant *now.*

"I'm not going to change it," Roz said. "The story. I want that clear. I'm not going to soften it because you're sitting there."

"I didn't ask you to."

"It's approved. It goes as written."

"Then tell it as written," said Gus. "I'll be on the bench."

He climbed up first. She stood by the wheel for three more seconds, which was all she gave herself, and then she climbed up after him and buckled her belt and picked up her walkie and said, "Hank, Wagon One rolling," in a voice nobody on the wagon could have told from any other year's.

* * *

She told it seven times that night.

She told it on the 7:40 with Mateo whispering the words along with her, because he knew them. She told it on the 8:20, when the line had grown past the drive to the road, and somebody near the back of the wagon held up a phone with its screen lit and then put it away, embarrassed, when Roz looked at it. She told it on the 9:00 and the 9:40 and the 10:20. She told it exactly as written, every word in its place, the veil and the 6:10 and the lantern, and every time the whistle came, the wagon turned to look at him, and every time he sat still and looked at the water.

By the 10:20, Danny was putting cars on the shoulder of Hollins Road.

"It's on Facebook," he reported between rides, breathless, headlamp askew. "Somebody posted 'the groom is ON the wagon,' all caps, with a crying emoji. It's got two hundred shares. Bev's going to need more ones."

"Bev always needs more ones."

"Roz." He hesitated. "Are you okay?"

"It's handled," she said, and went back under the wagon to look at the battery box.

The 11:00 was the last ride. It went out three-quarters full of teenagers and one exhausted family who had waited an hour and a half and were going to get their money's worth. The night had gone properly cold. The teenagers had brought a blanket and a thermos of something that wasn't cocoa.

"That's peppermint schnapps," Roz said at the Orchard Gate, without turning around. "I can smell it from here. Pass it up."

"It's cocoa," said a boy in a letter jacket.

"It's cocoa with a felony in it. Pass it up, Tyler, or I call your grandmother, who is sitting in a lawn chair by the ticket table right now with a thermos of actual cocoa and a phone she knows how to use."

"Ma'am, that's so specific," said Tyler.

The thermos came forward hand over hand. Roz took it and gave it to Gus without looking at him.

"What do I do with it?" he said.

"Hold it."

"For how long?"

"Until it's a cocoa again," she said, and he held it in his lap all the way to the pond like a man holding a baby he hadn't been introduced to.

She told it. He hit the whistle. They turned.

On the way back, in the long dark stretch between the pond and the barn, where the lane ran along the back of the corn and there were no scares and the riders talked to each other and forgot about the guides, she sat with her hands on her clipboard and watched the yard lights come closer through the stalks.

She had one question. She had about four hundred, actually, collected over seven years and filed in a place she didn't open. But one was right at the front, the way her own ticket had been right at the bottom of the jar.

"Why'd you sign up?" she said.

She didn't look at him. She said it to the corn.

He didn't answer right away. Marguerite rattled over a rut. Behind them a teenager laughed at something on a phone.

"I wanted to hear you tell it," Gus said. "Guys at the shop in Chicago had heard of it. A lighting designer from Holland told me about it at a bar in 2022, the bride and the 6:10, like it was a thing that happened to somebody else. I wanted to hear it once in person, from you, and know it was mine." He paused. "That's the selfish answer. Dot asking is the other one."

The wagon came out of the corn into the yard light, and Hank swung it wide around the pumpkin scale, and Marguerite coughed to a stop.

* * *

At midnight, in the Grange shed, Bev counted the cash box twice with her lips moving and then picked up the chalk.

The shed was a converted corn crib beside the barn, with a space heater, two folding tables, a first-aid kit, and a chalkboard that had hung on the back wall since 1987. At the top, in Bev's careful capitals, it said SEASON GOAL $29,000 / GREAT LAKES MUTUAL / NEW WAGON BY JAN 1. Under that there were ten blank lines, one for each night.

Bev wrote $2,300 on the first one.

"Best first Friday," she said, "since 2021."

Nobody said anything. Lorna, who was counting the Wagon Two waiver forms, glanced at Roz. Danny, eating the last of the Ladies Aid apple crisp out of the pan with a plastic fork, glanced at Roz. Gus had gone home already; she had heard Delores's engine start in the parking field and turn out onto Hollins Road toward the VanderWal place, a quarter mile east, which she had not let herself measure in seven years and knew to the foot.

Roz pulled tomorrow's call sheet out of her clipboard. Seven rides. Two wagons. Every stop cued.

At the bottom, she wrote: FINALE: AS APPROVED.
