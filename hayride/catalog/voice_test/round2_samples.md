# Blind voice test samples (round 2)

Four ~1,000-word narration samples from close-third male narrators. Every name has been replaced, independently per sample, so names do not carry across samples.

## Sample 1

The Northside Rep scene shop had been a candy factory in 1921 and a scene shop since 1988, and on the last Wednesday of October 2026 it was mostly a room full of things with orange tags on them.

Nate stood in the loading door at eleven in the morning with his hands in his jacket pockets and looked at it.

Everything was tagged. The panel saw he'd learned on. The forty-foot rolling scaffold. The paint frame on the back wall, two stories tall, where a woman named Marisol had painted a Venetian palazzo for *Othello* in 2021 while he held her ladder and she sang Patsy Cline at it. The flats stacked in the racks: walls from fifty shows, kitchens and libraries and the inside of a submarine, lies that had stood in front of eight hundred people a night and now leaned against one another in the dark like tired actors, each with an orange tag on its corner that said LOT and a number.

It smelled the same. Sawdust and scene paint and the dry, burnt-coffee smell of a building that had been heated by the same boiler since Prohibition.

"There he is," said Sal Bruno, coming out of the office in back with two paper cups. "The Wisconsin farmer. You milk anything on the way in?"

"I sanded a hedge."

"Good. Hedges need it." Sal handed him a cup. It was the vending machine coffee, from the machine on the loading dock, and it tasted exactly as Nate remembered, like a penny somebody had dissolved in hot water. "Your chest's in the corner. Under the drop cloth. Nobody touched it, I made sure. Ernie wanted it. I told Ernie if he touched it I'd tell his wife about Milwaukee."

"What happened in Milwaukee?"

"Nobody knows," said Sal. "That's the power of it."

* * *

The Gerstner was where he'd left it in May: in the back corner of the shop by the band saw, under a canvas drop cloth splotched with every color of a seven-year repertory season. Nate pulled the cloth off.

It was oak, quarter-sawn, with brass hardware and eleven drawers lined in green felt, and it had cost him four months' rent in 2021, which he had not had, so he'd paid it off in installments to a man in Ohio who sold tool chests out of a pole barn not unlike Nate's own. He'd kept his chisels in it, and his marking gauges, and his good squares, and a folded Polaroid of the Miller pond in October that he'd taken in 2016 and that nobody else had ever seen. He opened the third drawer. It was still there, under the dovetail saw.

He closed the drawer.

"So," said Sal, behind him.

Nate knew the voice. It was the voice Sal used at production meetings right before he told a director that the turntable they wanted would cost forty thousand dollars and arrive after opening.

"So," said Nate.

"Harborline's head of shop retired in September." Sal leaned against a rack of flats with his coffee. "Earl Novak. You remember Earl. Forty years. They threw him a party, he cried, he took a fishing boat to Florida. And Harborline's board decided, since they're rebuilding anyway, since they put in a bid on half this building's inventory for the auction next week, they want to build a real shop. A *shop* shop. They're moving into the old Pullman warehouse on Ninety-Fifth. Thirty thousand square feet. Two paint frames. A CNC router the size of your truck."

"Sal."

"Head of Scene Shop," said Sal. "Crew of eight to start. Twelve by year two. Salary's eighty-four, benefits, pension, a parking space with your name on it, which in Chicago is like being knighted. Starts January fourth." He drank his coffee. "They asked me who. I said you. They looked you up. They saw *Othello*. They saw the submarine. They want to meet you. They want you."

Nate held the paper cup. His eyes went to the Gerstner, then around the shop, to the orange tags and the paint frame and the flats leaning in the dark.

Eighty-four thousand dollars was more money than he had ever made in a year. It was more than double what Holt Scenic would make in its first year if Holt Scenic got its second contract, which it had not.

"I've got a barn," he said.

"I know you've got a barn. You told me. You've got a barn and a hedge." Sal put his coffee down on the band saw table. "Nate. I'm going to say this once, because I've known you since you were a twenty-five-year-old kid from the sticks who showed up at my loading dock with a mattress tied to a Ford Ranger and asked if we had any jobs that didn't require a degree. You were the best carpenter I ever hired. You were a better shop foreman than I am a production manager, and I'm very good. You can build anything. You've got a gift." He spread his hands. "And you went home to build hedges in a barn with a floor that slopes."

"How do you know the floor slopes?"

"Because you told me on the phone. You said 'the floor slopes, Sal, a marble rolls from one end to the other, it's great.' You sounded like a guy describing his kid's first steps." Sal picked up his coffee again. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying here's the other door. Wide open. Parking space. Think about it."

"When do they need an answer?"

"Monday. The board meets Tuesday. If it's not you, they've got a guy from the Arena Stage who wants it bad, and he's fine. He's fine. He's not you." Sal finished his coffee. "Monday. Call me. Don't email, I don't read email."

* * *

They loaded the Gerstner into Bessie with a furniture dolly and two ratchet straps, Nate and Sal and a kid named Kip who'd been on Nate's crew for two seasons and had come down from the paint frame when he heard Nate's voice.

## Sample 2

"For seven years," Marion said. "Every time somebody at church asked. Every time somebody at the diner said, *well, what really happened with Jessamyn and the Lindqvist boy*. I said, he took the train. I said, she's still waiting. I said it like it was gospel, honey, because you wrote it, and it was what you wanted said."

"I know, Marion."

"I said it to Edie Lindqvist's *face*. At the Ladies Aid. In 2021. I said, it's a beautiful story, Edie, it's a shame it's about your son, and she got up and left, and she hasn't sat at my table since." Marion's voice wobbled very slightly and steadied. "I'd say it again. I want you to know I'd say it again. I'm not sorry for taking your side."

"I'm not asking you to be sorry."

"Then what *are* you asking?"

Jess didn't answer. She was standing by the space heater in her father's barn coat, with her arms crossed, and the heater's orange glow lit her profile. Her jaw was doing the thing. She looked about as tired as he'd ever seen her look, including the week of her father's funeral, when she had organized the luncheon and the flowers and the parking and had not sat down once in five days.

Marion waited. When nothing came, she stood up, and picked up her casserole carrier from under the table, and put on her quilted coat.

"Pat's taking the boxes to the night deposit," she said. "I'm going home."

She came toward the door, and saw him there with the twine, and stopped.

For a second Cal thought she would say something. Something about Edie, or the hotdish, or the train. But she only looked at him, a long, flat, tired look over her reading glasses, and then she went past him into the yard and across the gravel to her Buick, and the engine started and the tires crunched away down the drive.

* * *

Jess didn't move from the heater.

"You heard all that," she said, without turning around.

"Some of it."

"Most of it."

"Most of it," Cal agreed. He came in and put the twine in the barrel by the wall. Then, the way a stagehand grabs a push broom the second a scene gets uncomfortable, he started stacking the folded hay bale blankets that the riders used, which were lying on the second table in a heap. They were Army surplus wool, gray and scratchy, and they smelled like hay and kids and spilled cider. He folded each one in thirds the long way and then in half, and stacked them, and she watched him do four before she said anything.

"You don't have to do that."

"Somebody does."

"Tyler usually does."

"Tyler's helping Otto put Matilda away. Otto's teaching him to back the wagon into the shed, and from the sound of it, it's not going well." Through the shed's small window they could both hear, faintly, the tractor's engine revving and stopping, revving and stopping, and Otto's voice saying a single word, over and over, that might have been *left* or might have been a name. Cal folded another blanket. "Can I ask you one?"

"It's not your turn."

"It's not a turn. It's not part of the thing. It's just a question."

She was quiet. The heater ticked.

"Fine," she said.

"Why?"

He left the word out there with no direction attached. She knew what.

Jess turned around. She stood with her back to the heater now, her face in shadow, and her arms still crossed, and she looked at the stack of blankets and not at him.

"Because I wrote it," she said.

"That's not a reason. That's a byline."

Her mouth tried something and thought better of it.

"It's the only reason I've got," she said, "that doesn't have you in it. And I need one that doesn't have you in it, Cal, because if I do this because of you, it's the same thing. It's just the story with the ending changed. And everybody in this town would know it, and Marion would know it, and I'd know it."

He stood there with a blanket hanging from both hands like a drop that had come in on the wrong cue.

"So it's for you," he said.

"It's for me."

"Okay."

"Don't say okay."

"Marion doesn't like it either," said Cal. "It's very unpopular, okay. I'm going to have to find a new word."

Her mouth tried again. It got further this time.

Outside, the tractor engine cut. In the sudden quiet Tyler's voice came through the wall, very clearly: "I'm *getting* it, Otto, I'm just not *good* at it," and Otto said, "Yup."

Jess uncrossed her arms. She came over to the table and picked up a blanket off the heap and folded it in thirds the long way and in half, exactly the way he was doing it, and put it on his stack.

"What's the damage?" she said, after the sixth one.

"You don't want to know."

"I always want to know. I teach science. Give me the number."

"Marion rubbed it out, so I'm guessing. Refunds, plus the people who turned around in the field." He set a blanket on the stack. "Twenty-eight hundred. Maybe three thousand."

She folded the next one very precisely.

"That's a lot of pond," she said.

"It's a very nice pond."

"It's a pond with nothing on it."

"I liked it," said Cal. "The kid in the fourth row liked it. She said 'oh.' People said 'oh' all night. Different 'oh' than usual."

"What's the usual?"

"The usual's 'oh,' like a hand to the chest." He demonstrated, with a blanket. "Tonight it was 'oh,' like when you walk into a room you've been in a hundred times and somebody moved the couch."

Jess stopped folding and met his eyes over the heap of gray wool, the first time since he'd come in.

"That's a very specific 'oh,'" she said.

## Sample 3

At six that night he put his phone in the bowl, and after a second his watch beside it, and washed his hands at the kitchen sink. He laid a clean dish towel on the table and the cow on the towel, and rolled a swab, and rejected it for a tail, and rolled another.

One inch of field, low on the left, where nobody would look. Damp, not wet. Roll toward you once, and lift.

The cotton came up gray. He put it on the saucer he was using for a can and rolled another, and it came up gray, and another after that. He did not count them, and when he caught the habit trying, he let it go.

At 7:40 the web of his left thumb began to burn, and he stopped and said to the empty kitchen, "My hand's tired," and sat with it in his lap for ten minutes by the clock on the stove before he did it again, slower.

At ten he stopped, because it was ten.


* * *

On Wednesday at 12:35 Gwen put her head around his door.

"There's a Mrs. Birch at reception, and she's brought a tin."

He had a call at one and the Hagerstown numbers open on both screens. He closed one of them.

"Bring her up."

Rosemary came in with her good wool coat over the navy cardigan, her hair set, carrying a blue cookie tin in both gloved hands like a christening present, and the office filled with Jergens. She smiled at the window and at the river beyond it.

"Ben, dear, I won't keep you, Tessabelle says you have eleven hundred people."

"In fourteen buildings."

"Fourteen buildings." She sat in the chair across the desk without being asked and set the tin on his blotter, squarely in the middle. "Shortbread, Harold's mother's recipe. I made it last night, and I thought, he won't have had anything homemade in weeks, and he's lost weight, I can see it from here."

"Thank you."

"We've all thought of you, rattling around that big house on your own. We've all had such a difficult autumn," Rosemary said. "I said so to Father at St. Phil on Sunday. Everyone has been so upset. And Christmas coming."

He waited, as he had learned to wait in a cold room in Kensington with a swab in his hand.

"Livvie won't answer my calls," she said. "She won't come on Christmas Eve. She's in that factory with her poor hand and won't let a soul near her, and you know how she gets when she's dug in, she was like that at four years old. But I think she'd come around, Ben. I think she'd come around if only it were easier for her. If she could see it wasn't her husband who let her down."

"Who let her down?"

"Well." Rosemary smoothed her gloves on her knee. "Tessabelle has always been impressionable, everyone knows it. She wanted so much to be helpful, and she got carried away, and none of us saw how far, least of all you. Men don't notice these things, it isn't in them, and nobody would blame you for a moment." She leaned an inch toward him over the tin. "Tessabelle won't mind, she'd do anything for her sister now. Tell Livvie you didn't know. We'll all say Tess tricked you. It's the kind thing."

On the floor outside somebody laughed and was shushed, and a printer started up.

"I knew," Ben said.

"Knew what, dear?"

"That it was Tess at the table, from the first Friday. I opened the wine and poured it."

"Oh, Ben, that's just what a man says when he's feeling low. You were working all hours, you didn't—"

"I knew."

Rosemary looked at him with Liv's gray-green eyes set in her own soft face, and then down at the tin between them, and turned it a quarter turn on the blotter so the lid sat straight.

"Liv read your texts from October sixth," he said.

"I don't know what that is."

"Make it sound like her. Sharper."

Her face did nothing at all except go smooth, like a drawer shut with the folder in it.

"I'm sure I wouldn't know," she said. "You'd be welcome on Christmas Eve, of course, either way. We always do Christmas Eve. Tessabelle would make the lamb, the one with the rosemary that everybody liked so much."

"No, thank you, Rosemary."

"You don't have to decide now."

"I stopped waiting for her once, in October," Ben said. "I'm not going to stop again."

"Livvie always did take the hard way," Rosemary said. "I'd hoped you had more sense than to follow her."

She drew on her gloves, one finger at a time, and stood. She did not raise her voice, and she did not take the tin.

"You'll regret how honest you've become," she said.

"Probably."

She rested one gloved hand on the back of the chair, looking at the tin and not at him.

"Harold was honest," she said. "It never kept him home on a Sunday."

She went out past Gwen's desk, and he heard her tell Gwen what a lovely view of the river it was, and then the elevator came, and she was gone.

He carried the tin out and set it on the corner of Gwen's desk.

"I'm not eating that," Gwen said, without looking up.

"Nobody's asking you to."

"She asked me if you'd been eating." Gwen typed something. "On the way in, before she asked which office was yours."


* * *

On Thursday he did a second inch of the field and found a fence under the brown, a thin white line of rails nobody had seen since about 1935. At 8:40 the phone lit up in the bowl in the hall, and he went to it, because it was his mother, who called on Sundays and never on Thursdays.

"Benny."

"Ma, what's the matter?"

"Nothing's the matter, can't a person call her son?" The AM radio was going behind her, and the kitchen chair by the radiator creaked as she sat in it. "I'm asking about Christmas, are you bringing her?"

## Sample 4

At the bottom of the notebook page, under her arm, slow capitals slanted the wrong way, written by the hand that wasn't hers for writing. NOV 18. LOWER EDGE, LOSSES 1–91. He did not read any further than that.

He checked his watch at 5:41 and again at 5:44. He had never been good at waiting, and his mother used to say he'd been born three weeks early because he couldn't stand a line.

At 5:55 the timer went off with a rattle, and she sat up.

She came up out of sleep already looking at something, and the something was him, at the end of the bench in an old gray T-shirt and jeans, holding his coat.

"You're early," she said.

"I was twenty-five minutes early."

She turned the timer away from him, as if it had told on her. "Did you park under the light?"

"Same place as Saturday."

"Farid will tell Kostas."

"He already did."

She looked at his shirt. "You're wearing cotton."

"You said cotton."

He went to the bowl by the door and put his phone in it. Then he unbuckled his watch, a steel Seiko his mother had ordered from a catalog the year he made shift supervisor, and laid it in the bowl beside the phone, and there was a pale band of skin where it had been.

"Where do I stand?" he said, turning around.

She looked at the watch in the bowl, and then at the pale band.

"Here," she said, and pointed to a place at the corner of the big table where the boards dipped. "Wash your hands first, warm water, no soap after the first rinse."


* * *

The first hour was cotton.

She gave him a canvas apron cut for somebody smaller, a box of nitrile gloves in large, which on him were not large, a jar of bamboo skewers and a hank of cotton wool.

"Swabs," she said. "Watch me do one."

She did one left-handed, a pinch of cotton thinner than he'd have guessed and a twist of the skewer against her thigh, and the cotton wound itself on tight and came to a soft blunt point.

"Tight enough that it doesn't shed, loose enough that it rolls. Make fifty."

"Fifty," he said, and pulled the cotton toward him.

The cotton liked to clump on his fingers, and the first ones came out in knots. She went through them as he made them, holding each one up to the lamp and turning it.

"That one's loose, it'll shed," she said, dropping it into a coffee can. "That one's lumpy." Into the can. "And that one has a tail."

"What's a tail?"

"A wisp at the end. It leaves a fiber on the paint, and then I'm picking a fiber out of a crack with a needle at midnight." She held it up between two fingers. "Make it again."

Eleven of the fifty went in the can. He made eleven more, and she threw out two of those, and he made two more, and she kept them.

When it was time for the solution she reached across the bench with her right hand for a brown glass bottle labeled in the same slanted capitals, closed her fingers on the cap, and turned. The hand began to shake, low in the thumb first and then up into the fingers, a quick fine tremor, and the cap did not move, and she kept trying anyway.

"Let me take that—"

He stopped.

She looked at him over the bottle. He couldn't have said what was in her face, except that it was watchful.

He put his hands in the apron pocket.

She loosened the vise left-handed, swapped the coffee jar for the bottle, closed the jaws until the bottle sat tight, and unscrewed the cap in three neat turns, then poured a little into a glass dish.

So that was what the jar was for. He'd thought somebody was being funny.

"I have a system," she said.

"I see that."

"Come here," she said.

She folded the sheet back from the lower right corner. From eighteen inches the brown was not one color: there was a gray on top, bus-shelter grime, and under the gray a yellow, and under the yellow a darker shape that turned out to be a fold of cloth. In the corner was the blue, two inches by three, and beside it she had laid a square of tape, one inch by one inch.

"What you're taking off is surface dirt," she said. "A century of candles and coal smoke and people breathing. It sits on top of the varnish, and it comes off first, or everything after drags it around."

She dipped one of his swabs in the dish and touched it to a folded tissue. "Blot it. You want damp, not wet, because wet goes down the cracks, and then it's under the paint, and then it's my problem for a year. Then roll it. Don't rub. Roll it toward you, across, once, and lift."

"Toward me, once, and lift."

She did it in the air above the tape, not touching, and held out the swab. He took it in his left hand, the one that knew what it was doing.

"Inside the tape," she said, "and nowhere else. If your hand gets tired, you say so and you stop, because a tired hand is how paintings get hurt."

"Tired, I stop."

He blotted, rolled and lifted, and the cotton came up a dull gray-brown, like mop water at the end of a night shift.

"Like that?"

"Lighter on the stick. Now turn it to a clean face and do it again."

He turned it and did it again.

She put on the old headband magnifier, the one with the taped hinge, and pulled the kitchen chair up to his elbow, close enough that he could feel the air move when she breathed, and watched the tape through the lenses.

"Drier," she said, and later, "Lighter," and once, "Lift there. Good."
